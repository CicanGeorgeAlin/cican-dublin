## 2. Direct physical description — 1988 survey

The 1988 survey describes:
- A sub-rectangular limestone plaque.
- Decoration in “false relief” (the survey's wording).
- Six shields bearing arms, arranged around an Annunciation scene.
- A chamfered lower edge.
- An undulating scroll of leaves along the lower edge.
- Roman-capital inscription: “MAREARET SAINTE LAWRENS MADE THIS WORK DOND”.
- Four names above the shields, separated by Tudor roses and circles:
  - BRAHDEVODE
  - BURIFORDE
  - IHS HOWTHE
  - BARNEWAL
- Dimensions: H. 52 cm; W. 208 cm; T. 17 cm.

The survey's transcription is retained exactly as printed. “MAREARET” may be a damaged, variant or erroneous reading of a personal name; do not silently correct it to “Margaret” in the object inscription field. The inscription's final word “DOND” is also retained as transcribed. A specialist epigraphic review is needed before offering a normalised transcription or translation.

## 3. Provenance — reported removal from Bremore Castle

A secondary heritage profile reproducing National Monuments Service monument information for Bremore Castle (SMR DU002-002001) states that the castle is thought to have been under construction by 1546 because a “bridal mantlepiece tree”, part of the hall chimney piece, commemorates the marriage of James Barnewall to Margaret Lawrence around that year.

A separate heritage account of the Lusk church complex describes the Annunciation fireplace surround as removed from Bremore Castle. A 2023 revised entry for the Lusk bullaun stone on the same heritage profile quotes Hunt (1974) and Roe (1979): within the Lusk tower were a seventeenth-century font, a bullaun stone and “portion of a fireplace removed from Bremore castle”, decorated with six heraldic shields surrounding the Annunciation.

These sources support a reported association with Bremore Castle and a movement to Lusk. They do not provide a complete chain of custody, exact removal date, named mover, conservation treatment or proof that the surviving Lusk fragment is the complete “bridal mantelpiece” described in the Bremore entry.

**Important chronological distinction:**
- The Lusk archaeological survey dates the plaque as seventeenth century.
- The Bremore heritage profile associates a hall chimney-piece / bridal mantlepiece tree with the marriage of James Barnewall and Margaret Lawrence around 1546.
- These may describe different phases or related components of a larger fireplace assemblage. Do not transfer the c.1546 date to the surviving Lusk plaque or treat the marriage date as the plaque's proven carving date.
- Do not assume “MAREARET SAINTE LAWRENS” is definitively Margaret Lawrence without epigraphic and genealogical corroboration.

## 4. Heraldry and names — do not over-attribute

The 1988 survey lists four names above the six shields: “BRAHDEVODE / BURIFORDE / IHS HOWTHE / BARNEWAL”. The shields themselves are described as bearing arms, but the survey excerpt does not provide a formal blazon or confidently match each name to a particular shield.

The Bremore profile describes the castle as the Barnewall family manorial seat from the fourteenth century and links the hall chimney-piece to the marriage of James Barnewall and Margaret Lawrence. This contextual evidence may help guide a future heraldic study, but it does not prove that every shield on the Lusk fragment represents a named spouse, family or marriage alliance.

**Archive treatment:** preserve the four name readings verbatim, record the six shields as six visible heraldic motifs, and leave individual shield identifications unresolved pending expert comparison with the arms and genealogy of the Barnewall, Lawrence / St Lawrence, Howth, Buriforde and Brahdevode families.

Do not assume the word “IHS” is part of a surname: it may be a separate Christogram or abbreviation preceding “HOWTHE”. Record the original sequence and flag the reading for inspection.

## 5. Iconography — Annunciation

The 1988 survey identifies the central religious scene as an Annunciation. This is the Christian subject in which the angel Gabriel announces to Mary that she will conceive Jesus. The survey's identification is a source-attributed iconographic reading; this batch has not independently inspected the carved object or its detailed iconographic attributes.

Future photography and specialist examination should record:
- The angel figure and any attributes (for example, wings, scroll or lily) only if actually visible.
- The Virgin Mary and her posture.
- Any architectural setting, lectern, vase or other symbols if present.
- The relative position of the six heraldic shields.
- Tudor roses and circular separators above the shields.
- The leaf-scroll along the chamfered lower edge.
- Any tool marks, damage, repairs, mortar traces or evidence of reuse.

Do not add iconographic details to the catalogue based on generic Annunciation imagery.

The specific access arrangements are subject to change. Check the official Heritage Ireland page before visiting. ## 11. Conclusion and next steps

The Annunciation plaque is a high-value movable architectural fragment: a seventeenth-century limestone chimney-breast element documented at Lusk, with a reported earlier association with Bremore Castle. Its unusual combination of a central Annunciation, six heraldic shields, four inscribed names, Tudor roses, a leaf-scroll and a surviving Roman-capital inscription makes it a strong candidate for an individually documented micro-object.

The most important research caution is chronology and provenance: the Bremore profile's c.1546 marriage-related chimney-piece context does not prove that the Lusk fragment was carved in 1546. The Lusk survey assigns it to the seventeenth century. The exact relationship between the Lusk fragment and the earlier Bremore fireplace assemblage remains unresolved.

Next:
1. Keep the 17th-century survey attribution and c.1546 Bremore context separate.
2. Seek specialist epigraphic and heraldic review of the inscription and shields.
3. Confirm guided access and photography permission.
4. Recover and validate the canonical catalogue before assigning a child ID.
5. Continue with the octagonal limestone font as the next separately described Lusk tower object.
