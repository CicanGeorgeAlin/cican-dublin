# Clontarf Sheds — Historic Map Research Layer

**Research date:** 2026-10-26  
**Project:** CICAN DUBLIN ARCHIVE  
**Status:** DOCUMENTED RESEARCH LAYER — NOT A GEOGRAPHICALLY VERIFIED OBJECT INVENTORY  
**Area:** The former Clontarf Sheds waterfront, around the foot of Vernon Avenue, Clontarf, Dublin.

## Research objective

Establish a defensible cartographic sequence for the former fishing and fish-curing landscape at Clontarf. The immediate objectives are to identify the historic shoreline, the broad location of the fishing sheds, oyster-fishery references, access routes and later changes to the waterfront.

This document does not assign coordinates to vanished sheds, oyster beds or other features. A map symbol or historical place-name is not an exact modern location unless the source map can be georeferenced with suitable control points and a stated uncertainty.

## Findings supported by the sources

### 1. Clontarf was a fishing and fish-curing place

Samuel Lewis's *A Topographical Dictionary of Ireland* (1837), in its Clontarf entry, says that wooden buildings called the Clontarf sheds remained along the water's edge and had formerly been used to cure fish. It also describes a profitable oyster bank off the sheds. This is a contemporary gazetteer description of the landscape in the 1830s, not a surveyed footprint of the buildings.

Source: [Clontarf — Samuel Lewis, 1837, Vision of Britain](https://visionofbritain.org.uk/descriptions/4010720)

History Ireland's account of Clontarf's development describes a late-eighteenth-century fishing base and fishermen's thatched cottages facing the sea at the Sheds, where herrings and oysters were preserved. This is useful contextual interpretation, but it is a modern secondary source.

Source: [From village to suburb: the building of Clontarf since 1760 — History Ireland](https://historyireland.com/village-suburb-building-clontarf-since-1760/)

Clontarf Online's local history says the sheds stood at the foot of Vernon Avenue and that the name of the locality derived from the fish-curing sheds. Treat this as a local-history lead and cross-check its details against historic maps and contemporary records.

Source: [Clontarf — Clontarf Online](https://www.clontarfonline.com/history.htm)

### 2. The oyster fishery is visible in the 1760 cartographic record

Trinity College Dublin's Centre for Environmental Humanities identifies artificial oyster beds off Clontarf on a map extract attributed on the page to Rocque's *Actual Survey of the City of Dublin, 1760*. The article says these beds were replenished using oysters from natural beds elsewhere, including Arklow and Glascarrig in County Wexford and Carlingford in County Down.

This is strong evidence for an eighteenth-century oyster-fishery landscape. The map image must still be inspected at full resolution before tracing any bed outline or assigning coordinates. The page's caption and map title should be checked against the original sheet catalogue before final cartographic attribution.

Source: [History of the Irish oyster — Trinity College Dublin](https://www.tcd.ie/tceh/projects/foodsmartdublin/recipes/Sept_Oyster/HistoryEcology_oyster.php)

### 3. The 1728 harbour map directly labels a Clontarf fishing feature

Dublin Port Archive's collection identifies a 1728 map titled *A New and Correct Map of the Bay and Harbour of Dublin with a small Plan of the City*, published and sold in London by J. Bowles. The archive provides the map image itself:

- [Open the original 1728 map image — Dublin Port Archive](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Maps_Bowles.jpg)
- [Collection entry — Historical Maps, 1700s](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/collection/historical-maps-1700s/)

On the map, a label beside Clontarf appears to read **“Herring Shells”** (transcription provisional at the available image resolution). The map therefore provides direct visual evidence of a named herring-related feature on the Clontarf shore in 1728. Do not silently normalise “Shells” to “Sheds”: the exact lettering should be checked against a higher-resolution archival image or physical copy before the label is quoted definitively. The image's imprint identifies J. Bowles as publisher/seller; it does not, by itself, prove that Bowles surveyed or authored the map.

A separate page by Chapters of Dublin attributes a 1728 *Map of Dublin Bay and Harbour* and a “Herring Sheds” label to Captain Perry. The image inspected here is explicitly the J. Bowles map. These attributions must remain distinct unless an original Perry map is located and compared.

**Research conclusion:** the Bowles map is now an inspected primary cartographic source; the Captain Perry attribution remains an unverified separate lead.

Sources:
- [Dublin Port Archive — Historical Maps, 1700s](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/collection/historical-maps-1700s/)
- [Clontarf — Chapters of Dublin](https://www.chaptersofdublin.ie/NorthDub/cosgrave16.htm)

### 4. The Rocque 1760 map is available through a hosted historic-map service

A South Dublin County Council ArcGIS MapServer describes its map as John Rocque's actual survey of County Dublin, originally published in 1760. The service exposes the historic map as a raster image layer in Irish Transverse Mercator (EPSG:2157), alongside boundary and footprint layers. This is a substantial advance over relying only on a secondary reproduction: the historic county map can be viewed through a hosted map service.

- [Rocque 1760 County Dublin historic map service — South Dublin County Council ArcGIS](https://tiles.arcgis.com/tiles/PxbTDTskGHCe4sv6/arcgis/rest/services/Rocque_New/MapServer)
- [Rocque raster image layer metadata](https://tiles.arcgis.com/tiles/PxbTDTskGHCe4sv6/arcgis/rest/services/Rocque_New/MapServer/3)

Trinity College Dublin independently identifies artificial oyster beds off Clontarf on an extract from Rocque's *Actual Survey of the City of Dublin, 1760*. The Trinity article says these artificial beds were replenished from natural beds elsewhere in Ireland. The ArcGIS service establishes access to a Rocque county map; Trinity's figure specifically supports the Clontarf oyster-bed interpretation. The map service has not yet been visually inspected at the Clontarf location in this research pass, and no oyster-bed geometry or coordinates are asserted here.

Source: [History of the Irish oyster — Trinity College Dublin](https://www.tcd.ie/tceh/projects/foodsmartdublin/recipes/Sept_Oyster/HistoryEcology_oyster.php)

### 5. Later large-scale mapping is available

UCD Digital Library catalogues an Ordnance Survey Ireland map titled *City of Dublin: Township of Clontarf: sheet XVIII.29*, dated 1889, at a scale of 1:1056 (five feet to one statute mile). The catalogue identifies the source as the OSi 19th Century Historical Maps collection and gives source identifier DN018_029.

This is a high-value source for examining late-nineteenth-century plot boundaries, roads and built form. Its catalogue coordinates describe the map item/location and must not be mistaken for the position of an individual feature.

Source: [UCD Digital Library — City of Dublin: Township of Clontarf: sheet XVIII.29 (1889)](https://digital.ucd.ie/c0055/items/c0055_o0511.html)

UCD Library's map guide confirms access routes to historic Dublin maps, including Rocque's 1760 County Dublin map and the 1770–1840 Longfield collection held by the National Library of Ireland. The guide also points researchers to Tailte Éireann's Irish Townland and Historical Map Viewer for historic six-inch and 25-inch mapping, including first-edition six-inch maps and later editions.

Sources:
- [UCD Library — Historic Maps of Dublin](https://libguides.ucd.ie/findingmaps/mapshistDublin)
- [UCD Library — Historic Maps of Ireland and the Townland and Historical Map Viewer](https://libguides.ucd.ie/findingmaps/mapshistireland)

## Working interpretation — not yet a mapped conclusion

The sources support a historical fishing and fish-curing locality associated with the foot of Vernon Avenue and the Clontarf Sheds waterfront. They also support the existence of oyster beds off Clontarf in the eighteenth century and a continuing fish-curing landscape described in 1837.

They do **not yet** establish:
- the exact footprint, number or orientation of individual sheds;
- the precise modern coordinates or boundaries of the oyster beds;
- the exact location of wells, pipes, slipways or water infrastructure;
- whether a particular surviving wall, street line or plot boundary is physically continuous with a feature shown on an eighteenth-century map.

No new physical-object catalogue records or map pins are created by this research note.
