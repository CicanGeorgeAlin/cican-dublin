# Clontarf Sheds — Historic Map Research Layer

**Research date:** 2026-10-26  
**Project:** CICAN DUBLIN ARCHIVE  
**Status:** DOCUMENTED RESEARCH LAYER — NOT A GEOGRAPHICALLY VERIFIED OBJECT INVENTORY  
**Area:** The former Clontarf Sheds waterfront, around the foot of Vernon Avenue, Clontarf, Dublin.

## Research objective

Establish a defensible cartographic sequence for the former fishing and fish-curing landscape at Clontarf. The immediate objectives are to identify the historic shoreline, the broad location of the fishing sheds, oyster-fishery references, access routes and later changes to the waterfront.

This document does not assign coordinates to vanished sheds, oyster beds or other features. A map symbol or historical place-name is not an exact modern location unless the source map can be georeferenced with suitable control points and a stated uncertainty.

## Findings supported by the sources

### 1. Clontarf was a fishing and fish-curing place

Samuel Lewis's *A Topographical Dictionary of Ireland* (1837), in its Clontarf entry, says that wooden buildings called the Clontarf sheds remained along the water's edge and had formerly been used to cure fish. It also describes a profitable oyster bank off the sheds. This is a contemporary gazetteer description of the landscape in the 1830s, not a surveyed footprint of the buildings.

Source: [Clontarf — Samuel Lewis, 1837, Vision of Britain](https://visionofbritain.org.uk/descriptions/4010720)

History Ireland's account of Clontarf's development describes a late-eighteenth-century fishing base and fishermen's thatched cottages facing the sea at the Sheds, where herrings and oysters were preserved. This is useful contextual interpretation, but it is a modern secondary source.

Source: [From village to suburb: the building of Clontarf since 1760 — History Ireland](https://historyireland.com/village-suburb-building-clontarf-since-1760/)

Clontarf Online's local history says the sheds stood at the foot of Vernon Avenue and that the name of the locality derived from the fish-curing sheds. Treat this as a local-history lead and cross-check its details against historic maps and contemporary records.

Source: [Clontarf — Clontarf Online](https://www.clontarfonline.com/history.htm)

### 2. The oyster fishery is visible in the 1760 cartographic record

Trinity College Dublin's Centre for Environmental Humanities identifies artificial oyster beds off Clontarf on a map extract attributed on the page to Rocque's *Actual Survey of the City of Dublin, 1760*. The article says these beds were replenished using oysters from natural beds elsewhere, including Arklow and Glascarrig in County Wexford and Carlingford in County Down.

This is strong evidence for an eighteenth-century oyster-fishery landscape. The map image must still be inspected at full resolution before tracing any bed outline or assigning coordinates. The page's caption and map title should be checked against the original sheet catalogue before final cartographic attribution.

Source: [History of the Irish oyster — Trinity College Dublin](https://www.tcd.ie/tceh/projects/foodsmartdublin/recipes/Sept_Oyster/HistoryEcology_oyster.php)

### 3. The 1728 harbour map: Thomas Burgh, Captain John Perry and J. Bowles

Dublin Port Archive provides the original image of *A New and Correct Map of the Bay and Harbour of Dublin with a small Plan of the City* (1728):

- [Open the 1728 map image — Dublin Port Archive](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Maps_Bowles.jpg)
- [Dublin Port Archive — Historical Maps, 1700s](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/collection/historical-maps-1700s/)

A peer-reviewed article by Finnian Ó Cionnaith, published in *Irish Geography*, identifies this as **Thomas Burgh's** 1728 chart of Dublin Bay. It was based on a 1725 survey conducted on behalf of the Ballast Board, with assistance from **Captain John Perry (1670–1732)** and surveyor **Gabriel Stokes**. The chart was printed by **John Bowles** and engraved by **Emanuel Bowen**. This reconciles the shorthand “Captain Perry's map” used by Chapters of Dublin: Perry assisted the underlying survey, while Burgh is identified as the chart's author.

On the archive image, the label beside Clontarf appears to read **“Herring Shells”** (transcription provisional at the available image resolution). Chapters of Dublin transcribes the feature as “Herring Sheds” and associates it with the fish-curing locality. The two readings are not silently harmonised here. A higher-resolution archival image or inspection of the original is needed to settle the exact lettering.

**Research conclusion:** the 1728 chart is now identified with a strong scholarly attribution chain: Thomas Burgh (chart), 1725 Ballast Board survey (assisted by Captain John Perry and Gabriel Stokes), Emanuel Bowen (engraver) and John Bowles (publisher). It is a primary cartographic source for the Clontarf shoreline; the exact annotation remains a paleographic question.

Sources:
- [Finnian Ó Cionnaith, “Charles Brooking's 1728 map of Dublin: context, influences and compilation of an early Georgian city survey”, *Irish Geography* (PDF)](https://search.igsjournal.ie/pdfs/ngOhKEAjInGJxt1RLwS9tFpb4kGKtCE7Y6QLhsEl.pdf)
- [Dublin Port Archive — Historical Maps, 1700s](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/collection/historical-maps-1700s/)
- [Clontarf — Chapters of Dublin](https://www.chaptersofdublin.ie/NorthDub/cosgrave16.htm)
- [Dublin Port Archive — Access and Copyright](https://www.dublinportarchive.com/access-copyright/)

### 4. The Rocque 1760 map is available through a hosted historic-map service

A South Dublin County Council ArcGIS MapServer describes its map as John Rocque's actual survey of County Dublin, originally published in 1760. The service exposes the historic map as a raster image layer in Irish Transverse Mercator (EPSG:2157), alongside boundary and footprint layers. This is a substantial advance over relying only on a secondary reproduction: the historic county map can be viewed through a hosted map service.

- [Rocque 1760 County Dublin historic map service — South Dublin County Council ArcGIS](https://tiles.arcgis.com/tiles/PxbTDTskGHCe4sv6/arcgis/rest/services/Rocque_New/MapServer)
- [Rocque raster image layer metadata](https://tiles.arcgis.com/tiles/PxbTDTskGHCe4sv6/arcgis/rest/services/Rocque_New/MapServer/3)

Trinity College Dublin independently identifies artificial oyster beds off Clontarf on an extract from Rocque's *Actual Survey of the City of Dublin, 1760*. The Trinity article says these artificial beds were replenished from natural beds elsewhere in Ireland. The ArcGIS service establishes access to a Rocque county map; Trinity's figure specifically supports the Clontarf oyster-bed interpretation. The map service has not yet been visually inspected at the Clontarf location in this research pass, and no oyster-bed geometry or coordinates are asserted here.

Source: [History of the Irish oyster — Trinity College Dublin](https://www.tcd.ie/tceh/projects/foodsmartdublin/recipes/Sept_Oyster/HistoryEcology_oyster.php)

### 6. Dublin City Council's Vernon Avenue report provides a direct comparative map sequence

A Dublin City Council preliminary design report for Vernon Avenue (published in 2026) includes a historical-mapping comparison using three layers: **John Rocque's 1757 map**, the **Ordnance Survey 6-inch map (1829–1841)** and the **Ordnance Survey 25-inch map (1897–1913)**. Its accompanying text states that Vernon Avenue was already established as a route extending north from a cluster of herring sheds fronting the Coast Road, then turning west to join Castle Avenue north of Mount Prospect Avenue. It describes later development as extending northwards, with greater density at the southern end visible in the later OS maps.

This is an important official planning-document synthesis, but it is not a substitute for inspecting the underlying map sheets. The report's wording supports the broad relationship between the sheds, Coast Road and early Vernon Avenue; it does not provide a surveyed footprint or coordinates for individual sheds.

Source: [Dublin City Council — Vernon Avenue Preliminary Design Report (2026), historical mapping section](https://www.dublincity.ie/sites/default/files/2026-06/vernon-avenue_preliminary-design-report_0.pdf)

A separate street-history article transcribes the 1836 Ordnance Survey Name Books description of the road as a bye-road leading through “The Sheds of Clontarf Village” to the shore. It also notes that the route was named Green Lane on Taylor's 1816 map and an 1823 estate map. This is a useful research lead; the original Name Book entry and estate map should be inspected before treating the transcription as a primary-source quotation.

Source: [Vernon Avenue — Streets broad and narrow](https://streetsbroadandnarrow.ie/vernon-avenue/)

**Date distinction:** the Rocque map cited by Dublin City Council is dated 1757 and should not be conflated with the separate County Dublin survey published in 1760 that is available through the South Dublin County Council ArcGIS service and is cited by Trinity's oyster-history page. Both are relevant, but they are distinct cartographic references and must retain their own titles, dates and provenance.

### 5. Later large-scale mapping is available

UCD Digital Library catalogues an Ordnance Survey Ireland map titled *City of Dublin: Township of Clontarf: sheet XVIII.29*, dated 1889, at a scale of 1:1056 (five feet to one statute mile). The catalogue identifies the source as the OSi 19th Century Historical Maps collection and gives source identifier DN018_029.

This is a high-value source for examining late-nineteenth-century plot boundaries, roads and built form. Its catalogue coordinates describe the map item/location and must not be mistaken for the position of an individual feature.

Source: [UCD Digital Library — City of Dublin: Township of Clontarf: sheet XVIII.29 (1889)](https://digital.ucd.ie/c0055/items/c0055_o0511.html)

UCD Library's map guide confirms access routes to historic Dublin maps, including Rocque's 1760 County Dublin map and the 1770–1840 Longfield collection held by the National Library of Ireland. The guide also points researchers to Tailte Éireann's Irish Townland and Historical Map Viewer for historic six-inch and 25-inch mapping, including first-edition six-inch maps and later editions.

Sources:
- [UCD Library — Historic Maps of Dublin](https://libguides.ucd.ie/findingmaps/mapshistDublin)
- [UCD Library — Historic Maps of Ireland and the Townland and Historical Map Viewer](https://libguides.ucd.ie/findingmaps/mapshistireland)

## Working interpretation — not yet a mapped conclusion

The sources support a historical fishing and fish-curing locality associated with the foot of Vernon Avenue and the Clontarf Sheds waterfront. They also support the existence of oyster beds off Clontarf in the eighteenth century and a continuing fish-curing landscape described in 1837.

They do **not yet** establish:
- the exact footprint, number or orientation of individual sheds;
- the precise modern coordinates or boundaries of the oyster beds;
- the exact location of wells, pipes, slipways or water infrastructure;
- whether a particular surviving wall, street line or plot boundary is physically continuous with a feature shown on an eighteenth-century map.

No new physical-object catalogue records or map pins are created by this research note.

## Research update — 26 October 2026

### Rocque map identification: keep editions separate

The date discrepancy is not resolved by treating 1756, 1757 and 1760 as interchangeable dates for one map. The Dictionary of Irish Biography distinguishes John Rocque's *The City and Environs of Dublin* (1756) from *The City and Suburbs of Dublin* (1757), alongside other 1757 publications. UCD Library's historic-map guide independently lists several distinct Rocque titles and dates. Dublin City Council's Vernon Avenue report specifically labels its reproduced map **John Rocque Map 1757**. The South Dublin County Council ArcGIS service is a separate **actual survey of County Dublin (1760)**. Keep these as separate source objects unless inspection of the original sheet establishes a direct relationship.

## Research update — 26 October 2026 (continued)

### Rocque editions: identify the map by title, not by a loose year

UCD Library's historic-map catalogue distinguishes the following separate works:
- 1756: *An Exact Survey of the City and Suburbs of Dublin* (four sheets; Harvard/BNF copies and georeferenced Harvard material are listed).
- 1757: *A Survey of the City, Harbour, Bay and Environs of Dublin on the same Scale as those of London, Paris & Rome*.
- 1757: *Survey of the City and Suburbs of Dublin, with the division of the parishes*, a reduced version from the large four-sheet plan.
- 1760: *An Actual Survey of the County of Dublin*.

The Vernon Avenue article's paragraph calls the lane visible on a Rocque map of 1760, while its footnote cites Rocque 1756. Its own separate explainer says its default Rocque reference is the 1756 *Exact Survey*. This is an unresolved citation mismatch, not proof that any one of these sheets shows the Clontarf lane. Dublin City Council's 2026 preliminary design report labels its reproduced Rocque map 1757. The exact sheet used by each modern author must be identified before making a claim about the sheds or lane.

### County Dublin Name Books: correct volume identified

The digitised *Ordnance Survey Name Books — City of Dublin* compilation edited by Eoin C. Bairéad is a distinct City collection. A search of its 278-page PDF did not locate Clontarf. It must not be cited as the primary Clontarf Name Book.

The National Library of Ireland catalogue separately lists four typed-copy volumes of the County Dublin Ordnance Survey Name Books. Volume I (call number BB6442) runs from Aderrig to Coolock and is the correct volume to investigate for the Clontarf entry. The catalogue places it in the Main Reading Room; it is not offered there as a digitised online page.

### Newly identified primary map lead: Slip Field near Clontarf, 1792

The National Library of Ireland catalogue records a manuscript map titled *A survey of a piece of ground called the Slip field near Clontarf in the Barony of Coolock and County of Dublin let by A. Savage Esq. To John Ash by J.B. 1792. Scale 10 Perches to an Inch*. The catalogue attributes the survey to John Brownrigg, dates it to 1792, places it in the Longfield Map Collection, and states that a digital copy is available. Call number: MS 21 F. 51 / (081).

This is a potentially valuable local landscape/estate-survey lead, but its title only says **near Clontarf**. It does not establish that the surveyed field is the historic fishing sheds, the shore route, or the Vernon Avenue corridor. The map image and its boundaries still need direct inspection before any spatial interpretation. The catalogue says reproduction rights are owned by the National Library of Ireland; do not republish the image without checking permission.
