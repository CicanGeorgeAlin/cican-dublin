## 2. FHG 15 — Coolmine, Blanchardstown Millennium Park

**Address:** Blanchardstown Road, Coolmine, County Dublin, Ireland  
**Survey grid reference:** 306509, 238887 (site-level historic reference; not founder GPS)  
**RMP:** DU013-01902  
**RPS:** 701  
**NIAH:** none listed  
**Survey date:** 17 August 2008

### Site and archaeological description

The site is a raised, roughly oval earthen mound within Blanchardstown Millennium Park, about 1–1.2m above the surrounding ground, with a ring of trees. A slight ditch lies at the base of the eastern side and may represent part of an earlier enclosure. The 2008 survey found no visible grave markers.

The historical account says the church of Coolmine appears in the *Crede Mihi* around AD 1275 as the church of Culmyn and in the 1292 and 1294 taxation records. Archbishop Allen later claimed it had gone out of existence around 1490. The church is traditionally associated with the White Chapel of St Machutus or Mochta and with a monastery founded by St Mochta, but this is presented in the survey as tradition and historical interpretation, not proof of a surviving monastic building.

The survey records that human bone had been exposed in the area in the past. A local tradition recounts bones uncovered during ploughing, collected and reburied, after which ploughing stopped. A separate supernatural tale about a man digging for potting mould is folklore and should not be presented as historical fact.

### Archive treatment

- The raised mound and tree-ring are the principal archaeological feature and should be represented as one site-level landform/monument, not as separate invented graves.
- The eastern ditch is a possible enclosure feature, not a confirmed complete enclosure.
- No visible individual gravestones were recorded in 2008. Do not invent markers or infer precise burial locations from the mound.
- No individual bones or historic burials should be mapped as objects.
- Any future field photography should document the landform and boundary from public, safe access, without probing, digging or disturbing soil.

## 3. FHG 16 — Chapelmidway, Kilsallaghan

**Address:** Kilsallaghan, County Dublin, Ireland  
**Survey grid reference:** 312295, 246345 (site-level historic reference; not founder GPS)  
**RMP:** DU011-02202 (graveyard); DU011-02201 (church/tower remains, based on survey context)  
**RPS:** 641  
**NIAH:** none listed  
**Survey date:** 19 August 2008

### Historic church remains

Chapelmidway is named for its position midway between the old church sites at Kilsallaghan and St Margaret's. The 2008 survey says all that survives of the original church above ground is the base of a west tower, while foundations can be traced for approximately 8m east of the tower. A segmental-headed opening in the east wall leads to a barrel-vaulted ground-floor chamber. An external staircase on the south side leads to the first floor. Wall footings also extend east of the tower, with possible footings south of it.

The survey notes that the church was already in ruins by 1615. A local tradition attributes its destruction to the time of Cromwell; that story is tradition, not a securely established cause or date.

**Identifier caution:** the 2008 survey's archaeological-features paragraph appears to label the tower remains with DU011-02201 while its site-information box labels the graveyard DU011-02202. Preserve the distinction and confirm against the current NMS Sites and Monuments Record before any permanent catalogue entry.

### Individual grave-marker candidates

1. **John Kelly / Patrick Kelly two-sided mid-eighteenth-century stone.** The survey calls this the oldest marker, located southeast of the church. Its east face reads: “Jesus though to ye merits of thy passion deliver us John Kelly Patrick Kelly”. Its west face begins: “This stone belongs to”. The remainder is not supplied in the survey text. Record both faces as one physical stone, not two objects.
2. **Broken stone with crest and Crucifixion plaque.** Located east of the tower. The survey describes it as broken; the family identity and inscription are not provided in the text. Photograph both fragments in situ and establish whether the crest and Crucifixion are on one or separate pieces before counting objects.
3. **Uninscribed pillar stone.** Located east of the tower, described as a separate pillar stone without inscription. It may be a grave marker or another stone feature; keep its function provisional until field review.
4. **Possible reused architectural fragment.** Located north of the church. No exact form or original function supplied; research lead only pending inspection of survey plate and current survival.
5. **Slate slabs south of the church.** The survey records broken slabs with lamination. Do not count each loose piece as a separate monument until the physical joins and original layout are understood.
6. **Cast-iron cross and timber crosses.** The survey records these among twentieth-century markers. Individual inscriptions, dates and positions are not supplied in the text and require photo review.
7. **Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century headstones and free-standing crosses.** Earlier markers are concentrated east of the tower and include curved tops and IHS sunbursts. Individual entries require identification and transcription.

### Conservation observations

The survey describes the graveyard as well maintained and no longer in use in 2008. It identifies broken slate slabs south of the church, lamination, broken and loose stone around a tree in the southeast corner, and the need for careful maintenance of church masonry. It recommends accurate recording of fragments before any movement and careful treatment of vegetation. These are historical condition observations, not permission to carry out works.
