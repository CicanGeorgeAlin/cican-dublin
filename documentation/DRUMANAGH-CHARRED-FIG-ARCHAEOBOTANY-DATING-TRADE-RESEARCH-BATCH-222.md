# Drumanagh Charred Fig — Archaeobotanical Identification, Dating and Trade Interpretation (Research Batch 222)

**Project:** CICAN Dublin Archive  
**Site:** Drumanagh Promontory Fort, County Dublin  
**Recorded monument:** DU008-006001  
**Discovery programme:** Digging Drumanagh, identified during 2024 post-excavation analysis of earlier excavation material  
**Object:** Charred fig fruit fragment (*Ficus carica*)  
**Research status:** Documentation-only; no production catalogue or map changes  
**Research date:** 20 November 2026

## Executive finding

A large charred fig fruit fragment from Drumanagh was identified in 2024 by archaeobotanist Associate Professor Meriel McClatchie of University College Dublin. Its identification was checked in collaboration with specialists at Historic England's Fort Cumberland laboratories. The discovery is exceptional: the project and UCD describe it as the oldest example of an exotic fruit found in Ireland and the first actual fig fruit recovered from an Irish archaeological excavation; earlier Irish fig evidence consisted of seeds from medieval urban sites.

**Crucial dating qualification:** attempts to date the fig seeds directly by accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) were unsuccessful. The published date of **AD 105–222** is a radiocarbon date for the archaeological context, not a successful direct date on the fig itself. The fruit is therefore associated with a context dated to that range; the date must not be presented as a direct laboratory date of the fruit.

The identification and dating provide strong evidence that a fig fruit was present at Drumanagh in a Roman-period context. A likely southern European/Mediterranean origin and import through Roman-world trade routes are reasonable interpretations advanced by the specialists, but the precise growing location, trade route, trader and consumer are not established by the fruit alone.

## 1. Identification and specialist examination

The object was initially thought to resemble a rough or “gnarly” apple. During post-excavation work in 2024, McClatchie identified it as a charred fig. The visible internal structure and embedded seeds were decisive for the identification.

UCD reports that:
- the find is a large charred fragment of fig fruit;
- the fruit was preserved by charring;
- the specimen was examined at a Historic England laboratory;
- microscope imagery was captured using an AHRC-funded Keyence VHX7000 3-D digital microscope at ×30 magnification;
- Historic England specialists Dr Ruth Pelling and Gill Campbell contributed to confirming the significance of the identification.

The UCD image caption identifies the photographed specimen as the charred fig from Drumanagh and credits Historic England.

## 2. Dating: direct sample versus associated context

The specialist report published by *The Past* makes an important distinction:
- AMS dating of the fig seeds was attempted but was **unsuccessful**.
- Radiocarbon dating of the **context** returned a range of **AD 105–222**.

This is the most precise public statement found in this research about the dating method and result. Fingal and UCD describe the fig in public-facing summaries as “dating back 2000 years”, but the more technical account explains that the successful date belongs to the archaeological context.

**Correct archive wording:** “Charred fig fruit fragment recovered from an archaeological context radiocarbon-dated to AD 105–222; direct AMS dating of the fig seeds was unsuccessful.”

Do not write:
- “The fig itself was directly radiocarbon dated to AD 105–222.”
- “The fig has an exact age of 2,000 years.”
- “The seed AMS test confirmed the date.”

The context date gives a chronological association, not a direct measurement of the fruit's age. The full laboratory certificate, sample material and context record are needed to assess the dating in greater detail.

## 3. Archaeological context: what is public and what remains unknown

Public project sources state that the fig was recovered during the Drumanagh excavation programme and identified during post-excavation analysis in 2024. The Past gives the context's radiocarbon range, AD 105–222.

The public sources reviewed for this batch do **not** provide a securely verifiable:
- excavation season and trench number for the fig;
- context or feature number;
- sample number or flotation sample register reference;
- exact stratigraphic description;
- precise find coordinates within the trench;
- full laboratory code or dating certificate.

The Past article discusses the fig alongside the excavation's storage pits and spelt wheat, but the text does not explicitly assign the fig to a named pit. It would be unsafe to assign it to Season IV Trench 5A, F108 or F142 without the primary archaeobotanical register.

**Research action:** obtain the archaeobotanical report and original context/sampling records from the Digging Drumanagh project. The public announcement is not a substitute for the specimen's context sheet.

## 4. Botanical and comparative significance

UCD and Fingal describe the specimen as the first actual fig fruit found in an Irish archaeological excavation and the oldest exotic fruit example identified in Ireland. They distinguish it from earlier fig **seeds** recovered from medieval Dublin, Cork and other Irish towns, some dating as far back as the 13th century.

The find is a charred fruit fragment, not merely an isolated seed. It preserves internal structures and numerous seeds visible in the microscopy image. The public sources identify it as fig (*Ficus carica*) but do not publish a detailed botanical measurement sheet, seed count, cultivar identification or genetic analysis.

No cultivar, precise geographic growing origin or method of drying can be established from the public material reviewed here.

## 5. Trade and consumption interpretation

Figs were widely traded in the Roman world and could be transported dried. Specialists suggest that the Drumanagh fruit likely travelled from southern Europe, consistent with other fig finds in northern Europe and the wider distribution of Mediterranean foods.

The Drumanagh discovery is part of a broader assemblage indicating contact with Roman Britain and the Romanised world:
- Dressel 20 amphorae used to transport olive oil from Baetica in southern Spain;
- significant quantities of spelt wheat, a cereal associated with Roman Britain and relatively rare in Ireland's past;
- Roman-world pottery, glass and metal objects;
- local craft and domestic activity.

The evidence suggests that imported foods and objects reached people at Drumanagh. It does not establish that the person who ate the fig was Roman, that the fruit was consumed by a trader rather than a local inhabitant, or that the site was under Roman political or military control. UCD explicitly notes that the Roman Empire did not extend into Ireland.

## 6. What this object does and does not prove

### Established by the published sources
1. A charred fig fruit fragment was recovered from the Drumanagh excavation programme.
2. The specimen was identified by archaeobotanist Meriel McClatchie and examined with Historic England specialists.
3. It is the first actual fig fruit reported from an Irish archaeological excavation and is exceptional in the Irish archaeobotanical record.
4. Direct AMS dating of the fig seeds was unsuccessful.
5. The archaeological context returned a radiocarbon range of AD 105–222.
6. The specimen is evidence for the presence of an exotic fruit at Drumanagh in a context of Roman-period date.

### Not established by the published sources
- exact context/feature number and trench;
- direct radiocarbon age of the fig;
- precise source region, farm or orchard;
- exact route by which the fruit reached Ireland;
- identity or origin of the person who brought, stored or consumed it;
- whether it was eaten at the site or arrived as waste;
- whether it was imported dried or in another preserved state;
- the exact date of burning;
- a Roman military presence or Roman administration at Drumanagh.

**Batch 222 conclusion:** The Drumanagh fig is an exceptional charred fruit fragment identified in 2024, but the key scientific distinction is that direct AMS dating of its seeds failed. The AD 105–222 radiocarbon range belongs to the archaeological context. The find supports the presence of an exotic fruit at Drumanagh during the Roman-period horizon and fits a broader pattern of imported foods and objects, but its exact trench/context and route of arrival require the primary archaeobotanical and excavation records.
