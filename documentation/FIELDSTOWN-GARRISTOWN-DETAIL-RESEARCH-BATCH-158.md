## 2. FHG 18 — Fieldstown, Clonmethan

**Address:** Fieldstown, Clonmethan, County Dublin, Ireland  
**Survey date:** 5 November 2008  
**Survey grid reference:** 311598, 250387  
**RMP:** DU011-00202 (graveyard), DU011-00201 (medieval chapel), DU011-00203 and DU007-03001 (surrounding enclosures/field divisions)  
**RPS:** 326  
**NIAH:** none listed  
**Ownership:** Private (2008 survey)  
**Setting:** Pasture within the grounds of Fieldstown House, west of the R122.

### A. Raised oval burial enclosure and tree ring

The graveyard is a raised oval area defined by a low earthen bank and tree ring. The survey says the site was used for burials of unbaptised children. By the early twentieth century, old graves were reportedly visible; the 2008 survey found small pieces of masonry throughout the enclosure, potentially uninscribed grave markers or masonry. A single upright piece of limestone was visible.

**Archive treatment:** record the raised oval landform and tree ring as the parent archaeological feature. The scattered masonry and upright limestone are candidates, not confirmed gravestones; do not create separate objects until identified and located. The burial tradition should be attributed to the survey/historical source, not represented as a complete burial register.

### B. Medieval chapel foundations — St Catherine

The chapel was dedicated to St Catherine and had fallen into ruin by the seventeenth century. Its rectangular foundation platform is traceable in the centre of the raised area. Loose masonry runs north-south across the platform. This is a site component of the graveyard's archaeological remains, not a separate modern building.

The survey says there are no upstanding early medieval features visible. Do not describe the chapel as early medieval without further evidence.

### C. St Catherine's Well — west face of field bank

A holy well lies in the west face of a field bank at Fieldstown House, enclosed by a brick and stone structure. It was traditionally associated with St Catherine. The survey records that the former pattern is no longer venerated.

**Archive treatment:** treat the well as a distinct research candidate from the graveyard mound, with its own location and evidence. The source gives a descriptive location but no exact coordinate. Do not pin it at the graveyard site coordinate or claim current access. The RPS schedule lists St Catherine's Well as a “site of” holy well in Staffordstown; confirm whether this refers to this same well before assigning a separate archive record or map point.

### D. Surrounding field divisions / enclosure system

The survey records scarps and earthen banks in the surrounding fields, with identifiers DU011-00203 and DU007-03001. It does not establish that these are all parts of a single ecclesiastical enclosure. Preserve the separate monument references and avoid merging them.

### E. Cast-iron cross attached to tree

The survey's general description specifically identifies a single cast-iron cross attached to a tree at the east end of the site. This is a discrete visual candidate, but its present survival and the stability of the tree attachment require current field verification. Do not detach or touch it.

### F. Condition and access

The 2008 survey described the site as fair but accessible to cattle. The graveyard is within private Fieldstown House grounds. The report recommended fencing and specialist advice before any clearance. No public access is implied. Do not plan founder photography without permission from the landowner.

## 3. FHG 19 — Garristown, Church of the Virgin Mary

**Address:** Garristown, County Dublin, Ireland  
**Survey date:** 27 July 2008  
**Survey grid reference:** 307086, 258818  
**RMP:** DU003-01103 (graveyard/church complex); DU003-01101 (church)  
**RPS:** 121  
**NIAH:** 11313010 (graveyard)  
**Excavation reference:** 1990:035  
**Ownership:** Fingal County Council.

### A. Reused whetstone at the north-wall stile — exceptional micro-detail

The Fingal survey identifies a small flat, approximately square stone reused as the top step of the stile in the north wall of the graveyard. The separate council archaeological resource report describes it as approximately 0.3m thick by 0.4m wide by 0.4m long, with score marks on the upper surface. It is not in its original position. The report calls it the only whetstone in Fingal.

**Archive treatment:** this is a particularly strong individual micro-detail candidate. It is a reused stone with a specific secondary function and identifiable scoring. Preserve both interpretations: probable whetstone in original use; reused as the stile's top step in its present historic context. Do not remove, lift or clean it. Photograph in situ from safe public access, including the stile and north wall context.

### B. Female effigial slab — removed from the graveyard

The 2008 graveyard survey records fragments of a sixteenth-century female effigial slab once within the graveyard, now removed. The 1990 excavation report documents fragments of effigy tombs reused as grave markers, including a large female effigy fragment, and records negotiations to bring the slabs into safe-keeping. Fingal's heritage feature describes the lower half of a female effigy now in Garristown library: a heavy woollen skirt raised to reveal a smock, with feet resting on a tasselled cushion. It is dated to the fifteenth century and described as Pale-school figure sculpture.

**Important discrepancy:** the 2008 survey calls the removed female slab sixteenth-century; Fingal's later heritage feature dates the lower half to the fifteenth century. The 1990 excavation report discusses multiple fragments and planned safe-keeping at an OPW visitor centre in Lusk, while the council heritage feature says the lower half is now in Garristown library. These records may refer to different fragments or later movements. Do not assume they are all the same piece or state a current location without checking with Fingal Local Studies/Archives and the relevant collection custodian.

**Archive treatment:** create no on-site pin for the removed effigy. If later confirmed as a single object, record it as an off-site collection object with provenance and custody history; if multiple fragments, retain separate identities.

### C. Medieval / post-medieval church remains

The present ruined church was built around 1814 and probably occupies the site of the medieval parish church dedicated to the Virgin Mary. It has a two-stage west tower with battlements, pointed-arched ground-floor openings, blind round-headed and pointed upper openings, a three-bay nave and a pointed east window. A 1532 reference records the church as a possession of the Hospital of St John of Jerusalem.

The survey says there are no upstanding archaeological features of early medieval date at the site. Do not describe the visible church as an early medieval building.

### D. Catrine Russel headstone — 1637

The earliest visible stone in the 2008 survey was excavated during the digging of new plots and reads: “Here lieth the body of Catrine Russel who died the 12th of May 1637 aged 65 years”. It is described as the earliest visible headstone, but the text does not supply an exact current position or detailed physical description.

**Archive treatment:** strong memorial candidate; confirm current survival, exact position and inscription from the original survey plate and a current photograph. Do not infer that its original location is known.

### E. Other historic grave markers and reused fragments

The graveyard contains eighteenth-, nineteenth- and twentieth-century headstones and individual table tombs, generally east-facing and made of limestone, granite and marble. Many are dedicated to Dublin merchants. There are also numerous undecorated stone markers at the east end and reused architectural fragments. An early nineteenth-century Rogers family slab has a broken top; some other headstones are cracked or flaking.

These are a discovery set, not separate object records until each monument is individually distinguished and described.

### F. North-wall stile and cast-iron pedestrian gate

The graveyard has a cast-iron pedestrian gate on the south side and a stile in the north wall. The whetstone is the top step of the north stile. Preserve the gate and stile as architectural context for the micro-detail, but do not create separate objects for ordinary components without evidence of independent significance.

### G. Site history and condition

The 2008 survey says the graveyard is well and regularly maintained, and its extension remains in use. The graveyard is on high ground above the village, west of the R130. The official Buried in Fingal record gives the cemetery location at latitude 53.56828417, longitude -6.38418163. This is a cemetery locator from the council burial-ground database, not a location for the whetstone or effigy fragments.
