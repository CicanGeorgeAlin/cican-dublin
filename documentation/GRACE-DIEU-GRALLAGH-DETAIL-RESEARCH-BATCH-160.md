# CICAN Dublin Archive — Grace Dieu & Grallagh Detail Research (Batch 160)

**Status:** Research-only. No permanent archive IDs, catalogue records or new map pins approved.  
**Scope:** Fingal Historic Graveyards Project FHG 22 (Grace Dieu) and FHG 23 (Grallagh).  
**Method:** Prioritise Fingal County Council's 2008 survey, cross-check later official records and keep distinct archaeological components separate.

## 1. Grace Dieu — FHG 22

### Site identity
- Surveyed 4 November 2008.
- Townland: Gracedieu; parish: Lusk; County Dublin, Ireland.
- Survey grid reference: ITM 318056, 252468.
- RMP cemetery reference: DU007-01510. Associated complex components include DU007-01501 (priory/church building), DU007-01502 (grave slabs), DU007-01503 and DU007-01504 (two holy-well sites), DU007-01505 (possible mound/barrow), DU007-01506 (watermill), DU007-01507 and DU007-01508 (bridges), DU007-01509 (architectural/grave-slab fragment) and DU007-01511 (enclosure).
- Ownership was recorded as private in the 2008 survey. Treat access as permission-only until current landowner arrangements are confirmed.
- Setting: south of the R129, in flat tillage and pasture fields. Archaeological investigations identified two concentric enclosure ditches.

### Priory and standing fabric
- The cemetery is associated with the Priory of St Mary, endowed for Arrosian nuns by John Cumin, Archbishop of Dublin, around 1190.
- The 2008 survey states that only the south-east corner of the nunnery building survives, with sections of its south wall. A segmental-headed opening has limestone voussoirs; a stretch of east-west random-rubble limestone wall runs across the site.
- The survey's Plate 22.2 shows a standing, vegetation-covered ruin with an arched opening near its base and a tree growing from the top. Treat this image as a survey-era view, not evidence of current condition.
- The priory's former complex reportedly included a watermill, horse mill, dovecote, messuages and cottages. The claim that Turvey House, built by Patrick Barnewall in 1841, reused stone from Grace Dieu is reported as a supposition in the survey, not a proven material provenance.

### Grave slabs and the key discrepancy
- The survey records two 16th-century grave slabs under RMP component DU007-01502. It states one is north of the church.
- Its general description, however, says only one gravestone was evident during the 2008 inspection: an inscribed 16th-century slab north of the church, with a Latin inscription around its margins.
- Do not silently turn this into two currently visible slabs. Catalogue the component group as a source-recorded pair, but keep the visible slab and the second slab's present location/condition unresolved until the original excavation records and site photographs are checked.
- The surviving slab was considered vulnerable; the survey specifically recommended a conservation plan to prevent further deterioration. Do not touch, clean, lift or attempt to read it through physical contact.

### Wider archaeological landscape — separate records to investigate
1. **Two concentric enclosure ditches** — archaeological investigations found two ditches around the site. Distinct from the later graveyard boundary and from the priory structure.
2. **Two holy-well sites** — separate RMP components DU007-01503 and DU007-01504. Their exact positions and names were not established from the graveyard survey extract; do not assume they are visible or assign names without the excavation reports.
3. **Possible mound/barrow** — DU007-01505 is described as a possible mound/barrow. Classification is tentative.
4. **Watermill site** — DU007-01506; historical documentary context mentions a watermill and horse mill. Do not treat both mills as separately located archaeological remains without evidence.
5. **Two bridges** — DU007-01507 and DU007-01508; likely associated with the wider historic landscape, but their exact locations and surviving fabric require independent verification.
6. **Architectural/grave-slab fragment** — DU007-01509; determine whether this is one of the 16th-century slabs or a separate displaced fragment before creating an object record.
7. **Enclosure** — DU007-01511; retain separately from the cemetery and the two concentric ditches until the RMP geometry and excavation plans clarify the relationship.

### Condition and fieldwork
- The 2008 survey rated the site poor, overgrown by trees and vegetation and not maintained; only one grave slab was visible.
- Survey photographs are FHG 22_AP and FHG 22_1 to FHG 22_9.
- First priority is to obtain excavation reports 1988:16, 1988:17 and 1999:248, plus the original plan drawings, before fieldwork.
- If access is granted, document the ruin, voussoirs, east-west wall, visible grave slab and boundary ditch from safe public/authorised viewpoints. Do not clear vegetation or undertake any conservation work.

## 2. Grallagh — FHG 23

### Site identity
- Surveyed 24 July 2008.
- Townland and parish: Grallagh; address: Grallagh, Naul, County Dublin, Ireland.
- Survey grid reference: ITM 310743, 256443.
- RMP: DU007-00104 (graveyard); associated components DU007-00101 (church), DU007-00102 (holy well), DU007-00103 (holy stone).
- RPS 141; NIAH 11314007 (graveyard).
- Fingal County Council ownership/management is recorded in the survey and current Buried in Fingal cemetery information.
- Survey location description: in tillage fields on a third-class road west of the R122. The official Buried in Fingal page gives WGS84 53.54645726, -6.33015776. Preserve the survey ITM and official WGS84 values as separate source fields; verify by GIS conversion and field GPS before publishing a canonical coordinate.

### Medieval church
- Dedicated to St Michael; the parish church was certainly built before 1306.
- The west gable survives to approximately 2m high, built of roughly coursed limestone. The other walls survive as earthen and stone foundations.
- A 19th-century headstone has been inserted into the church masonry. It is a distinct memorial object and a distinct example of reused fabric; photograph its exact insertion location and transcribe it before any separate catalogue record.
- The 2011 structural assessment describes the west wall as approximately 5.4m long and 2.6m high, and describes the church interior as a flat hollow of approximately 6m by 6m. The measurements differ from the 2008 survey's c.2m height; preserve the survey dates and source-specific measurements rather than averaging them.
- The 2011 report notes a depression in the top of the east wall which may be the remnant of a former window recorded by Walsh in 1888. This is a possible architectural feature, not a confirmed intact window.

### St MacCullin's Well / St Michael's Well
- RMP component DU007-00102. The 2008 FHG survey calls it St Michael's Well and places it at the south end of the graveyard.
- The well is accessed by steps covered by a later stone building. A specialist holy-wells study identifies the name St MacCullin's Well (Tobar Mhic Chuilinn), also known as St Michael's Well and St Patrick's Well; it describes a tall pitched stone structure, Gothic-arched entrance, four steps down to the water and a shamrock carved into the top step.
- Local accounts disagree on dedication: St Michael, St Patrick and St MacCullin appear in different sources. Preserve all aliases and source attributions rather than selecting one as definitive.
- Reported cures (whooping cough, sore eyes, eye disorders and rheumatism) are folklore, not medical claims. A pattern day was held on the first Sunday in August.
- The shamrock on the top step is a high-priority micro-detail to photograph if visible. Do not enter the structure or disturb the water/stonework.

### Holy stone / boulder outside the entrance
- RMP component DU007-00103, outside the graveyard entrance adjacent to the public road.
- Fingal's 2012 archaeological resource report describes a water-eroded, irregular boulder approximately 1m in diameter, located about 1m from the graveyard wall at the entrance. It identifies it as Fingal's only recorded holy stone.
- Folklore varies: one tradition associates it with St Michael and a cure for backache; Oldtown Schools' Collection material records a St Patrick tradition involving five finger holes and sore hands. Do not label the boulder as physically bearing a saint's handprint without evidence; describe the erosion/hollows neutrally and keep folklore in a separate field.
- This boulder is outside the cemetery boundary and must have its own object/location record if field-verified.

### Enclosure and graveyard layout
- A curved ditch runs along the northern, western and southern boundaries; the 2008 survey interprets it as possibly representing an early medieval ecclesiastical enclosure.
- The cemetery is sub-rectangular, raised above surrounding ground, with an east limestone wall and coping, cast-iron gate and stone stile. Ground rises toward the church.
- Do not merge the ditch/enclosure with the modern graveyard wall. Record the archaeological interpretation as tentative.

### Memorials and individual details
1. **Anna Doran / Thomas Mangan headstone, 1768** — earliest inscribed marker identified by the FHG survey. Transcription: “IHS This stone was erected by Anna Doran in memory of her husband Thomas Mangan who departed this life Mar ye 18th 1768 aged 44 years”. Verify letterforms and punctuation against the stone; the quoted transcription is source-derived.
2. **19th-century headstone inserted into church masonry** — record separately from the wall and church ruin; the 2008 report does not transcribe the name in the relevant passage.
3. **Broken headstones** — the 2008 survey notes several broken markers. Each fragment requires an in-situ photograph, orientation, dimensions and any matching-fragment assessment before consolidation.
4. **Free-standing crosses and later grave markers** — 18th-21st century markers are present. Do not create generic aggregate object entries where individual stones can be distinguished.
5. **Modern burial plots** — concentrated in the western end according to the survey. Do not assume every plot is a heritage object.

### Condition and fieldwork
- The 2008 survey rated the graveyard generally good and regularly maintained, but the church remains poor, with disturbed archaeological material around it and possible undermining of wall foundations by burials within the church.
- The 2011 structural assessment records a 19th-century marker built into the east face of the church wall and recommends monitoring/repair of the church fabric.
- The official Buried in Fingal page describes the site as still in use and supplies burial records.
- Survey photographs: FHG 23_AP and FHG 23_1 to FHG 23_18.
- Fieldwork priorities: photograph the church walls and possible window depression; inserted headstone; well building, Gothic arch, steps and shamrock; holy stone from all sides with scale; ditch profile and boundary transition; 1768 Mangan memorial; and each broken marker in situ.

## 3. Discrepancies and archive controls

- Grace Dieu: RMP component register lists two 16th-century grave slabs, but the 2008 site description says only one was visible. Present status of the second slab is unresolved.
- Grace Dieu: component DU007-01509 is separately listed as an architectural/grave-slab feature; relationship to the two slabs needs the RMP file and excavation drawings.
- Grace Dieu: two holy wells, mound, mill, two bridges and enclosure are separate RMP components. No exact object-level coordinates assigned in this report.
- Grallagh: saint-name and healing traditions for the well and holy stone vary across sources. Keep folklore attributed and do not present cures as fact.
- Grallagh: church wall height differs between the 2008 survey (c.2m) and 2011 structural assessment (c.2.6m); retain source/date context.
- No permanent archive IDs assigned; no map pins or production catalogue changes made.
- Master catalogue access and duplicate clearance remain prerequisites for permanent publication.
