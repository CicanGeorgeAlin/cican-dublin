# CICAN Dublin Archive — Howth Old Abbey & St Mary's (CofI) Detail Research (Batch 163)

**Status:** Research consolidation and source reconciliation only. No permanent archive IDs, new catalogue records or production pins created.  
**Scope:** FHG 28 St Mary's Abbey, Howth (Old Abbey) and FHG 29 St Mary's (Church of Ireland), Howth.  
**Important duplicate control:** These two sites were already investigated in Batch 150. This batch is a deeper source reconciliation and adds no duplicate candidate objects without fresh evidence.

## 1. Two different Howth burial grounds — do not merge

There are at least two distinct St Mary's burial grounds in Howth:
- **St Mary's Abbey / Howth Old Abbey (FHG 28)** on Church Street, on the height overlooking Howth Harbour. Medieval collegiate church ruins stand within the graveyard.
- **St Mary's (Church of Ireland) (FHG 29)** on Howth Road, west of Howth village, associated with the mid-19th-century parish church.

The Schools' Collection account explicitly distinguishes “St Mary's, behind St Mary's Parochial Hall” from “the Abbey in and around St Mary's Abbey” and St Fintan's. This is a useful historic naming check, but the account's statement that all were then in use should not be applied to current status.

## 2. FHG 28 — St Mary's Abbey, Howth / Old Abbey

### Site identity and designations
- FHG survey date: 6 September 2008.
- Townland/parish: Howth / Howth.
- Address: Church Street, Howth, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG survey ITM: 328718, 239185.
- RMP graveyard component: DU015-02906.
- Wider ecclesiastical complex: DU015-029 (Ecclesiastical Remains); DU015-02901 (church); DU015-02902 (holy well); DU015-02903 (altar tomb); DU015-02904 (grave slabs); DU015-02905 (architectural fragment); DU015-02906 (graveyard).
- RPS 568; NIAH 11359010 (graveyard). Ownership/management: Fingal County Council and OPW; the abbey is a National Monument in State ownership.
- Official Buried in Fingal locator: 53.38740693, -6.06592917. The NIAH record gives coordinates 328726,239195; these are close to the survey ITM but must remain source-specific until GIS normalization.
- The graveyard closed to new burials on 31 May 1973, according to the FHG survey.

### Historical sequence — keep attributed
- The first church is reputed to have been founded by Sigtrygg (Sitric), King of Dublin, around 1042.
- In 1235 the church was amalgamated with the church on Ireland's Eye and rededicated to the Blessed Virgin Mary by Luke, Archbishop of Dublin.
- Much of the surviving abbey building dates to the late 14th and early 15th centuries, with later additions and alterations.
- The ruins south of the abbey are identified as a 15th- or 16th-century college where members of the community lived.
- Heritage Ireland describes the current ruin as likely late-14th/early-15th-century and notes the two earlier churches; it restricts visitors to external access and does not permit internal access. Follow current OPW access instructions; do not enter closed or restricted areas.

### Architectural and memorial objects
1. **Double-aisled collegiate church** — random-rubble limestone, divided by an arcade, with triple bellcote on west gable and triple-light east window. Keep the church structure as parent architecture, distinct from each embedded memorial or fragment.
2. **Christopher St Lawrence and Anna Plunkett effigy tomb** — mid-15th-century altar tomb (RMP DU015-02903), carved c.1470, in the chantry chapel at the south-east corner of the south aisle. The tomb commemorates Christopher St Lawrence, 13th Baron of Howth (d.1462), and his wife Anna Plunkett of Ratoath. The effigies, tomb chest, heraldry and any inscriptions should be separately documented as features of one tomb object, not assumed to be separate tombs.
3. **Two medieval cross slabs attached to church walls** — one attached to the east wall and one to the south wall of the south aisle. Both carry an inscribed cross with stepped base. Record as two distinct slabs, preserving their exact attachment locations.
4. **Third medieval grave slab reused in flowerbed kerbing** — located west of the church; sandstone with stepped base and thin cross shaft. This is a displaced/reused slab and its current landscaping function must be recorded separately from its original burial function.
5. **Additional medieval slab built into kerbing in the south-west corner** — the FHG architectural-features section describes a medieval grave slab built into kerbing in the south-west corner. It may correspond to the third slab described in the general description, but do not assume this without comparing Plate 28 photographs and on-site location.
6. **Holy spout / eye spout** — water currently issues from a short pipe in the retaining wall. The survey reports that the pipe is said to have come from a holy well dedicated to the Virgin Mary. Local tradition attributes a cure for sore eyes. The water outlet, retaining-wall feature and historical source well should not be conflated; the present pipe may not be the original well structure.
7. **Late-medieval structures along southern boundary** — separate from the church and cemetery wall. Map and describe only after component-by-component inspection.
8. **Loose architectural fragments** — loose stone is reported across much of the southern graveyard; several reused architectural fragments are noted. Do not assign original building context without a diagnostic match.

### Other grave markers and conservation
- The graveyard contains markers from c.1050 onward, including medieval markers and 18th-, 19th-, 20th- and 21st-century burials. The official survey says markers are predominantly east-facing and made from marble, limestone and granite.
- Table tombs include limestone slabs supported by granite legs. Simple undecorated stones are also present.
- FHG 2008 noted broken and leaning slabs and an overgrown cast-iron-railed plot in the north-east corner. It recommended stability checks, careful fragment recording, specialist vegetation advice and boundary-wall assessment.
- The NIAH record describes interments from c.1050 to 1973 and the abbey ruin within the burial ground.
- The official Buried in Fingal page transcribes a memorial to Anne Flinn (d.1766), including a six-line verse. Treat the transcription as source-derived until matched to the stone in situ.
- The same page records the burial ground as closed to new burials for many years and maintained by Fingal County Council.

### Fieldwork / photography priorities
- If access is permitted, photograph the two attached cross slabs separately, with context and scale.
- Photograph the sandstone cross slab reused in the flowerbed kerb and determine whether it is the same feature as the south-west kerbing slab.
- Photograph the St Lawrence/Plunkett tomb, effigies, heraldry, chest and location without touching or entering restricted areas.
- Record the triple bellcote, east window, arcade, chantry chapel and southern college/late-medieval structures as separate architectural features.
- Photograph the eye spout and retaining wall, noting the present pipe and the uncertain provenance of the original well.
- Locate Anne Flinn's 1766 memorial and verify the transcription against the stone.
- Survey loose fragments and overgrown plot only under authorised conservation supervision.

## 3. FHG 29 — St Mary's (Church of Ireland), Howth

### Site identity and designations
- FHG survey date: 6 August 2008.
- Townland: Howth Demesne; parish: Howth.
- Address: Howth Road, Howth, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG survey ITM: 327874, 239285.
- RMP: DU015-042 (burial ground).
- RPS 594 (church); NIAH 11358026 (church).
- Denomination: Church of Ireland; ownership recorded as Representative Church Body.
- The 2008 survey says the present church was built in 1866. The graveyard is on the south side of the R105, west of Howth village.
- The FHG survey describes the graveyard as well maintained and still in use at the time of the survey. The church construction disturbed earlier human remains.

### 1866 church and archaeological discoveries
- The current church is a mid-19th-century building, with a five-bay nave, oriel windows, side aisles, double- and triple-light windows, snecked granite masonry, dressed quoins, sandstone opening surrounds and a four-stage tower at the north-west corner.
- Human remains were discovered during construction of the church in the mid-19th century. Westropp (1922) also records sword fragments and a jet ring among finds associated with the remains.
- A local legend associated the artefacts with a mythical battle at Evora Bridge in 1177. This is folklore, not established archaeological provenance.
- The FHG survey states that no upstanding archaeological features were associated with the site, although the church may occupy an earlier church or graveyard.
- **2017 archaeological excavation (17E0254):** Excavations.ie records significant articulated human remains at St Mary's Church, Howth, on the earlier known medieval burial ground DU015:042. Preliminary results identified a minimum of 31 articulated individuals, plus substantial disarticulated human remains and animal bone. The report says the burials pre-date construction of the first church and are most likely early medieval, associated with a cemetery/settlement site.
- The 2017 excavation provides materially stronger evidence for an early burial landscape than the 2008 survey alone. It does not prove the sword fragments or jet ring belong to the same individuals or burial phase. Keep the excavation assemblage and 19th-century construction discoveries as separate source records until their contexts are reconciled.

### Remembrance garden and memorial features
- The FHG survey describes a small remembrance garden north of the church.
- The garden contains memorial stones set flat into the lawn, large inscribed mural tablets on the east wall, and a large iron cross at the east end.
- These are separate memorial objects within a designed garden space. Record each tablet, each lawn-set memorial stone and the iron cross independently if individually distinguishable.
- Entrance composition: curved wall sweep, square pillars and decorative cast-iron gates. The boundary wall and gates are architectural features, not memorial objects.
- The 2008 survey noted no conservation issues and recommended regular maintenance.

### FHG 29 fieldwork priorities
1. Locate the remembrance garden and photograph each flat-set memorial stone, mural tablet and the large iron cross independently.
2. Record all inscriptions on the east-wall tablets, including names, dates, dedications, material and dimensions.
3. Photograph the curved entrance wall, square pillars and cast-iron gates.
4. Obtain the complete 2017 excavation report and plans for licence 17E0254; establish excavation limits, contexts, skeletal minimum number, finds and final dating.
5. Seek the 19th-century construction records or Westropp's original 1922 account to clarify the sword fragments and jet ring's find context and subsequent custody.
6. Do not disturb, photograph identifiable human remains without authorisation, or treat archaeological excavation assemblages as publicly accessible objects.

## 4. Duplicate reconciliation with Batch 150

- Batch 150 already recorded the Howth Old Abbey cross slabs, St Lawrence/Anna Plunkett effigy tomb, holy spout, college remains and architectural fragments. Batch 163 consolidates these under the official RMP component structure and adds a key distinction between two and possibly three slab descriptions; no duplicate catalogue entries should be created from this report.
- Batch 150 described St Mary's (CofI) as a separate Howth site. Batch 163 adds the 2017 excavation evidence for at least 31 articulated individuals and a probable early medieval burial phase. This is a new evidence layer, not a new site.
- Do not merge FHG 28 and FHG 29. They have different addresses, coordinates, RMP references, ownership/access regimes, church histories and archaeological evidence.
- No permanent archive IDs, new production catalogue records or map pins assigned.
- All current conditions, exact object positions, access permissions and excavation assemblage custody require confirmation before publication.
