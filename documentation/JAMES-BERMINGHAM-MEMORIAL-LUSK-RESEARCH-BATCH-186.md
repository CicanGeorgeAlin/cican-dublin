# CICAN Dublin Archive — James Bermingham Memorial, Lusk Research Batch 186

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Parent-linked micro-object research for the James Bermingham funerary monument in the St MacCullin's historic church / belfry complex, Lusk, County Dublin.  
**Status:** Research-only. Existing parent record DUB-BLD-0177 remains unchanged. No permanent child ID, source-registry entry, visual record or production map pin created because the complete canonical object and component catalogues cannot be validated.

## 1. Identity and parent relationship

- **Preferred archive label:** James Bermingham Memorial.
- **Name variants in sources:** James Birmingham; James Bermingham; “James Bermyngham” in the 1988 inscription transcription.
- **Associated spouse:** Ellinor FitzWilliams / Eleanora Fitz William (source spellings differ).
- **Place association in inscription:** Ballough / Ballogh.
- **Parent:** DUB-BLD-0177 — Saint MacCullin's Church, Lusk.
- **Setting:** St MacCullin's church and belfry complex. Heritage Ireland currently lists the James Birmingham commemoration among the monuments within the adjacent square belfry.
- **Object type:** Limestone funerary slab with a relief effigy of an armoured knight and heraldic arms.
- **Primary archaeological reference:** Bradley & King, *County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey*, Part VIII(iv), 1988, Lusk section, p. 267; Fig. 49.
- **Historic monument identifier:** The RSAI lantern-slide record associates the monument with St Macullin's Church, DU008-010004-. This is a monument/site reference in that collection record, not a separate CICAN object ID.

Do not create a new church parent or outdoor location pin for this interior funerary object.

## 2. Date and identity — 1637 versus 1527

The strongest institutional and survey evidence supports **1637**:
- Bradley & King (1988), p. 267, heads the entry “James Birmingham. 1637” and transcribes 1637 in the inscription.
- Heritage Ireland identifies James Birmingham as commemorated with the date 1637.
- Trinity College Dublin's Edwin Rae Collection image record describes the effigy as dated 1637.
- RSAI's lantern-slide record likewise describes the effigy as dated 1637.

However, a secondary family-history page reports that the monument has been described as “1527 (or 1637)” and says the identity of the James Bermingham is uncertain. Patrick Comerford's blog also refers to James Bermingham (1527), citing earlier accounts. These references may preserve an older reading or date attribution; they do not outweigh the direct 1637 reading in the 1988 survey and the institutional image catalogues, but they must not be silently erased.

**Recommended archive date:** “1637, as read in the 1988 archaeological survey and repeated by Heritage Ireland, TCD and RSAI; an alternative 1527 attribution appears in some secondary accounts and remains unresolved.”

Do not state that the date conflict is definitively settled without direct epigraphic review of the monument.

## 3. Physical description from the 1988 survey

Bradley & King describe:
- A limestone slab.
- A relief effigy of an armoured knight.
- Coats of arms of the Birmingham and FitzWilliam families beside the figure's head.
- Marginal inscription naming James Bermingham of Ballough, esquire, and his wife Ellinor FitzWilliams.
- The inscription closes with “VAE [M]IHI PECCATORI” (Latin, “Woe to me, a sinner”).
- Published dimensions: **208 cm long × 104 cm wide × 18 cm high/thick**.

These are the survey's published measurements, not new CICAN measurements.

The 1988 report's Fig. 49 caption calls it “Monument to James Bermingham, 1637”. The image description shows a full-length standing/relief effigy set into a rectangular slab, with hands held together at the chest and a shield-like heraldic panel near the upper part of the slab.

## 4. Inscription and heraldry

The 1988 survey transcription reads:

“FOR JAMES BERMYNGHAM OF B[ALLOUGH] ESQVIRE/ AND HIS WIFE ELLINOR FITZWILLIAMS 1637/ VAE [M]IHI PECCATORI”

Preserve the brackets around uncertain or supplied letters. Do not silently expand the inscription, modernise the spelling, or assume that all later family-tree identifications refer to this same individual.

The heraldic arms are identified by the survey as Birmingham and FitzWilliam. The exact heraldic blazon and the identity of the armorial bearers should be reviewed by a specialist before any more detailed genealogy or family attribution is published.

## 5. Historical visual record and relocation history

The monument has an unusually useful visual record:
- **RSAI Lantern Slide, Box 10, item BOX10_060:** Sophie Hogan's lantern slide depicts the effigy at Lusk. The catalogue explicitly says the slide shows it outside, before it was relocated to the tower. It notes a child standing beside the sculpture. The digital catalogue gives a 2400 dpi scan and identifies the RSAI as rights holder.
- **TCD Edwin Rae Collection:** an image record dated 10 November 2007 is titled “Church of Ireland Church, Lusk, County Dublin - Effigy of James Bermingham and Tomb of Sir Christopher Barnewall and Wife”. Its description identifies the effigy as dated 1637.
- **National Library of Ireland catalogue:** a drawing titled “Graveyard of Lusk Church, March 1849” is described as a close-up of the James Bermingham effigy. The catalogue credits William Frazer as copyist after an original drawing by George V. Du Noyer. It is a pen-and-ink drawing, 17.7 × 24.6 cm; reproduction rights are owned by the National Library of Ireland.
- **1988 OPW-commissioned survey:** Fig. 49 reproduces a photograph and provides the main archaeological description.

The RSAI record's statement that the effigy was shown outside before relocation is a significant provenance clue. It does not establish the exact date, method or circumstances of relocation. The current interior location is supported by Heritage Ireland's present visitor description, but no detailed movement record has been identified in this batch.

Do not copy any historical image into the CICAN visual archive without a documented rights and reuse assessment. Catalogue access and image visibility do not equal permission to reproduce.

## 6. Historical descriptions and material discrepancy

The 1837 *Topographical Dictionary of Ireland* describes a black-marble monument to James Bermingham of Ballough, with a recumbent effigy in chain armour, in the north aisle of the church. The 1988 survey describes the present monument as a limestone slab with a relief effigy of an armoured knight. Other secondary descriptions mention the knight's feet resting on a dog and a sword across the left thigh.

These descriptions are not fully aligned in material, posture and detail. They may reflect differences in observation, historical movement, or descriptions of the same monument from different viewpoints; this batch cannot resolve the discrepancy. The 1988 survey's description and its Fig. 49 should be treated as the principal modern archaeological baseline, while the 1837 account and other secondary descriptions remain attributed historical evidence.

Do not describe the monument as black marble or as a recumbent effigy in the production record unless direct examination or a stronger conservation source confirms those details.

Source: https://www.libraryireland.com/topog/L/Lusk-Balrothery-Dublin.php

## 7. Setting, protection and current access

Heritage Ireland describes the adjacent square belfry as containing several historic monuments, including James Birmingham (d. 1637). It states that the Lusk Round Tower and Belfry is a National Monument protected under the National Monuments Acts.

The Heritage Ireland page currently lists 2026 tour dates and states:
- Interior access is by guided tour only.
- Access is restricted to the ground floor of the belfry.
- Tours are at 11am, 1pm and 3pm.
- Capacity is limited and admission is first-come, first-served.

These are the currently published 2026 arrangements, not a guarantee of future opening dates or permission to photograph. Check the official page before any visit. Do not assume that access to the ground floor permits entry to upper floors or unrestricted close inspection of the tomb.

Source: https://heritageireland.ie/places-to-visit/lusk-round-tower-and-belfry/

## 8. Founder photography brief — guided access only

If the monument is visible during a permitted tour and photography is allowed:
1. Capture a complete frontal image showing the full slab and surrounding architectural setting.
2. Capture the armoured figure in full, square-on, with the camera plane parallel to the monument.
3. Photograph the Birmingham and FitzWilliam heraldic panels separately.
4. Capture the full marginal inscription in overlapping sections, including the corners and date.
5. Take close-ups of armour, hands, head, feet and any animal support only where visible and without assuming the secondary descriptions are correct.
6. Record surface condition neutrally, including cracks, losses, deposits or repairs, without touching the stone.
7. Record image orientation, date, tour/access conditions and permission details.

Do not touch, wet, chalk, rub, trace, clean or place a scale on the monument. Do not use flash or lighting equipment if prohibited by the guide.

## 12. Conclusion and next steps

The James Bermingham memorial is a distinct, well-documented funerary object within the existing Lusk church parent record. The strongest evidence supports a 1637 date, a limestone slab, an armoured knight in relief, Birmingham and FitzWilliam heraldry, and an inscription naming James Bermyngham of Ballough and Ellinor FitzWilliams. An alternative 1527 date appears in secondary sources, and nineteenth-century descriptions differ from the 1988 archaeological description in material and posture; preserve these as attributed discrepancies rather than harmonising them without evidence.

Next:
1. Preserve the 1637 / 1527 and limestone / black-marble discrepancies in any future record.
2. Use the RSAI and NLI historical images as identification and provenance references, subject to rights restrictions.
3. Confirm current visibility and photography permission during an official guided visit.
4. Recover the canonical catalogue and child-object index before assigning a permanent ID.
5. Continue with the seventeenth-century chimney-breast plaque / Annunciation panel as the next distinct Lusk micro-object candidate.
