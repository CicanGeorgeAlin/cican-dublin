# CICAN Dublin Archive — Kenure Medieval Graveyard & St Doulagh's Reconciliation (Batch 171)

**Status:** Research report saved and fetched for verification. FHG 44 is newly detailed here; FHG 45 is a reconciliation/update against earlier CICAN research (including Batch 149), not a fresh duplicate catalogue entry. No permanent archive IDs, production records or map pins created.  
**Scope:** FHG 44 Kenure (medieval church/graveyard) and FHG 45 St Doulagh's, Kinsealy.  
**Critical separation:** FHG 43 Kenure (1866 Church of Ireland churchyard) lies east of the R128; FHG 44 Kenure (medieval church/graveyard) lies west of the R128. They are separate sites, ownerships and heritage records.

## 1. Kenure medieval church and graveyard — FHG 44

### Identity, location and designation
- FHG reference 44; survey dated 26 July 2008.
- Address: Skerries Road, Rush, County Dublin, Ireland; west side of the R128.
- FHG source coordinate pair: X 326520, Y 255940. The 2008 survey does not specify the coordinate reference system. Preserve as raw source coordinates and do not label as ITM until confirmed.
- RMP DU008-00402 (graveyard); medieval church DU008-00401.
- RPS 256; NIAH 11324029 (graveyard).
- Ownership: Fingal County Council, according to FHG.
- The 2022 St Catherine's Church / Kenure Demesne archaeological monitoring report and the 2008 FHG entry refer to this medieval church and cemetery, not the separate FHG 43 CofI church.
- The FHG source calls it Kenure, while other sources may call it St Catherine's Church or Kenure Church. Preserve all source names as aliases with their specific context; do not infer that every nearby St Catherine's dedication refers to this church.

### Setting and enclosure
- Oval graveyard on the west side of the R128, with the ground rising slightly toward the church.
- East boundary has squared rubble limestone wall with limestone coping. Other boundaries were rebuilt using concrete breeze blocks; rendered pillars, mild-steel gates and a stile are recorded at the northeast corner.
- A curving bank and stream to the south and east may represent an earlier ecclesiastical enclosure. This is a qualified interpretation, not a confirmed enclosure line.
- FHG records no upstanding early-medieval features on the site.
- The cemetery contains 18th–21st-century markers, undecorated stones and reused architectural fragments. Older markers cluster on higher ground around the church; later markers are mainly northeast.
- The graveyard is no longer in use; FHG 2008 described it as regularly maintained and in good condition. Reassess current condition.

### Medieval church architecture
- Late-medieval church remains (DU008-00401), built of roughly coursed rubble limestone.
- Single bellcote on the west gable; pointed-arched opening in south wall; pointed-arched east window with dressed-stone surround.
- The church ruins contain burials and two 17th-century tombs associated by the FHG survey with the Baron of Strabane and the Palmer family.
- The FHG survey records ivy on the bellcote and recommends specialist-led vegetation management. No removal by archive fieldworkers.

### High-priority tombs and memorials
1. **Early-17th-century mensa tomb fragments** — The RMP record, as quoted by FHG, describes two fragments of an early-17th-century mensa tomb bearing a Crucifixion scene. The fragments are not necessarily a complete or in-situ tomb. Locate and photograph each surviving fragment separately; record dimensions, iconography, stone type, current position and whether they are incorporated into the church fabric. Do not reconstruct a complete monument without evidence.
2. **Baron of Strabane tomb** — FHG describes a 17th-century tomb inside the church ruins, associated with the Baron of Strabane and the 15th-century Earl of Strabane tradition in the Irish Folklore Commission account. The survey does not provide a full inscription or a secure named individual. Record as an attributed tomb pending inscription and archival verification; do not assign the Earl's identity without evidence.
3. **Palmer family tomb** — Second 17th-century tomb inside the church ruins, associated by FHG with the Palmer family. Exact inscription, family member and tomb construction details need field photography and archival transcription.
4. **John Connors / “Jack the Bachelor” grave** — FHG locates the grave on the western side of the church and identifies John Connors of Rush as an 18th-century smuggler known as “Jack the Bachelor”. The Irish Folklore Commission source calls him Jack O'Connor and reports an old English inscription on his tombstone. This surname discrepancy must be preserved; do not normalize Connors/O'Connor until the stone inscription and archival records are compared.
5. **Polished marble obelisk** — Located southwest of the church; pictured in FHG Plate 44.2. Record as a separate memorial with full base, shaft, apex and inscription photographs. Do not assume its commemorated person from location alone.
6. **Cholera memorial plaque** — On the north wall of the church. FHG transcription reads: “In memory of the many victims of cholera who dies in this parish during the great famine”. The grammar may reflect a survey transcription error; photograph the plaque and transcribe letter-for-letter before normalizing. The wording links cholera victims and the Great Famine, but the precise epidemic dates and number of victims are not established by this plaque alone.
7. **18th–21st-century headstones and Celtic Revival crosses** — Older stones are concentrated around the church on higher ground; later stones and kerbed plots are northeast. Survey individually and preserve their exact position/orientation.
8. **Split headstone south of church** — FHG records one tall headstone split into two sections, plus the top of another broken headstone set into the ground. These are separate damage/fragment records until matched to their original memorials.
9. **Undecorated stones and reused architectural fragments** — FHG records both categories. Their date and original function must remain unknown unless form, tool marks, inscription or archaeological context supports interpretation.

### Folklore and historical claims — evidence grading
- Irish Folklore Commission Schools' Collection describes the graveyard as reputedly about 1,000 years old. This is folklore, not a measured foundation date.
- The same collection records a supposed tunnel from the church ruins across the carriage drive to an old building behind the mansion. Treat as an unverified folklore lead; no tunnel is confirmed by the FHG survey.
- The 15th-century Earl of Strabane association and the tomb attribution need specialist historical verification. Do not state that the visible tomb definitively contains the Earl.
- “Jack the Bachelor” is identified in FHG as John Connors; the Folklore Commission records Jack O'Connor. Preserve both forms with provenance.
- FHG says the church was medieval; do not use “1,000 years old” as a factual construction date.

### Condition and conservation
- FHG 2008: good, well kept, regularly maintained; no longer in use.
- Subsidence on sloping ground south of church, broken headstones, one tall headstone split in two, a broken headstone top inserted into ground, ivy on church and bellcote, gates requiring maintenance.
- FHG recommends stability assessment, accurate recording and retention of fragments, specialist advice before ivy removal, and gate maintenance.
- These are 2008 observations, not a current condition report. The 2022 monitoring report relates to conservation/archaeological works at the medieval St Catherine's Church site; compare its actual scope and findings before making current condition statements.
- Never move, clean, reset, repair or excavate the tombs, fragments or memorials without permissions and specialist supervision.

### Fieldwork priorities
- Locate and photograph both mensa fragments; determine whether the Crucifixion scene is on one or both fragments.
- Document both 17th-century tombs inside the church and establish whether inscriptions survive.
- Record Jack the Bachelor grave on the west side, photograph the inscription at high resolution and reconcile Connors/O'Connor.
- Photograph the north-wall cholera plaque exactly, preserving the apparent “dies” wording if that is what is physically inscribed.
- Photograph the marble obelisk from all sides and capture its inscription.
- Survey split and embedded headstone fragments, all Celtic Revival crosses, kerbed plots and reused stones.
- Record church bellcote, pointed south opening, east window, masonry, enclosure bank/stream, wall types, gate and stile.
- Obtain permission before accessing church interior or private/controlled portions of the site.

## 2. St Doulagh's, Kinsealy — FHG 45 (reconciliation update)

### Identity and spatial controls
- FHG reference 45; survey dated 19 August 2008.
- Address: Saint Doolaghs, Kinsealy, County Dublin, Ireland; west side of the R107.
- FHG coordinate pair: X 321131, Y 242090; coordinate reference system not explicitly stated in Volume 2. Preserve raw values and do not assume ITM.
- Buried in Fingal locator: 53.41515492, -6.18129841.
- Graveyard RMP DU015-009006; medieval church DU015-009001; medieval cross DU015-009002; St Catherine's Well DU015-009003; St Doulagh's Well / Baptistry DU015-009004; former ecclesiastical enclosure DU015-009005; medieval architectural fragments DU015-009007; field system DU015-009008; enclosure DU015-009009.
- RPS 459; NIAH 11350016 (church) and 11350017 (holy well).
- FHG ownership: Fingal County Council.
- The 2024 Conservation Management Plan identifies a multi-part ecclesiastical complex, not just a graveyard. Keep the church, graveyard, cross, two wells, enclosure, architectural fragments, field system and separate enclosure as distinct spatial/heritage objects.
- This is the same FHG 45 site researched in earlier CICAN Batch 149. This report is an update/reconciliation, not a second site or duplicate catalogue entry.

### Important coordinate discrepancy
- The 2024 Conservation Management Plan cover gives ITM 721051, 742104.
- A footnote on page 1 gives ITM 721051, 741104.
- The report's general coordinate-system note states that grid coordinates use ITM unless otherwise stated.
- This 1,000m northing discrepancy is significant and remains unresolved in the report itself. Preserve both values as conflicting source statements; do not silently select one for a graveyard or church pin. The FHG 2008 X/Y pair is a separate legacy coordinate set and must not be conflated with either CMP value.
- The CMP also gives individual ITM coordinates in its monument descriptions and asset appendices. These must be checked against the report's mapping/figure set before assigning precise pins.

### Ecclesiastical landscape and building chronology
- St Doulagh is associated with the 7th century; the earliest documentary reference is early 9th-century *Martyrology of Oengus*, cited in the CMP.
- The earliest surviving eastern portion of the church dates to the mid-12th century and includes a vaulted stone roof and croft.
- The 15th-century central tower followed demolition of the western gable.
- The 19th-century dressed-limestone church was built adjacent to the medieval structures in 1864.
- The church is a multi-phased two-/three-storey structure and remains in use as a place of worship. The CMP describes it as the oldest Irish stone-roofed church still in use.
- The hermit's cell is reputed to be St Doulagh's burial place; this is a tradition, not verified identification of a grave.
- The graveyard is sub-rectangular, with a curving masonry wall east and low earthen bank north. Its southeast curve may preserve part of the early ecclesiastical enclosure, which has been investigated by geophysical survey and excavation.
- The CMP confirms that the medieval granite cross now marks the roadside entrance and was formerly in the graveyard.

### Graveyard memorials and architectural details
- Headstones date from 18th to 20th century; the 2008 survey records free-standing crosses, flat slabs, cast-iron crosses, large railed plots, an unusual undecorated pillar stone east of the church, and an uninscribed sandstone headstone northeast.
- An early-18th-century IHS stone reads in the FHG transcription: “This burial place belongeth to James ? and his posterity who departed this life 1726 aged 65 years”. Name after James is uncertain; do not guess.
- A number of larger plots with low plinth walls and cast-iron railings are northwest and west of the church.
- Broken and fallen memorials, subsiding headstones in the northwest, and vegetation encroaching on a cross are visible in the CMP's 2024 plate list. Treat these as documented 2024 conditions, not necessarily present-day conditions.
- A 2024 plate documents a broken/fallen memorial and another documents subsiding headstones in the northwest sector. Compare with the 2008 survey and prior CICAN Batch 149 notes before identifying individual stones.
- The 2008 FHG survey notes a graveyard no longer in use, with 18th–20th-century headstones and medieval architectural fragments.

### The medieval cross
- Simple undecorated granite Latin cross (RMP DU015-009002), now by the roadside entrance, formerly inside the graveyard.
- FHG Plate 45.2 provides a reference photograph. The CMP also lists historic photographs/drawings of the cross, including a Du Noyer image.
- Record the present location as current position and the former graveyard location as historical position; do not create two separate cross objects.
- Do not conflate the cross with other roadside crosses in Kinsealy or St Doulagh's Field.

### Two separate holy wells
1. **St Doulagh's Well / Baptistry (DU015-009004)** — Circular stone-lined below-ground well enclosed by an octagonal building with cone-shaped roof; historically painted frescoes in 1609. The CMP describes the baptistry, approach, interior plaster and condition. Keep the baptistry building and the well as linked but distinct components if schema supports.
2. **St Catherine's Well (DU015-009003)** — Underground well adjoining the north wall of the Baptistry, enclosed by a rectangular vaulted building. It is not the same as St Doulagh's Well.
- Both lie c.25–30m north of the church and graveyard in St Doulagh's Field, according to the CMP.
- The CMP documents steps down to the vault and St Catherine's Well and includes a photograph of the interior showing refuse. Do not infer that this is the current state without checking date/context of the photograph.
- The traditional curative associations for sore eyes and pilgrimage patterns are intangible heritage claims; preserve as folklore rather than medical fact.
- St Patrick's Bed is a separate folklore/landscape feature in the field; do not merge with either well.

### Enclosure and surrounding archaeology
- Former ecclesiastical enclosure DU015-009005, identified through geophysical survey and excavation.
- Field system DU015-009008 south of the graveyard, comprising a sub-rectangular network of ditches.
- Subcircular enclosure DU015-009009, approximately 60m diameter, west of the church and graveyard.
- Medieval architectural fragments DU015-009007 form a separate archaeological asset group.
- Do not represent the full enclosure, field system or nearby enclosure as the graveyard boundary. They are larger landscape features.
- 2009 geophysical survey and subsequent excavation investigated the enclosure network. Excavation report 2015:0024753 describes a broad curving ditch interpreted as part of an early-medieval ecclesiastical enclosure.

### 2024 Conservation Management Plan and condition
- CMP title: *St Doulagh's Conservation Management Plan: Saintdoolaghs, County Dublin (CMF24-2-DF001)*, dated 28 November 2024; issue date stated as 8 November 2024.
- The plan covers archaeological, architectural, ecological, landscape, access, ownership and management aspects.
- The CMP plate list documents vegetation encroaching on a graveyard cross, 19th-century railings, broken/fallen memorial, and subsiding headstones in the northwest.
- The report also includes a LiDAR survey, hand-held LiDAR/laser survey, UAV-based LiDAR and thermal imaging, ecology and bat surveys, and detailed drawings.
- It is the key contemporary baseline for future archive fieldwork. Do not simply repeat 2008 conservation observations without comparing the 2024 survey and plate evidence.
- Conservation works and maintenance must be undertaken under the plan's policies, permissions and specialist advice. CICAN archive fieldwork should be non-invasive.

### Reconciliation against previous CICAN research
- Batch 149 already covered St Doulagh's and Grallagh. Do not create another church, graveyard, medieval cross, St Doulagh's Well or St Catherine's Well from this batch.
- Batch 149 flagged the CMP coordinate discrepancy: cover ITM 721051,742104 vs footnote 721051,741104. This report retains the same unresolved conflict and adds the CMP's detailed asset separation.
- Any earlier note referring to a single St Doulagh's Well must be refined to distinguish St Doulagh's Baptistry/Well from St Catherine's Well.
- The church, graveyard, cross, two wells, enclosure, medieval fragments, field system and western enclosure are distinct mapped entities, with relationships only where supported by the official RMP and CMP.
- The FHG 45 graveyard is not Kinsealy FHG 36 (the ruined St Nicholas churchyard containing Austin Cooper's mausoleum). These are separate locations in Kinsealy.
