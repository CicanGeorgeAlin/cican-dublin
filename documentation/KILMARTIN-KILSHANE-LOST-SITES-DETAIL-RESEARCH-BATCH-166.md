# CICAN Dublin Archive — Kilmartin & Kilshane Lost Ecclesiastical Sites (Batch 166)

**Status:** Desk research only. No permanent archive IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.  
**Scope:** Fingal Historic Graveyards Project FHG 34 Kilmartin and FHG 35 Kilshane.  
**Core finding:** Both former burial sites have lost their above-ground church/graveyard fabric, but their archaeological histories differ. Kilmartin retains a possible enclosure trace in field boundaries/cropmarks. Kilshane was largely removed by quarrying; recent archaeological assessment distinguishes the lost church and burial ground from a possible surviving natural spring/holy well beyond the quarry void.

## 1. Kilmartin — FHG 34

### Site identity and designations
- FHG survey date: 19 August 2008.
- Townland: Kilmartin; parish: Mulhuddart.
- Historic address: Kilmartin Lane, Kilmartin, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG survey coordinates: X/Y not supplied (N/A). Do not invent a point from the postal address.
- RMP: DU013-002 (ecclesiastical remains); the 2008 FHG entry identifies DU013-00202 as the enclosure component.
- RPS 668; no NIAH reference.
- Ownership recorded as private in the 2008 survey. Obtain current landowner permission before field investigation.
- Historic OS mapping: 1837 OS map depicts a church within an enclosure. FHG 2008 plate 34.1 shows the present agricultural landscape; plate 34.2 shows a curved field boundary/track.

### What survives — and what does not
- The church and graveyard were removed and the site levelled between 1971 and 1976, according to Healy (1974) and Stout (1993), as cited by the FHG survey.
- The former site is now covered by tillage fields. There are no surviving grave markers or standing church walls documented by the 2008 survey.
- The only evidence identified by FHG is the curved field boundary and Kilmartin Lane, which may represent the western section of an ecclesiastical enclosure.
- The survey also notes that the enclosure remains visible as a cropmark and that a curve in the road may represent a possible outer enclosure. These are landscape/remote-sensing traces, not standing masonry.
- The survey does not provide a coordinate for the former graveyard. The site must be georeferenced from the historic OS 1837 plan, RMP geometry, aerial/cropmark evidence and current cadastral/field boundaries before any map pin is published.

### Research and fieldwork priorities
1. Obtain the National Monuments Service SMR/RMP record for DU013-002 and its component geometry, including the enclosure DU013-00202.
2. Georeference the 1837 OS depiction of church and enclosure against modern aerial imagery and the curved lane/field boundary.
3. Review historical aerial photography and cropmark datasets to establish whether the enclosure cropmark is still detectable and whether the possible outer enclosure can be distinguished from modern field boundaries.
4. Review the 1971–1976 levelling history and any associated earthmoving/excavation records. Search Excavations.ie and local planning/landowner archives for any observations of burials or architectural material during removal.
5. If landowner permission is granted, photograph the curved lane and field boundaries from accessible positions and record current crop conditions, but do not enter cultivated land without permission.
6. Do not search for, recover or relocate human remains or stones. Any discovery of archaeological material must be reported to the National Museum/National Monuments Service through the appropriate channels.

### Archive treatment
- Create a **lost-site research candidate**, not an extant graveyard location.
- Preserve “Kilmartin former church and graveyard” as the historic site identity; record the surviving field-boundary/cropmark evidence as separate landscape traces with a tentative relationship.
- No individual grave objects can be recorded from the available evidence because the graveyard and church were removed and no specific surviving memorials are identified.

## 2. Kilshane — FHG 35

### Site identity and designations
- Official FHG name: Kilshane.
- Townland: Kilshane; parish: Finglas; County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG survey X/Y coordinates: N/A. No coordinate is supplied in the graveyard survey.
- RMP components:
  - DU014-01201 — church (possible site).
  - DU014-01202 — graveyard / burial ground.
  - DU014-01203 — ritual site, possible holy well.
- FHG lists RPS 663, “Kilshane Church (in ruins) & Holy Well”, off the N2/Ashbourne Road. However later planning/archaeological documentation says the church and graveyard were removed by quarrying and the site is within a quarry void.
- No NIAH reference recorded.
- The 1836 OS map labels an area “Old Burying Ground” and “Church Well”; the 1837/1842 first-edition mapping also contains the field-name “Church Field”. These are cartographic records, not proof of a standing church building.
- The 2022 EIAR gives the townland etymology Cill Sheáin (“John's Church”) and a 1326 documentary spelling “Kilshaane”; these place-name facts do not independently prove the exact church location.

### Lost church and burial ground
- FHG Volume 2 states that the church, graveyard and holy-well site have been removed by quarrying; no surface indication of the burial ground remained at the time of the survey.
- The RMP description cited in the 2016 EPA planning appendix says the church and graveyard are within a quarry void and not visible at ground level.
- The 2012 Fingal planning order states that the church no longer exists and the area was extracted to geological levels.
- The 2022 EIAR for the quarry site similarly states that no surface remains of the ecclesiastical complex are present within Hunstown Quarry.
- Healy (1975, p.27), as cited by FHG, reports that a pump and drinking trough were once present at the site. The pump/trough are historical leads only; their precise form, date and current survival are unknown.

### The Church Well — critical source distinction
- FHG Volume 2 classifies DU014-01203 as a possible holy well and says the church, graveyard and well site were removed by quarrying.
- A later 2022 EIAR identifies the former church and burial ground in the quarry void but separately refers to the “Church Well” shown on the 1836 map.
- The specialist secondary source *Ireland's Holy Wells County-by-County* (citing Gary Branigan, 2012, p.36) says Church Well survives as an unenclosed natural spring colonised by bullrushes in a waterlogged area north of a disused quarry, near a waste-recovery facility. It also says the burial ground's correct location was only discovered in 1988 during topsoil removal for pipe-laying.
- That well source further cautions that the “church” in the well name may derive from an Ordnance Survey mapping error, and that there may never have been a church at the well's exact location.
- These sources can be reconciled only by treating the **former burial ground**, **possible church site**, and **Church Well spring** as three separate spatial entities. The spring may survive outside the quarry void even though the FHG survey describes the ecclesiastical complex as removed. The well's exact location, access, current hydrology and relationship to the former burial ground require field/GIS verification.
- Do not label the natural spring as a confirmed saint-dedicated holy well or assume the historic pump/trough survives.

### Kilshane research and fieldwork priorities
1. Obtain the full 2022 EIAR cultural heritage chapter and site layout, including the exact plotted locations of the 1836 “Old Burying Ground” and “Church Well”.
2. Obtain the 2016 EPA licence application appendices and the 2012 Fingal planning order with maps, quarry phasing and mitigation commitments.
3. Compare the 1836 OS map, 1906 OS map, 1760 Rocque map and 2022 EIAR mapping; record the map edition and map-sheet reference for every historical footprint.
4. Request the 1988 pipe-laying/topsoil-removal record cited by Branigan to clarify how the burial ground was located and whether any archaeological monitoring or human-remains discoveries were documented.
5. Locate the possible natural spring north of the disused quarry using the EIAR and specialist well source; do not enter quarry land or waterlogged ground without permission and a safety plan.
6. If the spring can be accessed lawfully, photograph the spring head, bullrushes, surrounding waterlogged ground, any pump/trough remains and the quarry edge from safe positions. Do not clear vegetation or alter the watercourse.
7. No grave-marker photography or individual burial-object records are currently possible from the sources reviewed.

### Archive treatment
- Treat DU014-01201, DU014-01202 and DU014-01203 as separate archaeological component candidates.
- Mark the church and burial ground as removed/destroyed by quarrying according to the FHG and planning evidence.
- Treat the Church Well as a possible surviving natural spring requiring exact geolocation and field verification.
- Do not create a single “Kilshane graveyard” pin at a guessed location. If later verified, the lost burial ground and spring should have distinct geometry and status.
