# CICAN Dublin Archive — Kinsealy & St MacCullin's CofI Lusk (Batch 167)

**Status:** Research batch completed; no production catalogue records or permanent IDs created.  
**Scope:** Fingal Historic Graveyards Project FHG 36 Kinsealy (called “Kinsaley” in the 2008 survey) and FHG 37 St MacCullin's Church of Ireland, Lusk.  
**Method:** Official FHG survey, NIAH, Fingal burial-ground information, archaeological excavation reports and local heritage documentation cross-checked. Source-derived observations are distinguished from field verification.

## 1. Kinsealy / Kinsaley — FHG 36

### Identity, location and designations
- FHG number: 36; survey date 3 August 2008.
- The FHG survey spells the site name “Kinsaley”; the current Fingal burial database uses “Kinsealy”. Retain both as source-name variants, with Kinsealy as the current public-facing label.
- Townland/parish: Kinsaley.
- Address: Chapel Road, Kinsealy, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG ITM coordinate: E 321796, N 243129. The NIAH record for the graveyard gives E 321784, N 243141. These are close but not identical; preserve both source coordinates and do not silently merge them.
- Buried in Fingal published position: 53.42448653, -6.16873105. This is a cemetery locator, not a surveyed centroid of every individual monument.
- RMP: DU015-00202 (graveyard); church component DU015-00201.
- FHG RPS reference 455; NIAH entry 11350033 (graveyard).
- Ownership was “not determined” in the 2008 FHG survey. Buried in Fingal says Fingal County Council maintains the closed graveyard and its records. Treat operational maintenance as confirmed by the current burial database, but legal title should not be inferred from maintenance alone.

### Church and landscape
- The church is dedicated to St Nicholas and probably fell into ruin in the mid-17th century.
- The late-medieval church remains include the nave walls, a double bellcote on the west gable and a pointed chancel arch to the east; the church is built of roughly coursed limestone. Burial is present within the church.
- The church's former chancel area is occupied by an early-19th-century mausoleum associated with antiquarian Austin Cooper.
- The FHG survey notes a pronounced curve in the southeast/eastern graveyard boundary wall which may indicate an earlier ecclesiastical enclosure. This remains an interpretation, not a confirmed enclosure boundary.
- The wider Kinsaley landscape contains several separately recorded cropmark enclosures (including DU015-109, DU015-110, DU015-111, DU015-114 and DU015-115). Do not automatically treat these as parts of the graveyard enclosure; each has its own location and archaeological identity.
- A 2014 archaeological test excavation in Kinsaley townland recorded a possible pit with medieval pottery at ITM E 721550, N 743220, but this is a separate development-site feature and must not be attributed to the graveyard itself.

### High-value individual memorials and features
1. **Austin Cooper mausoleum (built 1813)** — A distinctive early-19th-century mausoleum at the former chancel end. Its shield-shaped inscription states that Austin Cooper, antiquarian and Fellow of the Society of Antiquaries / Royal Irish Academy, built the vault in 1813 as a burial place for himself and successive heirs; he died 30 August 1830, aged 73. A second rectangular plaque contains a resurrection text. A further plaque commemorates members of the Cooper family into 1939. Record the mausoleum as a distinct architectural object within the graveyard, and each inscription panel as a child detail only if the archive schema supports component relationships.
2. **Elias Reynolds family headstone (1754)** — Mid-18th-century headstone with winged angel and skull-and-crossbones imagery. The inscription names Elias Reynolds and his posterity, five children who died young, and Margaret Reynolds, who died in 1734 aged three. The FHG wording includes both an October 1754 date and the earlier death date of Margaret; do not mistake 1734 for the stone's erection date.
3. **Other marker groups** — FHG records 18th-, 19th- and early/mid-20th-century headstones and free-standing crosses, including a notable number of late-19th/early-20th-century cast-iron crosses and railed plots.
4. **Boundary wall and entrance** — Sub-rectangular graveyard enclosed by squared rubble limestone wall, with a pronounced curved section on the eastern side and gates/stile at the southeast corner. Photograph the curvature and gate/stile as separate built features only if independently useful and safely accessible.

### Condition and conservation leads
- FHG 2008 rated the site fair and said it was regularly maintained, but the majority of grave markers were damaged.
- The northwest corner was overgrown, possibly due to dumping of cleared vegetation.
- FHG recommended continued maintenance and consideration of conservation of the Cooper mausoleum.
- The 2023 Kinsaley Local Area Plan reiterates the graveyard/church RMP identities and describes the graveyard as rectangular and walled, enclosing the ruined church.
- Buried in Fingal says the graveyard is closed. Do not imply general public entry or unrestricted access; verify current access arrangements before a field visit.

### Conflicting earliest memorial date
- Buried in Fingal states the earliest recorded headstone dates from 1747.
- FHG 2008 identifies the Reynolds stone dated 1754 as one of the oldest headstones.
- These statements may refer to different stones or to differing transcription/recording coverage. Do not claim either is definitively the earliest surviving memorial until the headstone surveys and burial records are reconciled. Flag 1747 as a separate earlier-record lead.

### Fieldwork / archive actions
- Photograph the church's double bellcote, nave walls, chancel arch, Cooper mausoleum and each inscription panel, Reynolds headstone and carved mortality symbols, cast-iron crosses/railings, curved eastern wall and entrance.
- Take orthogonal close-ups of inscriptions with scale and oblique-light details of carved motifs; preserve full-frame originals before any crops or line-art derivatives.
- Check the 1990s *Journal of the Association for the Preservation of the Memorials of the Dead in Ireland* references listed by Buried in Fingal, and Fingal Local Studies copies of Egan's *Memorials of the Dead*.
- Reconcile the FHG ITM coordinate, NIAH coordinate and Buried in Fingal lat/long in GIS before final object geometries.

## 2. St MacCullin's (Church of Ireland) — Lusk — FHG 37

### Identity and designations
- FHG number: 37; survey date 24 July 2008.
- Denomination: Church of Ireland.
- Address: Church Road, Lusk, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG ITM coordinate: E 321569, N 254434.
- NIAH church coordinate: E 321593, N 254442; NIAH registration 11323010.
- RMP: DU008-01005 (graveyard), within the wider DU008-010 ecclesiastical/village complex.
- RPS reference 297; Fingal Development Plan appendix describes the former mid-19th-century church as now a community centre within an enclosed graveyard.
- FHG records Fingal County Council ownership of the graveyard. The church/community centre is managed as a local community space; current opening/access is event-dependent.
- The round tower is a National Monument in State guardianship (FHG notes reference 157 G); do not conflate the round tower's guardianship with ownership of the graveyard.

### The wider early-medieval ecclesiastical landscape
- The FHG survey associates the foundation of a monastic centre at Lusk with St MacCullin in the late 5th century. This is a historical/traditional foundation account, not a date established by excavation of the present graveyard.
- The early ecclesiastical enclosure is legible in the pattern of Main Street, the northern part of Dublin Road, Church Road, Church Street and some field boundaries.
- The FHG survey reports significant early-medieval burials found in archaeological investigations outside the present graveyard. The present cemetery boundary therefore does not define the full archaeological site.
- The 2004 monitoring of service trenches at the church identified at least two inhumations beneath 0.4–0.5m of made ground. The made ground included demolition material from the medieval church and two 15th-century floor-tile fragments. Works were halted/altered to preserve remains in situ.
- A 2005 excavation on Church Road, about 150m west of the round tower and within the wider precinct, investigated a burial ground and enclosure context. Treat those discoveries as separate off-site archaeological records, not graves within the current churchyard.
- A 2008 test excavation identified a substantial ditch with shell, animal bone and 13th–14th-century pottery; its position and character suggest it may represent part of the early-medieval monastic enclosure, but this interpretation remains qualified.
- Historical accounts record attacks and fires affecting Lusk in 825, 854, 1069 and 1089. Preserve these as annalistic/historical claims and do not use them as proof of a specific burn layer at the church.

### Architectural sequence
1. **Early-medieval round tower** — The prominent surviving round tower stands at the west end of the church complex and is a National Monument. It is distinct from the later square tower and later church fabric.
2. **Late-medieval square tower** — A four-stage tower was built against the round tower in the 15th century. It has circular/tapering corner towers and rubble limestone construction.
3. **Medieval parish church** — The earlier church was a large, multi-aisled building. A 1791 drawing by Francis Grose documents it; accounts describe two long aisles divided by seven pointed arches. A storm in January 1839 destroyed the roof and reduced the church to ruins.
4. **Current former Church of Ireland church (1847)** — Designed by Joseph Welland and built in snecked limestone, with four-bay side elevation, pointed-arched openings, crenellated parapets and a pitched slate roof. It closed for worship in 1959 and now functions as a museum/heritage and community venue.
5. **Reused/older architectural details** — The FHG survey records a possible medieval piscina and an inscribed 17th-century chimneypiece within the 1847 church. Both are important candidate objects but require close-up field photography and specialist confirmation of date/function before creating detailed records.

### High-value memorials and objects
1. **Sir Christopher Barnewall and Marion Sharl effigy tomb (1575)** — A large marble tomb inside the tower, bearing effigies of a knight and lady in ornate dress and inscriptions in Latin and English. FHG dates it to 1575. Secondary cemetery transcriptions sometimes give other dates/names; retain the FHG source wording as primary survey evidence and record inscription readings separately.
2. **Sir Robert Echlin box tomb (mid-18th century)** — Located east of the church. Inscription identifies Sir Robert Echlin of Russ, County Dublin, Baronet, born 13 November 1699 and died 13 May 1757; married Elinor Bellingham in 1727. A verse is also inscribed. The tomb has multiple inscribed and uninscribed panels and is a high-priority photo target.
3. **1734 ground-set slab** — An early-18th-century slab embedded in the ground north of the church. Photograph in situ; do not lift or clean.
4. **Other box tombs and family plots** — The graveyard contains several earlier box tombs linked to significant local families, plus a variety of 18th-, 19th- and 20th-century headstones, slabs, crosses, obelisk and railings. Each tomb should be recorded individually only after a complete walkover and duplicate check.
5. **Loose architectural fragments and damaged table tombs** — FHG reports loose fragments north of the church, a table tomb northwest of the church with its top missing, a cracked table tomb west of the tower, and many slabs lying on the ground. Their current positions must be field-verified; no fragment should be assigned to a parent structure without evidence.

### Saint's well and folklore
- The FHG survey records a well behind the old barracks associated with St MacCullin, with a tradition that the saint's footprints remained there and that the well cured sore eyes.
- A 1991 archaeological report identifies St MacCullin's Well south of the round tower, within the possible outer monastic enclosure; it describes the location as a former pool now enclosed with concrete pipes.
- The saint's leap from the tower and footprint story is folklore, not verified biography. Record the well as a separate archaeological/folklore lead with its own coordinate and condition check, not as part of the churchyard.
- The 1991 report and later research indicate the well area has changed; do not assume a visible open well survives today.

### Condition and conservation
- FHG 2008 rated the graveyard good and well maintained, but noted a tree in one grave that may cause damage, broken markers, a table tomb with missing top, cracked table tomb and loose architectural fragments.
- The 2012 Fingal archaeological-resource management report separately noted weedkiller-related denudation, exposed skeletal remains and past vandalism to the Echlin tomb, followed by repair. These observations are historical condition reports and need current reassessment.
- FHG recommends continued maintenance, stability assessment of leaning stones, careful resetting only where justified, and accurate recording/retention of loose fragments.
- Do not remove ivy, clean inscriptions abrasively, straighten stones, move fragments or undertake any intervention without conservation/archaeological advice and permissions.

## 3. Important distinctions and duplicate controls
- FHG 25 is St MacCullin's **Roman Catholic** graveyard, Lusk. FHG 37 is St MacCullin's **Church of Ireland** graveyard, Lusk. They are different sites and must never be merged.
- FHG 36 is labelled “Kinsaley” in the 2008 report but “Kinsealy” in the current burial database. Maintain the alias rather than creating two cemetery records.
- Kinsealy graveyard RMP DU015-00202 and church DU015-00201 are separate component records.
- Lusk graveyard DU008-01005 is one component within a larger ecclesiastical complex. The round tower DU008-01003, medieval church DU008-01004, enclosure DU008-01006 and holy well DU008-01007 remain distinct.
- The Barnewall effigy tomb, Echlin box tomb, Cooper mausoleum and Reynolds headstone are individual objects, not separate graveyards.
- No permanent CICAN archive IDs or production catalogue/map records created in this batch. Complete master catalogue access and duplicate/schema checks remain prerequisites.
