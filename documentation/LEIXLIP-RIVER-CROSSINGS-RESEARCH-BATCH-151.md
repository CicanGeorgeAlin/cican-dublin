# CICAN DUBLIN — Leixlip River Crossings and Ecclesiastical Details Research Batch 151

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Leixlip village, River Liffey / Rye Water confluence, County Kildare; correction and follow-up for the Lucan–Leixlip boundary corridor  
**Status:** Research-only. No catalogue objects, permanent CICAN IDs, source-registry entries, visual records or map pins created.

## Executive findings

Batch 151 identifies a substantial set of independently photographable heritage details in Leixlip: a 1732–34 three-arch bridge with mason's marks, the historic St Mary's church and its medieval-origin tower, early wrought-iron gates and railings, graveyard monuments, a riverside boathouse folly with an iron mooring ring, and the remains of historic mill and water infrastructure. The Leixlip Heritage Trail is useful as a discovery index, but claims from that local interpretive leaflet are kept distinct from official NIAH and archaeological records.

Repository text searches for the exact names and NIAH numbers listed below returned no indexed matches. This is not a full duplicate clearance: the canonical `data/objects.json` and source registry are too large to inspect completely through the available fetch route. Therefore no new catalogue records or IDs are created in this batch.

## 1. Leixlip Bridge — three arches and masons' marks

- **NIAH:** 11804078.
- **Date:** built 1732–1734; NIAH record date range 1730–1735.
- **Coordinates:** NIAH grid reference 300825, 235799. Source-derived locator only, not founder GPS.
- **Description:** Three round arches over the River Liffey, rough-cut limestone walls, limestone ashlar triangular cutwaters, and ashlar voussoirs and keystones.
- **Micro-detail priority:** NIAH specifically notes a unique collection of masons' marks on the limestone dressings and keystones. These are excellent close-up targets, as are the cutwaters, coping, arch springings and individual keystones.
- **Evidence:** National Inventory of Architectural Heritage (NIAH), record 11804078.
- **Field note:** Ivy and moss are described in the NIAH survey. Do not treat the survey description as a current condition report.

## 2. St Mary's Church — medieval fabric within a later building

- **NIAH:** 11804017.
- **Date:** present church c.1800 (NIAH date range 1780–1820); survey says it probably incorporates fabric of a medieval church, c.1250.
- **Coordinates:** NIAH grid reference 300535, 235818. Source-derived building locator, not founder GPS.
- **Features recorded:** rubble-stone church, west tower thought to be medieval in origin, iron clock face, battlemented parapets, stone stringcourse, cast-iron rainwater goods, timber sash windows, stained glass, and an L-shaped first-floor gallery.
- **Churchyard:** NIAH describes cut-stone grave markers c.1800 to present, wrought-iron gates and railings, and a north gateway with four limestone ashlar piers.
- **Separate archaeology:** Excavations.ie report 2005:767 (SMR KD011-004003, licence 05E1133) records a graveyard of about two acres, surrounded by a wall in the 18th century. Test trenches found disarticulated human remains, one adult burial and a stone-capped drain of 18th/early 19th-century date.
- **Micro-detail priorities:** medieval-origin tower masonry (without claiming all tower fabric is medieval), clock face, cast-iron rainwater goods, ashlar gate piers, wrought-iron gatework and individually documented grave markers.
- **Sensitivity:** Grave markers are funerary heritage. Photograph respectfully, avoid intrusive close-ups of recent/private memorials, and check access rules.

## 3. Graveyard inscription lead — Thomas Betson, 1707

- **Archival source:** National Folklore Collection, Schools' Collection, Leixlip, Volume 0773, page 285, collected 10 January 1939.
- **Transcribed description:** A diamond-shaped stone set into a wall, with an inscription commemorating Thomas Betson, who died 15 March 1707, and a carved lamb below.
- **Evidence status:** This is a 1939 community account, not proof that the stone remains in its described position or is currently legible. The Kildare eHistory graveyard transcription project also warns that some stones have become illegible or disappeared since earlier surveys.
- **Action:** Treat as a targeted field-identification lead only. Do not assign an object ID or publish a current location until the stone is positively identified and its present condition documented.

## 4. Leixlip Boathouse — riverside folly and iron mooring ring

- **Heritage Trail source:** Leixlip Tidy Towns, *Leixlip Heritage Trail*.
- **Interpretation:** The trail identifies the Boat House as a folly marking the eastern edge of Leixlip Demesne, built by George Cavendish, and records an iron ring fixed to the stone wall beneath it for tying up a boat.
- **Micro-detail priority:** The iron ring is a distinct and unusually specific photographic target, but it should initially be recorded as a parent-linked detail of the Boathouse if confirmed in situ.
- **Evidence caveat:** The iron-ring statement is from a local heritage-trail leaflet, not an NIAH survey in this research pass. Present survival and exact position need field verification.

## 5. Rye Bridge, covered well, old distillery and mill traces

The local Heritage Trail identifies additional targets along the Rye Water corridor:
- Rye Bridge and its historic crossing.
- A covered well on the Rye River Walk.
- The former distillery and Ryevale House.
- The old mill-related watercourse and structures.
- A historic bridge associated with the Ryevale / distillery area.

The leaflet explains that the Rye and Liffey were used to power mill wheels and generators. It also describes the historic water-powered industrial landscape and the former weir upstream of the mills at the bottom of Mill Lane, removed in the late 1940s by ESB works.

These are discovery leads, not yet official record-level identifications. The exact bridge names, construction dates, survival and public access need cross-checking against NIAH, RMP and historic mapping before any catalogue promotion.

## 6. Salmon Leap and hydroelectric transformation

- **Historical context:** Leixlip's name is associated with the salmon leap / rapids on the Liffey.
- **Change:** AskAboutIreland's transport-history resource states that the waterfalls are no longer visible, having been submerged in 1947 by a hydroelectric dam.
- **Archive implication:** The historic falls are a landscape/history subject, not a surviving waterfall photo target. Potential surviving evidence relates to the hydroelectric infrastructure and altered river landscape, subject to access and asset verification.
- **No assumption:** This research does not identify the exact dam component, operating status or current public access.

## 7. Coldblow Bridge — NIAH record checked

- **NIAH:** 11201145.
- **Date:** c.1765; NIAH survey range 1750–1780.
- **Description:** Squared limestone abutment of a former road bridge, with the remains of one arch, ashlar voussoirs and springing course, and steel railings above.
- **Historical map evidence:** The 2021 Lucan archaeological impact assessment reports that the surviving remains are labelled “Piers of Br.” on the first-edition Ordnance Survey map of 1836–37.
- **Archive status:** This is a clearly distinct surviving fragment, not a complete bridge. It was not added in this batch because the Lucan research pass already identified it and complete catalogue reconciliation is still outstanding.
