## 2. Primary published location

Bradley & King's 1988 County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey describes the fifteenth-century west tower as a rectangular structure with rounded towers at its angles. It records a carved stone head “high up on the southeast corner of the tower”. The survey provides no dimensions, material, detailed iconographic description or date for the head.

Nigel King's 1996 NCAD thesis, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk*, is more explicit: it places a carved stone head high in the south-east corner of the belfry. The thesis also reproduces a photograph captioned “Disembodied head from Lusk” (Fig. 31) and a sketch captioned “Stone head from Lusk”. The photograph is a distant architectural view and does not provide a sharp close-up of the carving.

Sources:
- Bradley & King (1988), p. 263.
- Nigel King, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk* (NCAD thesis, 1996), Chapter 4, p. 54; Fig. 31, p. 80: https://thesis.ncad.ie/T1727_The%20ecclesiastical%20buildings%20at%20Lusk_NC00210617.pdf

## 3. Proposed earlier origin — a hypothesis, not a proven date

King's 1996 thesis states that the head is thought to date from an earlier church and to have been fitted onto the tower during its construction. The thesis compares the practice with similar carved heads set into other medieval church structures in Fingal.

This is a useful interpretation, but the thesis does not provide a scientific date, stone analysis, excavation context, documentary record of reuse, or a measured architectural phasing analysis for this particular head. The 1988 survey itself only records the feature and its location.

Recommended archive wording:
“Carved stone head set high on the south-east corner of Lusk's square belfry. Nigel King (1996) suggests it may have originated in an earlier church and been reused when the tower was built; this proposed reuse and date are unconfirmed.”

Do not assign an Early Christian or medieval date as a settled fact. The current architectural setting is the fifteenth-century belfry, but the head's carving could pre-date that setting if the reuse hypothesis is correct.

## 4. Description and iconography — limits of the available image

King's thesis labels the feature a “disembodied head”. Its Fig. 31 drawing shows a roughly shaped frontal human face with prominent brow, eyes, nose and a downturned mouth. The accompanying photograph shows the projecting stone high on the masonry, but the face itself is too distant / poorly resolved in the reproduced image to establish fine details.

The thesis also contains a separate plate captioned “Stone head from Grace Dieu which is in the Museum at Lusk”. That is a different object and must not be used as a photograph of the Lusk tower head. The thesis separately illustrates a head from Balrothery (Fig. 14). Neither the Grace Dieu museum head nor the Balrothery head is the Lusk tower head.

No reliable evidence in the sources consulted establishes:
- The head's gender, age or personal identity.
- Whether it depicts a saint, donor, mason, warrior, grotesque or other figure.
- Whether it functioned as a gargoyle or had any water-discharge role.
- Its original position before any possible reuse.
- Its exact dimensions, stone type, carving technique or conservation condition.

Do not describe it as a gargoyle, sheela-na-gig, portrait or named saint without specialist evidence. “Carved stone head” is the safest name.

Source: King (1996), pp. 54 and 80–81, Fig. 31 and comparative figures.

## 5. NMS reference and catalogue cross-check

Fingal's 2023 RMP/SMR appendix lists **DU008-010010- — Stone head — LUSK**. The parish heritage profile also lists a Stone head, Unknown period, Other or unclassified class, DU008-010010.

The monument reference is a valuable identifier for the stone head. The period is listed as unknown, which is consistent with the absence of a secure date in the 1988 survey and the tentative reuse interpretation in the 1996 thesis.

A click-through from the parish heritage profile produced a mismatched ArcGIS query for DU008-006005 rather than DU008-010010. Do not use that click-through as the geometry or primary record for this head. ## 6. Current survival and access

The 1988 survey and 1996 thesis both record the head in the tower. These are historical observations and do not constitute a fresh 2026 condition inspection. The 1996 thesis includes a distant photograph, not a detailed current condition record.

Heritage Ireland currently advertises guided tours of Lusk Round Tower and Belfry during specified dates in 2026. The page identifies the adjacent fifteenth-century belfry and its interior monuments, but does not specifically state whether the high-level carved head is visible from the visitor route.

Do not promise that visitors can see or photograph the head. Confirm current tour arrangements and ask the guide/site operator whether the south-east corner is visible from an authorised visitor position. Do not climb, enter restricted spaces, use ladders, drones or long poles, or attempt close-up photography without express permission.

## 8. Founder field-verification brief

During an authorised visit:
1. Establish the south-east corner of the square belfry using the building orientation, not a guessed viewpoint.
2. Photograph the full corner and surrounding masonry from a safe public visitor position.
3. Use an optical zoom only if permitted; do not use a drone, pole, ladder or climb.
4. Record whether the head visibly projects from the masonry and how it relates to adjacent courses or blocks.
5. Capture a close view only if safely possible from the authorised visitor route.
6. Record apparent stone colour and surface condition without claiming a material identification.
7. Note visible losses, cracks, biological growth or repairs descriptively.
8. Compare the photograph with King's 1996 Fig. 31, while allowing for differences in camera angle, repairs and lighting.
9. Record date, camera position, permission and photographer.
10. Do not touch, wet, clean, chalk, rub or probe the stone.

## 9. Relationship to other Lusk carved heads and monuments

Keep this stone distinct from:
- The missing “Idol” / possible sheela-na-gig described by Austin Cooper in 1783 and later accounts as having been buried around 1843–1844. Its present location is unknown. It is not the head high on the belfry corner.
- The separate Grace Dieu stone head held in the Museum at Lusk, as described and illustrated in King's thesis.
- The separate carved head at Balrothery, shown in King's comparative illustration.
- The Lusk tomb effigies, grave slabs and fireplace plaque.

The word “disembodied” is King's descriptive label for a head without a body; it does not establish a connection with the missing Lusk “Idol”.

## 12. Conclusion and next steps

The Lusk tower head is a separately registered archaeological object (DU008-010010-) and is recorded by the 1988 survey at the south-east corner of the fifteenth-century belfry. Nigel King's 1996 thesis proposes that it may have come from an earlier church and been reused in the tower, but this remains an interpretation. The NMS period is listed as unknown. The thesis provides a sketch and a distant contextual photograph, not a clear modern close-up.

Preserve it as a carved stone head, not as a named person, saint, gargoyle or sheela-na-gig. Verify the official monument geometry and current survival before creating a production child record or image entry.

**Next research target:** the lost Lusk “Idol” / possible sheela-na-gig described by Austin Cooper in 1783. It must be treated as a separate, unlocated historical object, not confused with the surviving tower head.
