# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Bullaun Stone Research Batch 189

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Research the granite bullaun recorded at the entrance / flowerbed of St MacCullin's Roman Catholic Church, Chapel Road, Lusk, and resolve conflicting accounts connecting it with St MacCullin's Well.  
**Status:** Research-only. Do not merge the present church bullaun with the separate well-associated stone described as missing since the 1940s. No new permanent ID, source-registry entry, visual record or production map pin created while the canonical catalogue remains unavailable for complete validation.

## 1. Principal finding: identity is not fully resolved

The evidence establishes a bullaun stone at St MacCullin's Catholic Church, Lusk. It does **not** conclusively establish whether this is the same stone that formerly lay beside St MacCullin's Well.

Two strands of evidence conflict:

1. Fingal County Council's 2012 monument-management report records the church-entrance stone and says local tradition holds that it *may* be the stone formerly beside St MacCullin's Well.
2. Love Lusk's local heritage page distinguishes the bullaun at the church gates from a separate stone associated with St MacCullin's Well, reportedly in a back garden and missing since the 1940s. The Ireland's Holy Wells County-by-County record repeats the missing-stone account from Gary Branigan (2012).

**Archive decision:** maintain two research identities until field evidence, photographs or primary records demonstrate equivalence:
- **Object A — Lusk Church-Entrance Bullaun:** the extant stone recorded at St MacCullin's Catholic Church.
- **Object B — St MacCullin's Well Knee-Impression Stone:** a reported stone associated with the well, last seen in the 1940s according to the later published account; present whereabouts unknown.

This is a distinction between evidence records, not a claim that two physically distinct stones have been conclusively proven.

## 2. Object A — Lusk Church-Entrance Bullaun

### Official/local-authority record

Fingal County Council, *Managing Fingal County Council's Archaeological Resource* (March 2012), entry No. 51, identifies “Lusk Bullaun Stone”, reference DU008-010-01, at St MacCullin's Catholic Church, Chapel Road, Lusk.

The RMP description records:
- Sub-circular shape.
- Projecting lug.
- Width: 0.33 m.
- Depth: 0.26 m.
- Height: 0.30 m.

The same report reproduces a photograph showing a pale stone in a flowerbed, with a hollow in its upper surface. The Fingal Field Monument Advisor report says the stone had been overturned but was upright again on subsequent visits. The report records access through the church gates and lists removal as a threat.

The report's wording is cautious: local tradition says this *may* be the stone that formerly lay by St MacCullin's Well. It does not prove that identification.

Source: Christine Baker, *Managing Fingal County Council's Archaeological Resource* (March 2012), No. 51, pp. 142–143:
https://www.fingal.ie/sites/default/files/2019-03/Managing%20Fingal%20County%20Councils%20Archaeological%20Resource.pdf

### 2008 graveyard survey

Fingal's Historic Graveyards Project, Volume 2, survey FHG 25, records St MacCullin's (RC), Lusk, surveyed 27 July 2008. It describes the graveyard as having no upstanding archaeological features other than a bullaun stone, “likely to have been moved from its original position”.

The survey records:
- Townland: Greatcommon.
- Address: Chapel Road, Lusk, County Dublin, Ireland.
- RMP area: DU008-010.
- RPS reference: 301.
- NIAH church reference: 11323003.
- Church: Neo-Romanesque, c.1924.
- Ownership: Roman Catholic Church.

The survey's general description does not independently establish the stone's original location, date or association with the holy well.

Source: Fingal Historic Graveyards Project, Volume 2, FHG 25, St MacCullin's (RC), Lusk:
https://www.fingal.ie/sites/default/files/2019-03/Fingal%20Historic%20Graveyards%20Project%20Volume%202.pdf

## 3. Object B — reported missing stone at St MacCullin's Well

The Ireland's Holy Wells County-by-County record for St MacCullin's Well states:
- The well is in Lusk townland, County Dublin.
- Gary Branigan (2012:40) describes it as being in the back garden of Barrack House on Barrack land in Lusk.
- The well had been rebuilt in the form of a wishing well.
- Local legend says a stone in the well bore impressions of the saint's knees.
- The record says this stone, likely a bullaun, was no longer present and was last seen in the 1940s.

The claimed knee impressions and saint's jump from the round tower are folklore, not archaeological proof of an event or of the stone's identity. The healing association is also a tradition, not medical evidence.

Source: “St. MacCullin's Well”, Ireland's Holy Wells County-by-County, citing Gary Branigan, *Ancient and Holy Wells of Ireland* (2012), p. 40:
https://ihwcbc.omeka.net/items/show/402

Love Lusk's local heritage page adds a particularly important distinction: it says the bullaun most associated with St MacCullin's Well was located in the back of a garden in Lusk and had been missing since the 1940s, while the stone currently at the church gates is separately described as a possible bullaun. It invites local information about the origin of the church-gate stone and whereabouts of the well-associated stone.

Source: Love Lusk, “Lusk's Bulluan Stone”:
https://www.lovelusk.ie/home/loveluskheritage/bulluan-stone/

## 4. Later NMS-derived profile and the record-reference discrepancy

Daniel Kirkpatrick's Balrothery East Heritage Profile has a “Bullaun stone (present location)” entry, compiled/revised by Caimin O'Brien and revised 26 July 2023. It identifies the stone outside the entrance to the modern St MacCullin's RC church as the same stone formerly inside the medieval church tower at Lusk. It cites Hunt (1974, p.146) and Roe (1979, p.140) for the assemblage within the tower: a seventeenth-century font, a bullaun stone and part of a fireplace removed from Bremore Castle.

The profile labels the present-location record DU008-075 and cross-references DU008-010001-. The 2012 Fingal management report instead labels its church-entrance bullaun DU008-010-01. These identifiers should be preserved exactly as each source gives them until the official NMS record is checked directly and the relationship clarified. Do not silently normalise punctuation or assume the two entries are separate monuments.

The 2023 profile's claimed movement from the medieval tower is relevant, but it does not resolve whether the bullaun it describes is the missing well-associated stone in Object B.

Source:
https://www.danielkirkpatrick.co.uk/baronies/balrothery-east-dublin-heritage-profile/

## 5. Earlier archaeological report concerning the well

The Excavations.ie record for 1991 describes St MacCullin's Well as being in the back garden of a house immediately south of the monastic site. It says a concrete pipe enclosed the water and that “the stone with the marks of the saint”, described as a probable bullaun stone and mentioned by Stubbs in 1914, had been removed.

This is independent support for an earlier report of a stone associated with the well having been removed. It does not identify that stone with the bullaun now at the church entrance.

Source: Excavations.ie, 1991 report, Lusk (Dublin), site DU008-010:
https://excavations.ie/report/1991/Dublin/0001110/

## 7. Coordinates and mapping

The Fingal graveyard survey provides coordinates 321384 / 254823 for the St MacCullin's RC graveyard survey area, not a surveyed point for the bullaun itself. The 2012 management report provides a location map and says the stone is at the church entrance, but the coordinate cannot be treated as an exact object position.

Do not create a precise pin for the bullaun from the graveyard centroid. Do not map the missing well-associated stone as if it were extant. Any future map entry must use a verified monument point or clearly label the location as approximate / site-level.

## 8. Founder field-verification brief

If visiting, observe public access and parish/church permission:
1. Photograph the church entrance and flowerbed context without entering restricted areas.
2. Photograph the stone from above and all sides, including the projecting lug.
3. Record the hollow's outline, dimensions and orientation without touching or wetting it.
4. Look for any visible cracks, losses, modern repairs or movement supports; describe only what is visible.
5. Note whether the stone is still in the flowerbed and whether its position matches the historic photograph in the 2012 report.
6. Do not assume that a similar hollow or mark proves identity with the missing well stone.
7. If speaking with local residents or parish staff, record the speaker's permission, date and exact wording; distinguish oral testimony from documentary evidence.
8. Do not enter the Barrack House garden or private property to search for the missing stone.

## 9. Duplicate and production controls

Repository search found the bullaun already listed as a proposed Lusk micro-object in Batch 148. This research clarifies that the object identity itself needs a two-record investigation.

Before creating permanent records:
1. Restore and validate the complete canonical object catalogue.
2. Search for Lusk bullaun, St MacCullin's bullaun, St MacCullin's Well stone, DU008-010-01, DU008-075, DU008-010001 and the church entrance stone.
3. Determine whether the existing catalogue already has an object record for the church-entrance stone.
4. If so, retain its ID and add source-attributed identity uncertainty; do not create a duplicate.
5. If no record exists, create Object A as a child of the relevant church/archaeological-site parent only after schema and relationship checks.
6. Keep Object B as a missing-object research record only if the archive supports unlocated historical objects; otherwise retain it in documentation and do not map it.
7. Do not publish exact WGS84 coordinates from the graveyard survey centroid.

## 11. Conclusion and next steps

The church-entrance granite bullaun is a documented Lusk heritage object with a source-published form and dimensions. Its original position and relationship to St MacCullin's Well are uncertain. A separate tradition describes a stone with knee impressions at the well, reportedly missing since the 1940s. The two accounts overlap but do not establish that the stones are identical.

The correct evidence-first outcome is to retain both research identities, keep the well-associated stone unlocated, avoid a fabricated map pin, and seek primary NMS records or field/oral evidence before final catalogue reconciliation.

**Next research target:** the small piscina recorded in the west entrance porch of the historic Lusk church complex. The 1988 survey says it may have been salvaged from the medieval church; that origin must remain provisional until corroborated.
