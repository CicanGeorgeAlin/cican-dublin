# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Christianity Stone and the 1753-Discovered Monument Research Batch 194

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Distinguish the modern Christianity Stone created by Lusk Tidy Towns from the historical stone it is said to commemorate; investigate the 1753 reference in historical descriptions, its iconography, location, survival and whether it is the same object as the modern marker.  
**Status:** Research-only. The modern marker and historical monument are treated as separate objects. The 1753 event is a reported discovery date, not yet demonstrated to be a date carved on the stone. No permanent CICAN IDs, source-registry entries, visual records or production pins created while the canonical catalogue remains unavailable for complete validation.

## 1. Principal finding — two objects, and a date wording problem

Love Lusk describes the **Christianity Stone** as a modern heritage marker created by Lusk Tidy Towns. It says the marker draws inspiration from an historic stone “dated 1753”. The Lusk Heritage Group separately confirms that it marked an anniversary of Christianity coming to Lusk with a Christianity Stone.

A nineteenth-century historical description by Dalton, however, describes a “very ancient monument” in the old church, made of coarse grit stone, which **“was dug up in 1753”**. Dalton then describes a relief of Christ on the cross, a bust of an old man holding a ball and cross, and an armorial shield with a bird. This source wording indicates that 1753 was the reported year of discovery/excavation, not necessarily a date inscribed on the stone.

**Important unresolved discrepancy:** the modern Love Lusk page calls the source stone “dated 1753”, whereas Dalton's historical description says it was “dug up in 1753”. The two statements are not equivalent. No image or inscription reading located in this research pass establishes that the stone itself bears the date 1753.

Archive decision:
- **Object A:** Lusk Christianity Stone — modern commemorative marker created by Lusk Tidy Towns.
- **Object B:** Lusk Historic Relief Monument Discovered in 1753 — historical carved stone described by Dalton; present survival/location not established in this research pass.
- Keep the relationship as “modern marker said by Love Lusk to draw inspiration from historic stone”; do not assume the modern marker is the original stone or a replica of it.

## 2. Object A — the modern Christianity Stone

Love Lusk's place record identifies the Christianity Stone as:
- A commemorative stone / modern heritage marker.
- Created by Lusk Tidy Towns.
- Located at St Macullins Church, Lusk.
- Intended to mark Lusk's deep Christian heritage and long history as an ecclesiastical centre.
- Described as inspired by a historic stone said to be dated 1753.
- Listed as visible at all times and visible from surrounding public space.

The Lusk Heritage Group's overview says the group marked occasions such as the anniversary of Christianity coming to Lusk with a Christianity Stone. It also lists other separate group commissions, including the Raven Sculpture, Archaeology Stone and Sign of Unity & Friendship. Do not confuse those other community artworks with the Christianity Stone.

The modern marker's precise commissioning date, designer, sculptor, material, dimensions, inscription, unveiling date and exact coordinates are not stated in the sources consulted in this batch. The organisation is identified as Lusk Tidy Towns by the Love Lusk place record; the Heritage Group overview provides corroborating community context but does not give a detailed commission file.

## 3. Object B — Dalton's historical monument discovered in 1753

Dalton's *History of County Dublin*, Chapter 20, Lusk, describes a monument inserted into one of the blocked arches dividing the two aisles of the old church. He says it was “dug up in 1753” and was made of coarse grit stone, of ordinary dimensions.

Dalton's description of the relief:
- A representation of the Saviour on the cross occupies about two-thirds of the stone's length.
- In the upper third, at the left, is the bust of an old man.
- The old man holds a ball and cross.
- At the right is an armorial shield on which a bird can be plainly traced.
- The monument was inserted in a stopped-up arch between the two aisles.

This is a detailed antiquarian description, but Dalton does not provide a transcription of an inscription, exact measurements, a maker, a date of manufacture or a modern inventory reference. He does not say that the stone was made in 1753. The year is attached to the reported discovery (“dug up”), not to a carved date.

Source:
- Dalton, *History of County Dublin*, Chapter 20, Lusk, lines 17–20 in the online transcription: https://chaptersofdublin.ie/Dalton/Lusk.htm

## 4. Is the historic stone still at Lusk?

Dalton's description places the historic relief in the old church, inserted into a blocked arch between the aisles. The 1988 County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey and Nigel King's 1996 thesis are useful later accounts of Lusk's surviving church objects, but this research pass did not establish a definite modern identification of Dalton's relief monument or its present location.

The 1988 survey's Lusk monument list describes the font, bullaun, fireplace plaque and tombs, but the available description does not clearly identify a stone matching Dalton's crucifixion / bust / armorial composition. King's 1996 thesis lists several other finds and objects, including the cresset stone, carved heads, tiles, tombs and bullaun, but no confirmed match for Dalton's relief monument was established in the pages checked.

Therefore:
- **Historical presence at Lusk:** documented by Dalton.
- **Reported discovery:** 1753.
- **Later survival in situ:** not established.
- **Current location:** unknown from the sources checked.
- **Whether it survives as part of the modern Christianity Stone:** no evidence found.
- **Whether the modern marker is a faithful replica:** no evidence found.

Do not claim the historical relief is still installed in the church or that it was removed to a museum without a traceable source.

## 5. Iconography — what can and cannot be interpreted

Dalton's description allows a cautious visual inventory:
- Christ crucified, in relief.
- A bust of an older man.
- A ball and cross held by that figure.
- An armorial shield with a bird.

The ball and cross may represent an orb and cross, a traditional symbol of Christian rulership or authority, but Dalton's wording does not identify the person or explain the object. The bird on the shield is not enough to assign a family without heraldic comparison. No named donor, deceased person, saint or family is established.

Do not identify the bust as a king, pope, bishop, founder, patron or member of a named family without independent evidence. Do not infer that the shield is the arms of a particular Lusk family based only on a bird motif.

The composition could be a funerary or commemorative relief, but Dalton calls it a monument without defining its original function. Retain “historic relief monument” as the working object type until specialist assessment or an image is located.

## 6. Relationship to the modern Christianity Stone

The modern Christianity Stone is a contemporary community heritage marker. The historical relief is a separate object described by Dalton. Love Lusk says the modern marker draws inspiration from an historic stone said to be “dated 1753”. Dalton's account provides a likely historical reference point, but the discrepancy in date wording must remain visible.

Recommended relationship wording:
“Modern community marker created by Lusk Tidy Towns; Love Lusk states that it draws inspiration from a historic stone associated with 1753. Dalton's nineteenth-century account describes a separate historic relief monument discovered in 1753. The exact relationship between the marker and Dalton's monument has not been demonstrated.”

No claim of physical continuity, reuse, replica status or exact design copying should be made without a commission record or comparison of the two objects.

## 7. Site context

Dalton describes the old church as a two-aisled building divided by seven arches. The relief monument was inserted in one of the blocked arches between the aisles. This places the historic monument in the old parish church, not automatically at the modern marker's outdoor location.

The present Church of Ireland building was built in 1847 and is attached to the earlier square tower and round tower. The modern Christianity Stone is described by Love Lusk as located at St Macullins Church and visible from public space. These are related within the same wider ecclesiastical complex but remain distinct settings.

4. If an image is located, authenticate its Lusk provenance before registering it.

## 9. Coordinates and map policy

The modern Christianity Stone is described at St Macullins Church, but no object-specific coordinates were established in this research pass. Do not use the church's building centroid as a precise coordinate for the marker.

The historic relief's original setting is described inside the old church. Its current location is unknown. It must not receive an extant-object map pin. If the archive later maps the historical association, use the church complex as a contextual place only and clearly label the relief as unlocated.

## 12. Conclusion and next steps

The modern Christianity Stone is a community heritage marker created by Lusk Tidy Towns. Love Lusk says it draws inspiration from a historic stone associated with 1753. Dalton's historical description identifies a separate ancient relief monument in the old church and says it was **dug up in 1753**. He describes Christ on the cross, a bust holding a ball and cross, and an armorial shield bearing a bird.

The sources do not yet prove that Dalton's monument is the exact source stone behind the modern marker, that 1753 is carved on the original, or that the historic relief survives today. The archive must preserve those uncertainties.

**Next research target:** the small carved warrior figure in the wall opposite the entrance to Lusk's old church, mentioned by Dalton immediately after his account of the lost Idol. Determine whether this is the same as a separately recorded object, whether it survives, and whether later archaeological surveys or King's thesis document it. Do not confuse it with the carved head high on the belfry.
