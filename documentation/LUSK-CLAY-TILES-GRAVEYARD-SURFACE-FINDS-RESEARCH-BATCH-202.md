# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Clay Tile and Graveyard Surface Finds Research Batch 202

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Investigate National Museum Topographical Files references 1990:55 and 1999:151–173; distinguish these records from the 1973/1975 medieval tile finds; compare them with the 2004 excavation evidence for medieval floor-tile fragments in demolition material at St MacCullin's Church.  
**Status:** Two Topographical Files records are documented in a 2012 assessment. The 1999 group is a mixed surface collection with no precise context. A separate 2004 excavation report confirms two 15th-century floor-tile fragments in demolition material. The identities and relationships between these records are not established. No production catalogue entries or map pins created.

## 1. Principal finding — three distinct evidence groups

The sources establish at least three documentary groups that must remain separate:

**A. NMI Topographical Files 1990:55**
- Townland: Lusk.
- Find: one clay tile.
- Find place: unknown.
- Description: clay tile.
- No date, dimensions, design, fabric, photograph or exact find context is given in the 2012 summary.

**B. NMI Topographical Files 1999:151–173**
- Townland: Lusk.
- Find place: surface at Lusk Graveyard.
- Find list: clay floor tiles, roof tiles, brick, baked-clay fragments and three pottery sherds.
- Description column: clay floor tiles, roof tiles, brick, six unclassifiable pieces of baked clay and glazed pottery sherds.
- The discrepancy between “3 pottery sherds” in the find field and “glazed pottery sherds” in the description is preserved as published; do not silently resolve it.
- The entry is a mixed group of building-material and pottery finds, not a single identified medieval tile.

**C. 2004 archaeological monitoring at St MacCullin's Church**
- Two fragments of floor tile were found in made ground containing material from demolition of the medieval church.
- The Excavations.ie report dates these two fragments to the 15th century.
- The fragments were recovered in service-trench monitoring during refurbishment of the 19th-century church.
- The report does not identify them with either NMI record 1990:55 or 1999:151–173.

These three records must also remain separate from the 1973 mosaic fragment and seven tiles recorded in the 1975 maintenance-work group in Nigel King's 1996 thesis.

## 2. NMI record 1990:55 — one clay tile, unknown findspot

The 2012 Greater Dublin Drainage cultural heritage assessment reproduces a Topographical Files entry:
- Museum number: 1990:55.
- Townland: Lusk.
- Find: clay tile.
- Find place: unknown.
- Description: clay tile.

The record is too brief to classify the object as a medieval floor tile, roof tile, paving tile, brick fragment or architectural fitting. It contains no published dimensions, decoration, date or photograph.

The year-like prefix in the museum number should not be assumed to be the exact discovery year without consulting the original file or accession register.

**Archive decision:** preserve as a single clay-tile record with unknown find context and unknown type beyond the source's generic term “clay tile”. Do not attach it to the church floor, graveyard surface collection or 1975 group.

## 3. NMI group 1999:151–173 — mixed surface finds at Lusk Graveyard

The same 2012 appendix records a range of materials under museum numbers 1999:151–173:
- clay floor tiles;
- roof tiles;
- brick;
- baked-clay fragments;
- pottery sherds.

The find place is described as “Found on surface at Lusk Graveyard”.

The description field separately lists:
- clay floor tiles;
- roof tiles;
- brick;
- six unclassifiable pieces of baked clay;
- glazed pottery sherds.

The source's find field says “3 pottery sherds”, while its description says “Glazed pottery sherds” without repeating the count. Retain this difference in any future transcription and check the original Topographical Files to establish the exact inventory.

The record does not state:
- that the tiles are medieval;
- that all pieces came from one deposit;
- that the surface collection was recovered during a controlled excavation;
- the exact point or area of the graveyard;
- whether floor tiles and roof tiles belong to the same building;
- the date or function of the brick;
- the fabric, dimensions or decoration of individual pieces;
- whether the material is associated with the old medieval church or later graveyard activity.

A surface find context is not a stratified archaeological context. The mixed material could represent more than one period or building episode. No specific chronology should be assigned without inspection of the objects or the original museum records.

**Archive decision:** treat this as one museum-number range representing a mixed surface collection, with individual material categories recorded separately only when the original inventory permits. Do not split the range into invented individual artefacts or assign medieval dates to all clay objects.

## 4. Independent 2004 evidence — two 15th-century floor-tile fragments in demolition material

Excavations.ie entry **2004:0621 — LUSK: St Macullin's Church**, licence 04E1031, by Jonathan Dempsey, records archaeological monitoring during refurbishment of St MacCullin's Church.

The report states:
- Two service trenches were monitored on 12 October, 30 November and 1 December 2004.
- Under approximately 0.4–0.5 m of made ground, at least two inhumations were identified in an undisturbed palaeosol.
- The made ground consisted mainly of material from demolition of the medieval church.
- This demolition material included two fragments of floor tile, dated in the report to the 15th century.
- The trench works were altered to preserve the human remains in situ.

This is important independent evidence for 15th-century floor-tile material in the church demolition deposits. However, the report does not give museum numbers for the two fragments and does not link them to 1990:55, 1999:151–173 or the 1973/1975 tile finds.

The two 2004 fragments should therefore be recorded as a distinct archaeological recovery episode. Their presence in demolition material does not mean they were found in their original floor position.

The report provides a site coordinate for the church investigation (ITM E 721533, N 754446; 53.525904, -6.167016). This is the location of the monitored church works, not a precise findspot coordinate for either individual tile fragment.

Source:
https://excavations.ie/report/2004/Dublin/0011745/

## 5. Relationship to the 1973 and 1975 tile discoveries

Nigel King's 1996 thesis describes:
- one line-impressed mosaic fragment found among debris removed from the belfry in 1973;
- a separate group of seven tiles found during maintenance work in 1975: one line-impressed mosaic, four line-impressed tiles and two relief tiles.

These are not the same documentary entries as the 1990:55 clay tile or the 1999:151–173 surface collection, unless original museum documentation establishes a link.

The 2004 report's two 15th-century tile fragments may be broadly consistent with King's discussion of medieval church floor material, but the 2004 report gives no cross-reference to King's finds or NMI museum numbers.

**Do not merge any of these groups.** The evidence currently describes separate dates, recovery circumstances and record systems.

## 6. What does “clay tile” mean here?

The 2012 summary's generic term “clay tile” is not enough to establish function. The mixed 1999 group expressly distinguishes floor tiles and roof tiles, while the 1990:55 entry says only “clay tile”.

A tile's function may be identified by shape, thickness, surface finish, glaze, fabric, wear, fixing holes, curvature or association with a structure. None of these diagnostic details is supplied for 1990:55 in the published summary.

For the 1999 group, the published categories provide a broad distinction between floor tiles and roof tiles, but not individual dimensions or designs. The “baked clay fragments” should not be relabelled as tile fragments when the source itself calls six of them unclassifiable.

## 7. Custody, access and original-file verification

The 2012 report reproduces information attributed to the National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files. It does not establish the current physical custody of every object or whether the records include photographs.

The National Monuments Service's public page lists Topographical Files among its catalogued collections and states that most listed collections are available for researcher consultation by prior appointment. It provides the archive contact address **nmarchive@housing.gov.ie** for enquiries about relevance and access.

This is an access lead for the NMS archive collection. It does not prove that the physical finds represented by the 1990:55 and 1999:151–173 entries are held there or available to view.

Recommended request:
- confirm the exact wording and original documentation for 1990:55;
- obtain the itemised list for 1999:151–173;
- check whether photographs, drawings, finders' correspondence or acquisition records exist;
- establish whether the 2004 tile fragments received museum numbers;
- cross-reference the records against the 1973/1975 Lusk tile finds.

## 8. Context and location confidence

- **1990:55:** townland Lusk only; findspot unknown.
- **1999:151–173:** surface of Lusk Graveyard; no precise location within the graveyard.
- **2004:0621:** service-trench monitoring at St MacCullin's Church; site coordinate is known, but individual tile-fragment findspots are not.
- **1973 fragment:** debris removed from belfry; original floor position uncertain.
- **1975 group:** maintenance-work find at Lusk; exact room or floor position not established in the thesis.

No individual tile coordinate should be created. A church or graveyard site coordinate must not be represented as the exact findspot of a portable artefact.

## 11. Conclusion and next target

The 1990:55 record is one clay tile from Lusk with an unknown findspot and no published diagnostic description. The 1999:151–173 record is a mixed surface collection from Lusk Graveyard, including floor tiles, roof tiles, brick, baked-clay fragments and pottery sherds. It cannot be assumed to be a medieval floor-tile assemblage.

A separate 2004 archaeological monitoring report records two 15th-century floor-tile fragments within made ground derived mainly from demolition of the medieval church. These are a distinct recovery episode and are not linked by accession number to either Topographical Files entry.

The archive should preserve the five documentary episodes separately: 1973, 1975, 1990:55, 1999:151–173 and 2004. The next step is to obtain the original Topographical Files itemisation and compare it with the published descriptions, rather than guessing at object identities.

**Next research target:** Lusk's medieval and post-medieval pottery sherds, beginning with the three pottery sherds included in the 1999:151–173 surface-find range. Determine whether the original file identifies ceramic fabrics, forms, glaze, date or provenance, and keep them separate from the unclassified baked-clay fragments.
