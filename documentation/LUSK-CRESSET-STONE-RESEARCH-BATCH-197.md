# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Cresset Stone Research Batch 197

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Identify the cresset stone found at Lusk, document its physical description and discovery context, and trace the evidence for its custody and visual record.  
**Status:** Historical object research. A photograph and measured description are present in Nigel King's 1996 thesis. Current custody and present accessibility have not been independently verified. No production catalogue entry or map pin created.

## 1. Principal finding

Nigel King's 1996 thesis, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk*, documents a small stone cresset found at Lusk during maintenance work. King describes it as sandstone, under 20 cm high, approximately 12 cm across the top and 10 cm across the bottom. It has a single cup, a chalice-like profile, a flat underside, no decoration, visible chipping inside the cup and evidence of exposure to fire.

King assigns it to the first class of Irish cresset stones and proposes a date between the tenth and twelfth centuries. These are the thesis author's reported classification and dating, not a new independent scientific assessment in this batch.

**Crucially, the thesis includes a photograph of the actual Lusk object**, captioned “Cresset stone from Lusk” on printed page 77 (PDF page image following page 76). The image shows a compact, brown, cup-topped stone with a narrowed stem and broader base; a measuring scale is visible beside it.

## 3. Photograph and figure-number caution

The thesis's figure numbering is inconsistent in the scanned copy:
- The text identifies the cresset discussion in the section “The Cresset Stone (O.P.W.)”.
- The photograph on printed page 77 is captioned “Fig 31 Cresset stone from Lusk”.
- Elsewhere in the thesis, Fig. 31 is also used in connection with a stone head from Lusk, and the list of illustrations is imperfectly aligned with the scanned figure sequence.

For archive purposes, identify the image by its **caption and printed page**, not figure number alone:
**“Cresset stone from Lusk”, Nigel King, 1996 thesis, printed p.77.**

The photograph is evidence for the object's appearance as recorded in 1996. It is not proof of its current condition or current location.

## 4. Custody — what the thesis actually says

King gives two related but not perfectly identical custody statements:

1. In the introductory section, he says most Lusk finds were in an OPW warehouse and the National Museum, making access difficult, but that he personally succeeded in seeing the cresset stone “in the office of Public Works”.
2. In the conclusion, he says the cresset stone and medieval tiles, among other finds, were kept by the OPW in one of its warehouses.

The most cautious interpretation is that the object was in OPW custody and King personally inspected it during his 1996 research. The exact room or storage location is not fully clear from the thesis, and its present custody cannot be inferred from a 1996 account.

Do not state that the cresset is currently in a public exhibition, currently in a particular warehouse, or available for public inspection without a recent OPW or National Museum confirmation.

## 5. Date and classification — evidence limits

King dates the Lusk stone to the tenth–twelfth century and places it in Class 1. His discussion treats cressets as stone reservoirs for lamps. The thesis refers to Irish cresset-stone typology and cites Mac Noicill and Wallace (1988), pages 367–372, for the distribution map.

This report preserves King's attribution as a published interpretation. It does not upgrade it to a definitive archaeological date. No laboratory analysis, independent specialist reassessment or later catalogue entry confirming the date was located in this batch.

The broad proposed date is consistent with a medieval ecclesiastical context, but that contextual fit alone is not proof of the object's exact date or original use at Lusk.

## 6. Distinguish this object from other Lusk finds

The cresset stone is a separate portable artefact. Do not merge it with:
- the Lusk bullaun stone at or near the church entrance;
- the bullaun stone associated in local tradition with St MacCullin's Well;
- the medieval floor tiles found during maintenance work in 1975;
- the bronze pins found in a nearby cottage garden;
- the stone axehead;
- the carved head high on the south-east corner of the belfry;
- the lost “Idol” or the small warrior carving described by Dalton.

King discusses several of these finds in adjacent sections, but proximity in the thesis does not mean they share a findspot, date, material, ownership or current repository.

## 7. Research leads for custody and catalogue confirmation

The next evidence needed is an official record that can connect the 1996 thesis object to a present-day collection record:
1. Ask the OPW / Heritage Ireland collections or site management whether a Lusk cresset stone is held in their care and whether an accession or inventory number exists.
2. Check National Museum of Ireland collection catalogues and archive records for “Lusk”, “cresset”, “lamp”, and the maintenance-work find.
3. Check whether the original photograph or a higher-resolution image is held by NCAD, the thesis author, OPW or the Lusk heritage exhibition archive.
4. Locate the maintenance-work record that produced the find; the thesis does not specify a date, exact building or precise findspot.
5. Verify whether the 1988 cresset typology reference (Mac Noicill & Wallace, 1988, pp.367–372) includes the Lusk specimen in its catalogue, and whether that reference provides an independent description or classification.

No accession number or official current repository record is supplied by the thesis passages examined here.

## 8. Production and visual archive decision

**Research status: strong historic identification; incomplete modern provenance.**

The thesis photograph is a legitimate historical visual-source lead for this specific object, with its caption and page providing a clear attribution. Before copying it into the production website, CICAN must confirm reproduction rights with the relevant rights holder. The thesis being publicly readable does not itself grant permission to republish its photograph.

Do not register the 1996 thesis photograph as a production asset until:
- the full canonical catalogue and visual registry are restored and validated;
- duplicate source and image IDs are checked;
- image-reuse permission is confirmed or a new authorised photograph is obtained;
- the object record clearly states that the image documents the object in 1996, not its present condition.

No permanent object ID, source-registry record, production visual record or map pin created in this batch. The exact findspot is unknown, so a pin would be misleading.

## 10. Conclusion and next target

The Lusk cresset stone is now supported by a detailed published description and a photograph in King's 1996 thesis. It is described as a small, undecorated sandstone cresset, under 20 cm high, with a single cup and a chalice-like profile. King proposed a tenth–twelfth-century date and reported personally viewing it in OPW custody in 1996.

The object's exact findspot, accession number, current custody, current condition and image-reuse rights remain unverified. The historical photograph should be preserved as a research lead, not silently treated as a current photograph.

**Next research target:** the medieval floor tiles found at Lusk during maintenance work in 1975. Investigate the individual tile designs, original discovery context, published drawings and photographs, current custody, and whether any tile is now on display. Keep each tile type distinct and avoid assigning a precise findspot unless the source supports it.
