# CICAN Dublin Archive — Dan Sherry's Lusk Bronze Pins and Old Coins Research Batch 201

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Follow up the newspaper statement that Dan Sherry had previously found bronze pins and old coins on his property; test whether those pins can be identified with the three-pin Lusk group already documented; check the National Museum Topographical Files summary for coin records.  
**Status:** The newspaper statement is verified as a passage reproduced in Nigel King's 1996 thesis. The three-pin group is independently documented in specialist and survey sources. A connection between Sherry's finds and that group is plausible but unproven. No identifiable coin group, coin type, date, accession number or current custody has been established. No production catalogue entries or map pins created.

## 1. Principal finding — the newspaper gives a lead, not an inventory

The reproduced *Drogheda Independent* clipping, titled **“An historic Lusk find”**, says Dan Sherry “has also found bronze pins and old coins in the past on his property”. This sentence appears in the article about Sherry's discovery of a polished stone axehead during house construction at Barrack Road.

The clipping does not state:
- how many bronze pins he found;
- whether they were one group or separate discoveries;
- how many coins were found;
- whether the coins were bronze, silver or another metal;
- whether the coins were ancient, medieval or modern;
- whether any of the pins or coins were reported to the National Museum;
- whether they were the same three bronze pins later described in archaeological publications.

The phrase “in the past” is not a date. The newspaper extract is reproduced in King's 1996 thesis, but the issue date, page number and original newspaper scan were not established in this batch.

Source: Nigel King, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk* (1996), printed p.56, Fig.16:
https://thesis.ncad.ie/T1727_The%20ecclesiastical%20buildings%20at%20Lusk_NC00210617.pdf

## 2. The three bronze pins — an established separate documentary group

Three bronze stick pins from Lusk are independently recorded in:
- Damian C. MacGarry, “Three Bronze Pins from Lusk, County Dublin”, *Journal of the Royal Society of Antiquaries of Ireland*, vol.110 (1980), pp.153–155;
- Bradley and King, *County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey* (1988), p.268;
- Nigel King's 1996 thesis, printed p.54 and illustrated in the thesis;
- the National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files summary reproduced in the 2012 Greater Dublin Drainage cultural heritage assessment.

The 1988 survey says the three stick pins were found in a cottage garden at Lusk between 1920 and 1950. King's thesis describes a cottage garden approximately 100 yards from the church. The 2012 report's Topographical Files summary describes three decorated dome-headed pins from surface soil in a garden near the round tower.

These descriptions are geographically and materially compatible with the newspaper's broad statement about Sherry having found bronze pins on his property. However, none of these sources names Sherry as the finder of the three-pin group. The link must remain **possible, not confirmed**.

## 3. A second Topographical Files pin entry — possible duplicate or alternate location description

The 2012 report's Appendix 6.14.3 contains two separate entries for three bronze stick pins in Lusk:

1. Museum number “Record only”; find place “Surface soil of a garden near round tower”; description “Decorated dome headed pins”.
2. Museum number “No Number”; find place “Monastic enclosure”; description “3 bronze stick pins”.

The table does not establish whether these are:
- two separate groups of three pins;
- two location descriptions for the same group;
- duplicate legacy records copied from different Topographical Files notes;
- a general site attribution and a more specific garden location.

It would be unsafe to count six pins. The 1980 specialist article and 1988 county survey refer to a group of three, and the two later summary entries may refer to that same group. The original NMI Topographical Files need to be checked to resolve the duplication.

Source: Greater Dublin Drainage 2012, Appendix 6.14.3, printed p.38:
https://www.water.ie/sites/default/files/docs/greater-drainage-dublin/alternative-sites-assessment-phase2/Appendix-6_Cultural-Heritage-Assessment.pdf

## 4. The old coins — no object-level identification

The newspaper gives no description of the coins beyond the phrase “old coins”. No coin date, denomination, issuing authority, metal, number, inscription, condition or photograph is supplied.

A search of the Lusk entries in Appendix 6.14.3 of the 2012 report found no corresponding Lusk coin entry. The appendix does list Lusk's axeheads, bronze pins, tiles and other finds. This absence does not prove that no coins were ever recorded by the National Museum: the appendix is a summary of Topographical Files relevant to a particular cultural heritage assessment, not a complete catalogue of all finds from Lusk.

No securely matching Lusk coin record was identified in this batch. Do not create a generic “Lusk coin hoard” or assign a coin date.

## 5. Correcting the thesis illustration numbering

The thesis's **List of Illustrations** identifies:
- Fig.16 — extract from the *Drogheda Independent*, p.55;
- Fig.18 — three bronze pins found at Lusk, p.64;
- Fig.19 — stone axehead found at Lusk, p.65.

However, the scanned image captions and page sequence are not perfectly consistent with the list. In the text extraction, the newspaper clipping appears on printed page 56, the pins are captioned Fig.17 on the next scanned page, and the axehead is captioned Fig.18. This is an internal numbering/scan discrepancy.

For source citations in CICAN records, use the descriptive caption and printed page, not a figure number alone. The previous Batch 199 report already notes the discrepancy; this batch reinforces that precaution.

## 6. What can be said about Dan Sherry's property

The clipping identifies Sherry's address as Church Road and says the new house was being built on the site of his mother's old house at Barrack Road. It also says he lived near the old church and round tower.

This is historical address context, not a surveyed archaeological findspot. It does not establish that:
- the axehead, pins and coins were all found at the same exact spot;
- the pins were found during the house construction;
- the coins were found in the same garden;
- the old house plot can be identified with a present-day property.

No modern address or coordinates should be inferred. Respect the privacy of any current residents and avoid attempting to identify a private property from the historical description.

## 7. Research required to close the lead

1. Locate the original *Drogheda Independent* issue containing “An historic Lusk find”; record date, issue, page and full article text.
2. Check whether the newspaper's original report names the earlier pin or coin discoveries in a longer passage not reproduced by King.
3. Consult MacGarry's full 1980 article to determine whether the three-pin find has a named finder, discovery date, exact context or ownership history.
4. Ask the National Museum of Ireland to compare the two Topographical Files pin entries (garden near round tower / monastic enclosure) and confirm whether they represent one group of three or separate finds.
5. Ask whether any Topographical Files entry or accession register mentions Dan Sherry, Barrack Road, bronze pins or coins.
6. Search NMI coin and stray-find records for Lusk and the Barrack Road / Church Road context, but do not match a coin solely by broad place-name.
7. If no independent coin record exists, preserve the coins only as a newspaper-reported lead with unknown number, type, date and custody.

## 10. Conclusion and next target

The newspaper establishes that Dan Sherry was reported to have found bronze pins and old coins on his property before the axehead discovery. It does not identify the number or type of pins, or any details about the coins.

The three bronze stick pins from Lusk are a separate, well-documented group of three. Their cottage-garden find context is compatible with Sherry's newspaper statement, but no source names him as their finder. The 2012 NMI Topographical Files summary itself contains two three-pin entries with different find-place descriptions; these may be duplicate or alternate descriptions, not evidence for six pins.

No Lusk coin group matching Sherry's “old coins” was identified in the sources examined. The coin reference remains an unverified documentary lead.

**Next research target:** investigate the Lusk clay tile recorded as National Museum number 1990:55 and the separate 1999:151–173 group of clay floor tiles, roof tiles, brick, baked-clay fragments and pottery from the surface of Lusk Graveyard. Establish whether these are part of the 1973/1975 medieval tile discoveries or separate later surface finds. Keep the museum numbers and contexts distinct.
