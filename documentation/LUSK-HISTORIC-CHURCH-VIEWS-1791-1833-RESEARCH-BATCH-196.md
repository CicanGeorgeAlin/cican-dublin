# CICAN Dublin Archive — Historic Views of Lusk Church, 1791–1833 Research Batch 196

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Identify and assess historic visual records of Lusk's old church before its replacement in 1847, with particular attention to whether the warrior carving described by Dalton can be recognised in any image.  
**Status:** Visual-source research only. No image of the warrior carving has been authenticated. No visual record, permanent object ID or production map pin created. The images document the church and its setting, not necessarily the small carved object.

## 1. Principal result — an earlier image source has been identified

The research found three relevant pre-1847 visual records:

1. **Francis Grose, “Lusk Ch.”, 1791 drawing** — National Library of Ireland (NLI), Grose Collection, call number PD 1976 TX 128. The NLI catalogue describes a pencil and ink-wash drawing dated 2 May 1791 and notes that it was engraved and published as plate 19 in *Antiquities of Ireland*. The image depicts the church and tower. This is the earliest identified visual record in this batch and predates Dalton's 1783 account? No: the drawing is dated 1791, eight years after Cooper's description and before the 1847 rebuilding.
2. **George Petrie / John Greig, “Lusk, Co. of Dublin”, 1821 print** — NLI, call number ET A401. NLI describes a view of cottages with Lusk Church in the background, engraved by John Greig from a drawing by George Petrie for *Excursions through Ireland*, published 1 April 1821. The church is background context rather than a close architectural elevation.
3. **Samuel Lover / Lewis, “Lusk Castle”, Irish Penny Magazine, 11 May 1833, p.144** — the engraving depicts Lusk Castle with the church buildings in the foreground. The visual source is by Samuel Lover, not George Petrie. The title is “Lusk Castle”; it should not be mislabelled as a close-up of the church. The drawing was published shortly before the old church was taken down.

These images are useful for architectural context and the church's pre-1847 appearance. None, in the available reproduction and catalogue detail, gives a sufficiently close or clearly identifiable view of the wall opposite the entrance to confirm Dalton's small warrior carving.

The National Library of Ireland catalogue records:

- Artist: Francis Grose (1731?–1791)
- Created: 2 May 1791
- Collection: Grose Collection (PD 1976 TX)
- Format: pencil and ink wash drawing
- Dimensions: 26.7 × 37 cm
- Call number: PD 1976 TX 128

The digitised image shows the medieval church and tower complex. It is an exterior architectural view. The reproduced view is not a close-up of the entrance wall and does not allow a small carved warrior figure to be securely recognised.

The NLI record and image are the best identified visual starting point for the old church as it appeared in the late eighteenth century.

## 3. NLI record B — George Petrie / John Greig, 1821

The NLI catalogue record “Lusk, Co. of Dublin” describes:
- A view of cottages with Lusk Church in the background.
- Engraved by John Greig (fl. 1801–1828) from a drawing by George Petrie (1790–1866).
- Published by Longman & Co., Paternoster Row, London, 1 April 1821.
- Format: line engraving.
- Image size: 6 × 9.4 cm; plate mark 10.2 × 15.3 cm; sheet 10.7 × 20.4 cm.
- Call number ET A401.
- Reproduction rights owned by the National Library of Ireland.

The drawing is associated with *Excursions through Ireland*. The NLI metadata identifies the subject as cottages with the church in the background. This is not a close-up view of the church or its entrance wall. The small print format also limits the likelihood that a small carved detail could be read with confidence.

**Date correction:** do not refer to this Lusk image as a 1819 drawing. The NLI record dates the Lusk print to 1 April 1821. Other George Petrie prints in the same series were issued in 1819, but the NLI record for Lusk gives 1821. The date “1819” should not be transferred from other Petrie works.

Source:
https://catalogue.nli.ie/Record/vtls000152372

## 4. Irish Penny Magazine — “Lusk Castle”, 11 May 1833

The 11 May 1833 issue of the *Irish Penny Magazine*, Vol. I, No. 19, includes an engraving titled **“Lusk Castle”** on p.144. The image caption attributes the drawing to Samuel Lover, Esq., R.H.A.; the engraving is signed “LEWIS” in the reproduced image.

The illustration depicts:
- Two prominent round castle/tower structures.
- A central fortified structure.
- A long low church building in the foreground.
- A pointed-arch window at one end of the church.
- Trees, boundary wall and surrounding landscape.

This image is frequently useful as a broad view of the Lusk ecclesiastical and fortified complex immediately before the 1847 church rebuilding. However:
- The title is “Lusk Castle”, not “Lusk Church”.
- The artist is Samuel Lover, not George Petrie.
- The image is an exterior general view, not a close-up of the old church's entrance wall.
- No small warrior carving can be confidently identified in the available image.

A separate prints-and-engravings index maintained by Dublin City Library and Archive records the Lusk image as *Irish Penny Magazine*, Vol. 1, 11 May 1833, p.144. The search result for the issue itself also describes the image and caption.

Sources:

## 6. What do the images tell us about the warrior carving?

Dalton's *History of County Dublin*, Chapter 20, describes a small carved warrior figure in the wall opposite the entrance to the old church. This batch has identified pre-1847 visual sources that show the church complex:
- Grose, 1791.
- Grose, c.1790–1800.
- Petrie/Greig, 1821.
- Lover/Lewis, 1833.

However, none of the accessible views provides a clear, authenticated close-up of the specific wall described by Dalton. The general architectural views cannot be used to prove the carving's presence or absence. A small stone may be hidden by distance, angle, vegetation, shadow or engraving simplification.

**Conclusion:** no visual confirmation of the warrior carving. Its existence remains supported by Dalton's written description, not by an authenticated image.

## 7. The 1847 architectural plan — a new comparison source

The Representative Church Body Library holds Joseph Welland's 1847 “Ecclesiastical Commission Design for Lusk Church. Diocese of Dublin. Ground Plan. 140 sittings”. The record identifies the drawing as an architectural plan, dated 1847, with internal arrangements and the medieval west tower and round tower included in the subject description.

This plan is important for understanding the transition from the old church to the new church. It is not a pre-1847 picture of the warrior carving, and a ground plan cannot identify a small carved figure in a wall unless the stone is specifically annotated. It should be compared with the old church's earlier layout, not treated as a visual record of the carving.

Source:
https://archdrawing.ireland.anglican.org/index.php/items/show/2837

## 8. Rights and image registration

The NLI catalogue explicitly states that reproduction rights for the Grose drawings and Petrie/Greig print are owned by the National Library of Ireland. Public online viewing does not automatically permit CICAN to republish the images.

The 1833 magazine engraving is a historical publication, but CICAN should still retain the exact publication details and verify the digitisation/reproduction conditions before copying the scan into the production site.

No image file was downloaded, copied into the repository or registered as a production visual asset in this batch. The report links the catalogue and publication records so that the archive preserves provenance without assuming reuse permission.

## 10. Duplicate and production controls

Repository search found no dedicated Batch 196 report or visual record for the historical views. The warrior itself remains a separate unlocated research object.

Before adding any visual record:
1. Restore and validate the complete canonical catalogue and visual registry.
2. Search existing image IDs and source records for all NLI and magazine records listed above.
3. Keep each distinct print/drawing as its own source record with its own creator, date, call number, rights and exact subject.
4. Do not treat a general view of the church as a photograph of the warrior.
5. Do not confuse Grose PD 1976 TX 106 with PD 1976 TX 128.
6. Do not transfer the 1819 date from other Petrie works to the Lusk print dated 1821.
7. Do not call the 1833 Lover/Lewis image a Petrie drawing.
8. Do not add any of these images to the warrior's visual record until the carving is clearly visible and provenance is established.

No permanent CICAN object ID, source-registry entry, visual record or production map pin created.

## 12. Conclusion and next steps

The research identifies a sequence of historic views of Lusk's old church and tower before the 1847 rebuilding. The strongest visual candidates are the 1791 Grose drawing, the separate c.1790–1800 Grose drawing, the 1821 Petrie/Greig village print, and the 1833 Lover/Lewis engraving titled “Lusk Castle”. A 1847 Welland ground plan documents the church rebuilding arrangement.

None of the accessible general views confirms Dalton's small warrior carving. The 1819 date previously mentioned in the research target should not be applied to the Lusk Petrie print: the NLI catalogue dates that Lusk print to 1 April 1821. The 1833 illustration is by Samuel Lover and titled “Lusk Castle”, not a Petrie church drawing.

The next useful step is to compare the 1791 and 1833 visual records at full resolution with the 1847 Welland plan and the known position of the old church entrance. If no carving can be positively identified, preserve the warrior as a text-only historical object with unknown survival and location.

**Next research target:** the cresset stone found at Lusk, now held in an OPW warehouse according to Nigel King's 1996 thesis. Investigate its form, dimensions, date, find context, current custody and whether a photograph or official collection record exists. Do not assume public display or visitor access.
