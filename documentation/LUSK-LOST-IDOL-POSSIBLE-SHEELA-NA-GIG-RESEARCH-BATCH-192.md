# CICAN Dublin Archive — The Lost Lusk “Idol” / Possible Sheela-na-gig Research Batch 192

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Research the lost carved figure formerly recorded inside Lusk Church, locally called “The Idol”; examine Austin Cooper's 1783 description, the later burial account, official monument designation, dating/classification confidence and the limits of locating it today.  
**Status:** Research-only historical object. Its present location is unknown. No production map pin, fabricated coordinates, permanent CICAN child ID, source-registry entry or visual record created while the canonical catalogue remains unavailable for complete validation.

## 1. Object identity and official reference

**Working name:** The Lusk “Idol” (possible Sheela-na-gig)  
**Object type:** Historical carved stone figure; possible sheela-na-gig  
**NMS / RMP reference:** DU008-010008-  
**Recorded context:** Inside the medieval church at Lusk, monument DU008-010004-  
**Present location:** Unknown; described in later sources as buried in or near the church complex. No verified recovery or excavation has been identified.

Fingal County Council's 2022 Appendix 6 lists DU008-010008- as “Sheela-na-gig” in Lusk. The 2019 archaeological impact assessment prepared by Rubicon Heritage Services for Fingal County Council describes the monument more cautiously as an **“unlocated possible sheela-na-gig”** formerly inside Lusk Church. It says the figure was described by Austin Cooper in 1783 and its current location is unknown.

The 2019 assessment gives the record high baseline value and records the RMP reference DU008-010008-. Its approximate ITM values (northing 721521, easting 754467) are site/context coordinates in the assessment table, not the object's surveyed location. Since the object is unlocated, these coordinates must not be represented as a precise pin for the carved stone.

## 2. Earliest known description — Austin Cooper, 1783

The antiquarian Austin Cooper (1759–1830) recorded a figure at Lusk in 1783. The description is reproduced in later scholarship from the *Journal of the Royal Society of Antiquaries of Ireland*, volume 44 (1914), p. 253, and cited by Edith Guest (1936, p. 111).

Cooper's description, as reproduced by Theresa Oakley (2004), is:
“the human features [are] fancifully hideous; the face being seven inches broad, and the head without neck or body, being attached to a pair of kneeling thighs and legs”.

The seven-inch figure is a reported face width in Cooper's description, not a full object measurement. No total height, depth, material, precise architectural position or drawing securely attributable to Cooper was identified in this research pass.

Oakley notes that Edith Guest incorrectly calls the antiquarian “Arthur” Cooper and that Guest did not consider Cooper's description sufficient to equate the figure with a sheela-na-gig. This is an important scholarly qualification: the modern classification as a possible sheela is not unanimous or certain.

Source:
- Theresa Catherine Oakley, *Lifting the Veil: A New Study of the Sheela-na-gigs of Britain and Ireland* (PhD thesis, University of Southampton, October 2004), p. 38, discussion of Austin Cooper: https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/465598/1/979332.pdf

## 3. The name “The Idol”

The figure was locally known as “The Idol”. The 2019 archaeological assessment attributes this name to Clibborn (1844, p. 575). The same account is repeated in later heritage profiles.

The name is a historical/local designation, not evidence that the figure was an object of formal worship or represented a deity. Avoid interpreting “Idol” as a confirmed religious function.

The Sheela-na-gig Project's discussion of names also notes the use of “The Idol” for the lost Lusk figure and refers to Austin Cooper's notes. It is a specialist research resource, but the primary description remains Cooper as reproduced in the academic literature.

Sources:
- Rubicon Heritage Services (2019), Appendix 1, CH004.
- The Sheela-na-gig Project, “Theories / The many names of sheela-na-gig”: https://sheelanagig.org/theories/

## 4. Removal and reported burial — 1843 or 1844?

The figure was removed from view and reportedly buried by the Reverend Mr Tyrrell.

The dates differ across the historical chain:
- Clibborn (1844, p. 575) is cited for the name “The Idol”.
- Edith Guest (1936, p. 111) is cited for the statement that Rev. Mr Tyrrell buried the figure in 1844.
- Theresa Oakley (2004, p. 111) assesses the event as having occurred at least a year earlier, in 1843 or possibly earlier.
- The 2019 Rubicon Heritage assessment repeats this qualification and states that the current location is unknown.
- Love Lusk's local heritage page says it was removed and buried by Rev. Mr Tyrrell at about 1843.

**Recommended archive wording:** “Reportedly removed and buried by the Rev. Mr Tyrrell around 1843–1844; the exact date and burial location are unverified.”

Do not state that it was definitely buried in 1844. Do not claim the figure was buried at a precise point within the graveyard or church. No archaeological excavation confirming the burial location or recovery of the object was identified.

- Oakley (2004), p. 111 and p. 38.

## 5. Is it definitely a sheela-na-gig?

No. The modern monument record classifies it as a sheela-na-gig, but the 2019 archaeological assessment explicitly calls it a **possible** sheela-na-gig, and Oakley records Guest's reservation about the identification.

Cooper's description is unusual: a head without neck or body attached to kneeling thighs and legs. This could be compatible with the broad category of exhibitionist figures, but Cooper's text alone is insufficient for a secure iconographic classification. The description does not clearly mention the defining anatomical display used in the National Monuments Service's class description.

The NMS *Monument Class and Scope Notes* describes a sheela-na-gig as a small carved figure of a naked female posed to display and emphasise the genitalia, typically found on Romanesque and later medieval churches and on defensive structures. The surviving description of the Lusk figure does not explicitly document those anatomical details.

**Recommended name:** “The Lusk ‘Idol’ (possible Sheela-na-gig)”. Preserve the NMS classification in a source-attributed field, but do not promote the identification to certainty in CICAN's own prose.

Sources:
- Oakley (2004), p. 38.
- National Monuments Service, *Monument Class and Scope Notes*, “Sheela-na-gig”: https://www.archaeology.ie/app/uploads/2025/03/monument-class-and-scope-notes-v1-1.pdf
- Rubicon Heritage Services (2019), Appendix 1, CH004.

## 6. Dating

No secure date for the carving was found. The official Fingal RMP appendix classifies it by monument type but does not provide a period in the list. The 2019 assessment calls it a possible sheela-na-gig and does not assign a construction date.

If it were confirmed as a sheela-na-gig, the NMS class notes give a broad comparative range of the twelfth to seventeenth centuries for the class. That generic class range cannot be used as a specific date for the missing Lusk object.

**Archive date:** Unknown.  
**Comparative context:** Broad medieval / later medieval class range only if the identification is confirmed.  
**Do not assign:** 12th century, 13th century, or any exact date.

## 7. Historical setting — Lusk Church

The 2019 archaeological assessment identifies the figure as having been inside Lusk Church, monument DU008-010004-. Its context is the historic ecclesiastical complex at Lusk, which includes the round tower, fifteenth-century square belfry, church and ecclesiastical enclosure.

The 2008 Fingal Historic Graveyards Project recounts that the medieval church was rededicated to the Blessed Virgin Mary after the Norman invasion and that the current Church of Ireland church dates to 1847. It also records that the earlier church was a large structure with two aisles and seven arches. This provides site context but does not identify the figure's precise position within the medieval church.

Do not claim it was mounted above a doorway, on the exterior, on a tower, or at a particular height. Cooper's description does not provide such a location, and the 2019 assessment says only “inside Lusk Church”.

Sources:
- Rubicon Heritage Services (2019), Appendix 1, CH004.
- Fingal Historic Graveyards Project, Volume 2, St MacCullin's Church, Lusk: https://www.fingal.ie/sites/default/files/2019-03/Fingal%20Historic%20Graveyards%20Project%20Volume%202.pdf

## 8. Coordinates and map treatment

The 2019 assessment table gives ITM_N 721521 and ITM_E 754467 for CH004. The table also gives an approximate distance from the proposed development site, but the entry explicitly describes the object as unlocated and says its current location is unknown.

The coordinates should therefore be understood as a site/context locator used in the planning assessment, not as the position of the buried figure. They must not be converted into a visitor pin or represented as a verified burial location.

**Map status:** no object pin. If a future map displays the historic association, it must map the Lusk church complex as the historical context and label the figure “unlocated / historical record”, not show a pin purporting to mark the object.

## 9. Distinction from other Lusk carved objects

This lost figure is separate from:
- The carved stone head high on the south-east corner of the fifteenth-century square tower (Batch 191; DU008-010010-).
- The separate Grace Dieu stone head in the Museum at Lusk, described in Nigel King's 1996 thesis.
- The octagonal limestone font in the belfry (Batch 188).
- The west-porch piscina (Batch 190).
- The bullaun stone at the modern Roman Catholic church entrance (Batch 189).
- The grave slabs and effigial tombs in the tower complex.

The fact that both the lost “Idol” and the surviving tower head are described as heads or head-like carvings does not make them the same object. Cooper describes a head attached to kneeling legs; the 1988 survey's surviving tower head is a separate carved architectural detail.

## 11. Protection and fieldwork cautions

Fingal's 2022 Appendix 6 explains that the Record of Monuments and Places is the statutory mechanism for protection and that works at or in relation to a recorded monument require advance notice under the National Monuments Acts. The Lusk figure's RMP number is DU008-010008-.

Because its location is unknown:
- Do not excavate, probe, metal-detect or disturb ground in an attempt to find it.
- Do not search private gardens or church property without express permission.
- Any future investigation must be designed and directed by a suitably qualified archaeologist and authorised by the relevant landowner and statutory bodies.
- Do not encourage visitors to search for the burial place.

## 14. Conclusion and next steps

The Lusk “Idol” is a documented but unlocated carved stone figure, recorded by Austin Cooper in 1783 and later reportedly buried by Rev. Mr Tyrrell around 1843–1844. Its RMP reference is DU008-010008-. The most defensible modern classification is **possible sheela-na-gig**, not a certain identification. Its present location and burial place are unknown, and no authenticated image has been identified.

CICAN should preserve the historical description, local name, source chain, date discrepancy and uncertainty. It should not map a fictional object location, imply a confirmed burial spot, or use an unrelated sheela-na-gig image.

**Next research target:** the Lusk obelisk / memorial associated with the Rev. Mr Tyrrell, including its inscription, erection date, historical purpose and whether the association with the removal of the “Idol” is documented. Keep the memorial and the lost figure as separate catalogue objects.
