# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Medieval Floor Tiles Research Batch 198

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Record the medieval floor-tile discoveries at Lusk, distinguish the 1973 mosaic fragment from the 1975 maintenance-work group, describe the identifiable designs, and trace published visual documentation and reported custody.  
**Status:** Strong historic documentation and several published drawings/photographic references. Present custody and current display status are not independently verified. No production catalogue entry or map pin created.

## 1. Principal finding — two discovery episodes must remain separate

Nigel King's 1996 thesis records two distinct episodes:

**A. 1973 — line-impressed mosaic fragment**
- Found among approximately 80 feet of debris removed from the belfry.
- King suggests the debris may have resulted from a deliberate clearance, probably in 1847, from the floor of the medieval church.
- The fragment was not found in its original floor position.
- King describes it as a seven-sided piece shaped like a concave-sided arm of a cross, with part of an encircled rosette.
- It measures 70 mm in length and 24 mm thick.

**B. 1975 — maintenance-work tile group**
- One line-impressed mosaic tile.
- Four line-impressed tiles.
- Two relief tiles.
- Total: seven finds as enumerated by King.
- The thesis does not provide a precise room, trench, floor level or exact point of recovery for this group.

Do not merge the 1973 fragment into the 1975 group or count the 1973 fragment as one of the seven without further primary documentation.

Source: Nigel King, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk* (NCAD, 1996), printed pp.61–67:
https://thesis.ncad.ie/T1727_The%20ecclesiastical%20buildings%20at%20Lusk_NC00210617.pdf

## 2. The 1973 mosaic fragment — unusually detailed description

King's description:
- Seven-sided, cross-arm-like shape with concave sides.
- Damaged on three of its seven sides.
- Length: 70 mm.
- Thickness: 24 mm.
- White bedding mortar survives on the underside.
- Line-impressed decoration includes two petals of an encircled rosette at the straight edge.
- The arm is outlined by one or two impressed lines.
- A tiny impressed circle marks the terminal.
- Pale whitish slip on the surface.
- Glaze described as greenish-yellow before firing.
- Stamped decoration cuts through the slip, leaving a dark brown design.
- Tile body is reddish-brown with small mica inclusions.

King compares the piece with line-impressed mosaic tiles from Llanfaes, Anglesey, Wales. He notes a similar cross-like form there but says the Lusk example is larger: 70 mm compared with 54 mm for the half-cross measurement of the Anglesey piece. He proposes a possible relationship in design tradition, but the surviving evidence does not establish a direct workshop, maker or direction of transfer.

King considers the Lusk fragment an important survival from a decorative panel, probably laid in the early fourteenth century. This is his interpretation, not an independently verified date for the fragment.

## 3. The 1975 maintenance-work group — four identifiable design references and two relief fragments

King lists the group as one line-impressed mosaic, four line-impressed tiles and two relief tiles.

The thesis illustrates or describes the following Lusk designs:

### 3.1 Lion rampant sinister in a quatrefoil
- King labels Fig.25 “Lion rampant sinister in 4-foil found at Lusk”.
- The drawing shows a heraldic-style lion facing sinister within a four-lobed quatrefoil frame.
- The thesis says the design is also found at Dublin Castle.
- The motif is a design description; it does not prove a personal or institutional heraldic association with Lusk.

### 3.2 Vine-scroll border
- Fig.26 is captioned “Vine scroll border. found at Lusk”.
- The drawing shows a curling vine with leaves and a lobed central element.
- King lists parallels at Christchurch Cathedral, St Audoen's, St Patrick's Cathedral, Swords Castle, Jerpoint Abbey, Kells Priory, Mellifont Abbey, Dunbrody Abbey and St Peter's Church, Waterford.

### 3.3 Four-foil in clockwise scroll
- Fig.27 is captioned “4-Foil in clockwise scroll found at Lusk”.
- The drawing shows a four-foil within a circular band and surrounding scrollwork.
- King lists parallels at Christ Church Cathedral, College Street, Dublin Castle, St Patrick's Cathedral, St Audoen's, St Begnet's Church, Swords Castle, Great Connell Priory, Mellifont Abbey and Waterford Deanery.
- The thesis also reproduces a photograph of an in-situ pavement at Mellifont Abbey as a comparison. That photograph is not a photograph of the Lusk tile.

### 3.4 Further line-impressed tile
- Fig.28 is captioned “Line impressed tile found at Lusk”.
- It shows an interlacing four-part design made from broad curved bands crossing around a central junction.
- King gives parallels at St Audoen's Church, Malahide Church and Great Connell Priory.

### 3.5 Two counter-relief fragments
- Fig.30 shows two irregular fragments captioned “Two Counter relief unidentifiable tile fragments found at Lusk”.
- Both have raised decoration against a depressed or textured ground.
- King cites Eames and Fanning (1988, p.133).
- Their exact pattern identities are not established in the thesis.

**Counting caution:** the 1975 group is enumerated as one mosaic, four line-impressed tiles and two relief tiles. The thesis's figure list and captions do not provide a fully transparent one-to-one mapping between each of the seven physical finds and every drawing. Do not assign the 1973 cross-arm fragment to one of the 1975 illustrations without a direct source link.

## 4. Dating and archaeological interpretation

King discusses line-impressed mosaic tiles as an early medieval floor-tile form in Ireland, with examples dating to the first half of the fourteenth century. He associates the Lusk mosaic fragment with a likely early-fourteenth-century decorative panel.

The 1975 group includes line-impressed tiles and relief tiles. King explains that relief decoration became popular around the sixteenth century and interprets the Lusk relief fragments as evidence for a later phase of floor furnishing.

These are broad typological interpretations. The thesis does not supply scientific dating, a stratified excavation context or a complete floor plan for the Lusk finds. The tiles were recovered out of their original floor arrangement, so their precise original positions and relationships cannot be reconstructed from this evidence alone.

The presence of multiple tile types supports King's interpretation that the medieval church had more than one phase or style of decorative floor furnishing. It does not establish that all tiles were laid at the same time.

## 5. Published specialist catalogue — Eames and Fanning, 1988

The National Library of Ireland catalogue confirms:
- Elizabeth S. Eames and Thomas Fanning, *Irish Medieval Tiles: Decorated Medieval Paving Tiles in Ireland with an Inventory of Sites and Designs and a Visual Index*.
- Published by the Royal Irish Academy, Dublin, 1988.
- 144 pages, illustrated, with maps.
- Royal Irish Academy Monographs in Archaeology, volume 2.
- NLI call number BB457, Main Reading Room.

King cites this work for the Lusk tile designs:
- p.119 for the lion, vine-scroll and four-foil motifs;
- p.123 for the further line-impressed tile;
- p.133 for the counter-relief fragments.

Google Books describes the 1988 volume as an inventory of 88 sites and a visual index of 505 designs. This is a major comparative reference for Irish medieval tiles.

## 6. Custody and display — what is and is not established

King's 1996 conclusion states that the OPW exhibition was housed in the belfry and that a number of finds from Lusk, including the medieval tiles, were kept by the OPW in one of its warehouses.

This is evidence of reported OPW custody in 1996. It does not establish:
- the present repository;
- an accession number;
- whether the tiles remain together;
- whether any are now on display;
- whether the 1973 and 1975 finds were catalogued as separate lots;
- whether all seven 1975 pieces were transferred to the same store.

No current OPW or National Museum record specifically identifying these Lusk tiles was located in this batch.

The 1996 thesis is a historical source for custody, not proof of present-day availability or public access.

## 7. Image and reproduction controls

The thesis includes:
- a drawing of the 1973 cross-arm-like mosaic fragment and a suggested reconstruction of its pattern;
- drawings of the lion-in-quatrefoil, vine-scroll, clockwise four-foil scroll and further line-impressed tile;
- drawings of the two counter-relief fragments;
- comparative images of pavements at Swords and Mellifont.

These are valuable visual documentation, but they are not all photographs of the Lusk artefacts. In particular, the Mellifont pavement photograph and Swords pavement photograph show comparison sites, not Lusk.

The thesis reproduces material credited to Eames and Fanning (1988), and the 1988 book is a separately copyrighted publication. Do not extract or republish the drawings and images into the production website until the relevant image rights are checked. Public access to the thesis PDF does not itself grant republication permission.

No image was copied into the repository or registered as a production visual asset in this batch.

## 8. Research leads still open

1. Consult Eames and Fanning, *Irish Medieval Tiles* (1988), especially pp.119, 123 and 133, to confirm exact inventory numbers and design catalogue references for each Lusk tile.
2. Request OPW confirmation of current custody, inventory/accession numbers, and whether the pieces can be viewed or photographed.
3. Check National Museum of Ireland object records and archive registers for Lusk medieval floor tiles, including possible older museum numbers.
4. Locate the original 1975 maintenance-work report or correspondence and establish the building area and circumstances of recovery.
5. Locate the 1973 belfry-debris clearance record and identify whether the fragment was retained by OPW, the National Museum or another custodian.
6. Compare the tile drawings against the Eames and Fanning visual index, preserving each pattern as a separate visual/source record.
7. Investigate whether later archaeological works at St MacCullin's Church produced additional medieval tile fragments, without automatically associating later finds with the 1973 or 1975 group.

## 11. Conclusion and next target

The Lusk medieval floor-tile evidence is richer than a generic note about fragments. King's thesis records a 1973 cross-arm-shaped line-impressed mosaic fragment with measurements and a detailed description of its glaze, fabric and decoration. It separately records seven tiles found during maintenance work in 1975, including a lion-in-quatrefoil, vine-scroll, clockwise four-foil scroll, a further line-impressed design and two unidentified counter-relief fragments.

The 1988 Eames and Fanning volume is the key specialist comparative catalogue. The thesis reports OPW warehouse custody in 1996, but present custody and display status remain unknown.

**Next research target:** the three bronze pins found at Lusk. King's thesis illustrates them (Fig.18) and gives a separate discussion. Investigate their forms, materials, measurements, find context, dating, museum record and present custody. Keep the pins distinct from each other and from the tile and cresset finds.
