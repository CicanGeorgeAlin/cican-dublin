# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Historic Warrior Figure in Church Wall Research Batch 195

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Investigate the small carved warrior figure described by Dalton in the wall opposite the entrance to Lusk's old church; establish the earliest source, location wording, iconographic description, possible survival and whether it is the same as the surviving belfry head or another Lusk carved object.  
**Status:** Historical research-only. No present-day survival, exact architectural position, date, material or identity established. No permanent CICAN child ID, source-registry entry, visual record or production map pin created while the canonical catalogue remains unavailable for complete validation.

## 1. Principal finding

Dalton's *History of County Dublin*, Chapter 20, Lusk, records a “curious stone” in the wall opposite the entrance, carved with “a small, but not disproportioned figure of a warrior”. This is a distinct statement immediately following his note that the lost Lusk “Idol” could no longer be found.

The source does not say the warrior figure was the Idol, part of the Idol, a tomb effigy, or a figure on the belfry. It is described as a separate stone in a wall of the old church.

No later archaeological survey, NIAH entry, conservation record or image located in this research pass securely identifies the same warrior carving. Nigel King's 1996 thesis on Lusk lists a carved head at Lusk and other finds, but a search of its text for “warrior” and “Dalton” returned no match. The 1988 County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey does not provide a matching warrior figure in its Lusk monument descriptions consulted.

**Archive decision:** retain this as a separate historical carved-stone object with an unverified current status. Do not merge it with the surviving carved head high on the south-east corner of the belfry (Batch 191), the missing Lusk “Idol” (Batch 192), or any tomb effigy.

## 2. Earliest located description — Dalton

The online transcription of Dalton's *History of County Dublin*, Chapter 20, “Lusk”, states:

“...but there is in the wall opposite the entrance, a curious stone, carved with a small, but not disproportioned figure of a warrior.”

This passage appears on the same page as Dalton's description of the old church's two aisles, seven arches, the 1753-discovered relief monument and the lost “Idol”. It follows the account of the Idol and is grammatically a separate observation: “This relic cannot, however, now be found; but there is ... a curious stone...”.

The wording establishes:
- A carved stone was observed by the author or source being transcribed.
- It was set in a wall opposite “the entrance”.
- The carving represented a small but proportionate warrior figure.
- The figure was described in the context of the old parish church at Lusk.

It does not establish:
- Whether the warrior was standing, seated, mounted or in combat.
- Whether the figure carried a weapon, shield or armour.
- Whether it was a relief, incised carving or part of a larger architectural block beyond the general phrase “carved”.
- The stone's material, dimensions, date, maker or original purpose.
- Which entrance was meant, or the compass direction of the wall.
- Whether the stone survived the rebuilding of the church in 1847.

Source:
- Dalton, *History of County Dublin*, Chapter 20, “Lusk”, lines 38–40 in the online transcription: https://www.chaptersofdublin.ie/Dalton/Lusk.htm

## 3. Historical building context and chronology

Dalton describes the old parish church as a two-aisled building divided by seven arches. He describes the warrior stone as being in a wall opposite the entrance. The old church was replaced in 1847 by the present Church of Ireland building, which was constructed against the medieval belfry.

The NIAH record for Saint MacCullin's Church, Lusk (Reg. 11323010), dates the present Church of Ireland building to 1847 (NIAH range 1845–1850). The old church's two aisles and internal walls were not retained as the same complete building. The medieval square tower/belfry and round tower survive as distinct parts of the wider complex.

This chronology creates an important uncertainty: Dalton's described wall belonged to the old church as he knew it before its replacement. The warrior stone may have been removed, reused, destroyed, or incorporated into surviving fabric. No source consulted here establishes which happened.

Do not assume the warrior carving is still in the same wall or even still on site.

## 4. Comparison with other Lusk carvings — separate identities

### A. The belfry south-east corner head (Batch 191)

The 1988 survey records a carved head high on the south-east corner of the fifteenth-century square tower. Nigel King's 1996 thesis also discusses and illustrates that head. It has a different published location and form from Dalton's warrior: a head set high on the belfry corner versus a small warrior figure carved on a wall opposite the entrance to the old church.

No evidence links them. Do not merge.

### B. The lost Lusk “Idol” / possible sheela-na-gig (Batch 192)

Austin Cooper described a head without neck or body attached to kneeling thighs and legs. It was locally called “The Idol” and later reportedly buried by Rev. Tyrrell. Dalton says the relic could no longer be found, then separately mentions the warrior stone. The warrior is therefore not the Idol in Dalton's text.

### C. The historic 1753-discovered relief (Batch 194)

Dalton describes a separate monument with Christ crucified, a bust holding a ball and cross, and an armorial shield with a bird. This was inserted into a stopped-up arch between the aisles. The warrior is described separately in the wall opposite the entrance. There is no evidence they are the same stone.

### D. Funerary effigies and tomb monuments

Lusk contains several carved tombs and effigies, including the Barnewall-Sharl table tomb and the James Bermingham slab. Dalton describes these separately and in detail. His “small ... figure of a warrior” is not identified as a tomb effigy or linked to either tomb. Do not assume the warrior is James Bermingham, Sir Christopher Barnewall or another named person.

The following later sources were checked as likely places to find the carving:

1. **Bradley & King, 1988 County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey:** the Lusk section records the carved head on the belfry, font, bullaun, fireplace plaque and funerary monuments, but no securely identifiable warrior figure was found in the descriptions consulted.
2. **Nigel King, 1996, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk*:** the thesis includes a chapter on objects found at Lusk and illustrations of the carved head, but text searches for “warrior” and “Dalton” did not find a matching entry. Absence from the searchable text does not prove the carving was not present or photographed.
3. **2019 Rubicon Heritage Services archaeological assessment for Fingal County Council:** no match for “warrior” was found in the searchable report text. This is a planning assessment, not a comprehensive object inventory.
4. **Fingal Heritage / NMS records:** no object-specific monument entry for a Lusk warrior carving was identified in the sources checked.

No authenticated photograph, drawing or measured record of Dalton's warrior figure was identified in this research pass. Do not use a generic medieval warrior carving, the James Bermingham tomb effigy or the Lusk belfry head as an image of this object.

## 7. Field and archival follow-up

Because the described wall belonged to the old church replaced in 1847, a field visit alone may not resolve the object's fate. The next steps should be archival:
1. Examine original editions and page images of Dalton's *History of County Dublin* to verify the exact passage and context.
2. Search the 1847 church construction records, parish vestry minutes, architect's drawings and stone-reuse records for salvaged medieval fabric.
3. Check historic drawings and photographs of the old church before demolition, especially G. Petrie's 1819 and 1833 views and the 1833 *Irish Penny Magazine* view discussed by Nigel King.
4. Examine those images at high resolution for a small carved figure in the wall opposite the entrance; do not claim the object is visible unless it can be positively identified.
5. Ask Lusk Community Council, the parish, Fingal Heritage Officer and local heritage researchers whether the stone is known or has been moved to a store, garden or collection.
6. If a candidate carving is identified, require a documented chain of provenance linking it to Dalton's warrior before creating an image or object record.
7. Do not probe walls, remove stonework, enter restricted areas or search private property without permission.

## 11. Conclusion and next steps

Dalton's account records a small carved warrior in a wall opposite the entrance to Lusk's old church. It is a separate object from the lost Idol, the 1753-discovered crucifixion relief, the belfry corner head and the known funerary effigies. No later source checked in this batch securely identifies it, and its fate after the 1847 rebuilding is unknown.

The evidence supports a historical research record only. The next priority is to inspect pre-1847 visual records of the old church and search parish / building archives for evidence that the carving was removed or reused.

**Next research target:** historic views of Lusk's old church by George Petrie (1819 and 1833) and the 1833 *Irish Penny Magazine* view. Assess what architectural features and carved objects are actually visible, and whether the warrior carving can be located in any image. Do not assume it is depicted.
