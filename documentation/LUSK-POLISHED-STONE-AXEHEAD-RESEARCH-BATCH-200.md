# CICAN Dublin Archive — Lusk Polished Stone Axehead Research Batch 200

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Reconcile the Lusk polished stone axehead references: the axe illustrated in Nigel King's 1996 thesis, the Dan Sherry newspaper discovery at Barrack Road, and two entries in the 2012 report's summary of National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files.  
**Status:** At least one Lusk polished stone axehead is securely documented and illustrated. The available records may refer to more than one find. The links between the newspaper discovery, the thesis illustration and the NMI entries are not proven. No permanent object ID or map pin created.

## 1. Principal result — do not merge the records yet

The research found three distinct documentary descriptions:

**Record A — the axehead illustrated by Nigel King (1996)**
- King says a stone axehead, thought to be made of Irish limestone, had been found at Lusk.
- The discussion appears under the heading “Other Finds (privately owned)”.
- Fig.18 is captioned “Stone Axehead found at Lusk. (National Museum)”.
- The illustration shows two views, broad face and side, with a 0–5 cm scale bar.
- King does not give a find date, exact findspot, individual dimensions or a museum accession number in the text visible in this section.

**Record B — Dan Sherry's axehead, reported in the Drogheda Independent**
- The newspaper clipping reproduced in King's thesis is headed “An historic Lusk find”.
- It says Dan Sherry discovered a polished Stone Age axehead while helping his son build a new house on the site of his mother's old house at Barrack Road, Lusk.
- Sherry is identified in the clipping as a pensioner and member of the Lusk Historical Society, with an address at Church Road.
- The clipping says he had previously found bronze pins and old coins on his property.
- The newspaper issue date and page are not legible in the reproduced extract available in the thesis.

**Record C — NMI Topographical Files entries reproduced in 2012**
The Greater Dublin Drainage cultural heritage assessment lists two separate Lusk polished stone axehead entries:
1. Museum number **1980:119**, find place “Topsoil in the ‘commons’”.
2. Museum number **Record only**, find place “Found in foundations of a thatched cottage 200 yards from the round tower at Lusk”.

The report does not state that these are the same object, nor does it link either entry to Dan Sherry or to King's Fig.18.

**Working conclusion:** the Barrack Road newspaper find may correspond to the cottage-foundation entry, but this is not proven. The 1980:119 “commons” entry should remain separate unless the original NMI files establish a connection. King's illustrated axehead also remains unlinked to a specific NMI record.

## 2. The 1996 thesis — form and visual record

Nigel King, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk* (NCAD, 1996), printed page 54, states:

“A stone axe head thought to have been made of Irish limestone has also been found.”

The paragraph is placed under “Other Finds (privately owned)”. This is important, but the subsequent illustration caption says “(National Museum)”. Those two statements are not fully reconciled by the thesis itself.

On printed page 57, Fig.18 shows two views of a polished stone axehead:
- a broad face with gently tapering sides and rounded ends;
- a narrow side view;
- a dotted or stippled surface convention in the drawing;
- a scale bar marked 0–5 cm.

The scale bar is a drawing reference. Do not infer exact dimensions from the scan without checking the original drawing scale and reproduction size.

The drawing is a visual record of an axehead attributed to Lusk. It cannot be securely identified as the Dan Sherry object or either NMI Topographical Files entry on the evidence currently available.

## 3. Dan Sherry and the Barrack Road discovery

King reproduces an extract from the *Drogheda Independent* titled “An historic Lusk find”.

The extract states that:
- a “stone age axe head” had been discovered by a Lusk man;
- the finder was Dan Sherry, described as a pensioner and member of the Lusk Historical Society;
- Sherry was helping his son construct a new house;
- the house was being built on the site of Sherry's mother's old house at Barrack Road, Lusk;
- the stone was “beautifully polished”;
- Sherry had also found bronze pins and old coins on his property in the past.

The clipping includes a photograph of Sherry holding the axehead.

**Date limitation:** the thesis extract does not show a reliably readable publication date, issue number or page number. The newspaper article should not be assigned a year without locating the original issue.

**Findspot limitation:** Barrack Road is a named street, but the newspaper does not provide a house number or surveyed coordinates. Do not geolocate the discovery to a present-day property based on the old house description.

## 4. NMI Topographical Files — two separate axehead records

The 2012 Greater Dublin Drainage cultural heritage assessment includes Appendix 6.14.3, described as NMI Topographical Files.

It records:

| Museum number | Find | Find place | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1980:119 | Polished stone axehead | Topsoil in the “commons” | Polished stone axehead |
| Record only | Polished stone axehead | Foundations of a thatched cottage 200 yards from Lusk round tower | Polished stone axehead |

These entries should be treated as two records in the published summary. The second has no museum number and is described as “Record only”.

The cottage-foundation description has a possible contextual similarity to the newspaper story of a new house built on the site of a thatched cottage / Sherry's mother's old house. However, the published sources do not explicitly make this identification.

The “commons” find is a different recorded find context. It may be a separate axehead, but it is also possible that the NMI files contain overlapping or duplicated legacy reports. Only the original topographical files can resolve this.

## 6. Relationship to the bronze pins

The newspaper clipping says Sherry had previously found bronze pins and old coins on his property. This is an important lead, but it does not establish that these were the three bronze stick pins discussed in the previous batch.

The three-pin group is described in King's thesis as found in a cottage garden approximately 100 yards from the church. The newspaper says Sherry had found bronze pins and coins on his property. The two descriptions could relate to the same finds, but there is no explicit identification in the sources examined.

Do not merge the pins with Sherry's property or the axehead record without corroborating evidence.

## 7. Custody and ownership — unresolved

The thesis text places the axehead under “Other Finds (privately owned)”, but the illustration caption credits the National Museum. This may mean the Museum supplied the illustration, held a photograph or held a record; it does not by itself prove ownership of the physical object.

The 2012 NMI Topographical Files table gives a museum number for the “commons” axehead (1980:119), but the cottage-foundation axehead is “Record only”. The table does not state the current physical custody of either object.

Therefore:
- do not claim the Sherry axehead is currently in the National Museum;
- do not claim the 1980:119 object is the one in King's illustration;
- do not claim the “record only” axehead is the Sherry find;
- do not state that any of the axeheads is on public display.

## 11. Conclusion and next target

The Lusk polished stone axehead evidence cannot yet be reduced to one securely identified object. Nigel King's thesis illustrates a polished axehead, tentatively thought to be Irish limestone, and reproduces a newspaper photograph of Dan Sherry holding a polished Stone Age axehead discovered during house construction at Barrack Road. Separately, a 2012 report reproducing NMI Topographical Files lists two Lusk polished stone axehead records: one registered as 1980:119 from topsoil in the “commons”, and another “record only” find from the foundations of a thatched cottage 200 yards from the round tower.

The cottage-foundation description may correspond to the Sherry/Barrack Road story, but that identification is not proven. The “commons” record may be a separate find. The thesis illustration's National Museum credit and “privately owned” text also require reconciliation.

**Next research target:** Lusk's prehistoric polished stone axehead records should remain open until NMI file cross-referencing is complete. To keep the catalogue moving without inventing certainty, the next separate target will be the Lusk bronze and old-coin finds mentioned in the Dan Sherry newspaper clipping. Investigate whether the coins and pins are independently recorded, whether they can be linked to a dated report or collection record, and whether any of them are distinct from the already documented three bronze pins.
