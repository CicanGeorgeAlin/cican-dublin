# CICAN Dublin Archive — Six Unclassifiable Baked-Clay Pieces from Lusk Graveyard Research Batch 204

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Investigate the six unclassifiable baked-clay pieces listed within National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files range 1999:151–173, recorded from the surface of Lusk Graveyard; test whether later summaries or nearby excavations clarify their material, function or date.  
**Status:** Six pieces are explicitly recorded as unclassifiable baked clay in a 2012 Topographical Files summary. No diagnostic form, function, date, image, individual registration number or exact findspot is supplied. A later 2021 heritage assessment compresses the wider 1999 group into a broad medieval stray-find entry but does not discuss the six pieces separately. No production object record or map pin created.

## 1. Principal finding — the source deliberately leaves the pieces unclassified

The 2012 Greater Dublin Drainage cultural heritage assessment reproduces an NMI Topographical Files entry for museum-number range **1999:151–173**, Lusk.

The entry's find field lists:
- clay floor tiles;
- roof tiles;
- brick;
- baked-clay fragments;
- three pottery sherds.

The description field specifies:
- clay floor tiles;
- roof tiles;
- brick;
- **six unclassifiable pieces of baked clay**;
- glazed pottery sherds.

The source does not call these six pieces daub, kiln lining, furnace lining, pottery, tile, brick, crucible, mould, hearth material or industrial waste. The adjective “unclassifiable” is a clear warning not to assign a functional type from the summary alone.

The only defensible object description is:
**Six pieces of baked clay, explicitly described as unclassifiable, from a mixed surface-find collection at Lusk Graveyard.**

## 2. Museum-number range does not equal six object numbers

The published Topographical Files summary assigns the mixed group the range 1999:151–173. It does not itemise which number corresponds to each tile, brick, pottery sherd or baked-clay fragment.

The range covers multiple material categories. It must not be interpreted as:
- 23 baked-clay objects;
- six individually numbered objects;
- six objects all recovered together in one sealed deposit;
- six pieces from one original artefact.

The source provides a count of six for the unclassifiable baked-clay category only. It does not provide individual object-level numbers or prove whether any of the six fragments join together.

## 3. Find context — surface at Lusk Graveyard

The reported find place is “Found on surface at Lusk Graveyard”.

This does not identify:
- a grave plot or grave number;
- a church wall or building;
- a floor or roof collapse;
- a kiln or hearth;
- a stratified deposit;
- a precise point within the graveyard;
- the circumstances or date of collection.

Surface material can be displaced, redeposited or mixed from different episodes. The six pieces cannot be assumed to derive from the medieval church merely because the wider group includes floor tiles and roof tiles.

The National Museum Topographical Files are described in the same 2012 report as the national archive of known finds recorded by the Museum. The report's appendix is a secondary summary, not the original item-level documentation.

## 4. A later 2021 heritage assessment — useful but not a specialist re-identification

A 2021 archaeological heritage assessment for proposed development at Kilhedge Lane, Lusk, reproduces a shorter list of National Museum Topographical Files stray finds from Lusk.

It includes the record **1999:151173**, described as “Glazed pottery, brick floor & roof tiles”, with findspot “Lusk” and period “Medieval”.

The 2021 table does not mention the six unclassifiable baked-clay pieces, does not repeat the graveyard surface context, and does not give an object-by-object inventory. It therefore appears to be a compressed summary of the wider museum-number range, not a fresh examination or reclassification of the six fragments.

The period “Medieval” is attached to the condensed 1999:151173 record as a whole. It cannot safely be transferred to every material component, particularly the six fragments that the earlier detailed table left unclassified.

**Interpretive limit:** the 2021 assessment supports a broad medieval attribution for the mixed Lusk stray-find entry as summarised by that report. It does not establish that the six baked-clay fragments themselves are medieval or that they belong to one medieval building.

## 5. Can they be daub, kiln lining or building material?

Those are possible archaeological categories for baked clay in general, but no evidence in the Lusk Topographical Files summary allows one to be selected.

To identify daub, an analyst might look for impressions of wattle, timber, straw or other construction materials, as well as fabric and firing characteristics. Kiln or furnace lining may preserve vitrification, intense heat alteration, slag adhesion or a curved/structural surface. Tile and brick fragments may preserve regular thickness, planar faces, moulding, glaze or other diagnostic features. None of these observations is supplied for the six Lusk pieces.

Accordingly:
- **Daub:** not demonstrated.
- **Kiln or furnace lining:** not demonstrated.
- **Hearth lining:** not demonstrated.
- **Pottery fragments:** not demonstrated; the source separately lists pottery sherds.
- **Tile or brick fragments:** not demonstrated; the source separately lists those categories.
- **Industrial waste:** not demonstrated.

The six pieces should remain unclassified until the objects or original museum documentation can be examined.

## 6. Nearby Lusk evidence for burning and kilns — not a match

Archaeological investigations elsewhere in Lusk have recorded burnt material and possible kiln-related features. For example, the 2002 excavation record for Lusk (Excavations.ie 2002:0623, licence 02E0794) describes a charcoal-rich pit and a possible figure-of-eight-shaped kiln north of the medieval town.

This establishes that archaeologists have recorded prehistoric activity involving burnt deposits and a possible kiln in the wider Lusk landscape. It does **not** connect those features to the six baked-clay fragments found on the surface at Lusk Graveyard.

The finds are from different documentary contexts and must not be merged.

Source:
https://excavations.ie/report/2002/Dublin/0007939/

## 7. Distinguish from the other Lusk clay and tile records

The six unclassifiable baked-clay pieces are one component of the 1999:151–173 mixed surface collection. Keep them separate from:

- **1990:55:** one generic clay tile from Lusk, findspot unknown.
- **1999:151–173 floor tiles:** described separately in the same group.
- **1999:151–173 roof tiles:** described separately in the same group.
- **1999:151–173 brick:** described separately in the same group.
- **1999:151–173 pottery:** three sherds in the find field; described as glazed pottery in the description field.
- **1973:** one line-impressed mosaic fragment from debris removed from the belfry.
- **1975:** seven tiles found during maintenance work, as described by Nigel King.
- **2004:** two 15th-century floor-tile fragments found in made ground containing demolition material from the medieval church.

The 2004 Excavations.ie report states that the two floor-tile fragments were found in made ground derived mainly from demolition of the medieval church and dates them to the 15th century. It does not refer to the six baked-clay pieces or the NMI museum-number range.

## 8. Visual record and custody

No authenticated photograph, drawing, measurement or individual inventory description for the six fragments was identified in this batch.

The 2012 summary attributes the information to NMI Topographical Files but does not establish the present physical repository, current owner, display status or whether the six fragments remain together.

A request to the National Museum should ask for:
1. the itemised records within 1999:151–173;
2. the individual identifiers assigned to the six baked-clay fragments, if any;
3. original photographs or drawings;
4. measurements, weight, fabric description and colour;
5. any notes on surface texture, inclusions, vitrification, impressions or curvature;
6. whether the fragments were examined by a ceramic or archaeometallurgical specialist;
7. the exact collection circumstances and any field notes;
8. present custody and conditions for researcher access.

Do not use generic images of daub, kiln lining or medieval building material as images of the Lusk objects.

## 12. Conclusion and next target

The six pieces are not presently identifiable beyond the source's own cautious description: **unclassifiable baked clay**. Their find context is the surface of Lusk Graveyard, within a mixed Topographical Files range that also includes floor tiles, roof tiles, brick and pottery. No evidence identifies them as daub, kiln lining, furnace lining, hearth material or any other specific category.

A 2021 heritage assessment compresses the wider NMI range into a medieval stray-find entry described as glazed pottery, brick, floor and roof tiles. It omits the six fragments and does not provide a separate date for them. That secondary summary cannot resolve their identity.

The correct next step is not to guess at their function. It is to seek the original NMI item-level records and images.

**Next research target:** investigate the Lusk stone ball recorded as NMI 1999:41. Establish its material, dimensions, shape, possible function, find context, date, image evidence and whether the later Lusk heritage assessments add any description. Keep it separate from the stone rubber recorded as 1999:43 unless the evidence establishes a relationship.
