# CICAN Dublin Archive — Three Bronze Pins from Lusk Research Batch 199

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Establish the published evidence for three bronze stick pins found in Lusk; compare the 1980 specialist note, 1988 county survey, 1996 thesis and later National Museum topographical-file summary; document form, dating, find context, image evidence and custody.  
**Status:** Three-pin find securely documented in several publications. The original 1980 specialist note is identified, but its full text was not accessible in the sources examined for this batch. Present custody and individual measurements remain unverified. No production catalogue entries or map pins created.

## 1. Principal finding

Three bronze pins were found in a cottage garden at Lusk, approximately 100 yards from the church according to Nigel King's 1996 thesis. The thesis describes them as dating to the tenth and twelfth centuries and labels them “privately owned”. It reproduces a drawing captioned “Three Bronze pin found at Lusk. (National Museum)” with a scale marked 0–2 cm.

The 1988 *County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey* records the find as “Three bronze stick pins”, found in a cottage garden at Lusk between 1920 and 1950, and cites Damian C. MacGarry's 1980 note in the *Journal of the Royal Society of Antiquaries of Ireland*, volume 110, pages 153–155.

A later 2012 cultural heritage assessment, reproducing National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files information, describes the find as three decorated dome-headed bronze stick pins from surface soil in a garden near the round tower. It does not supply a museum number.

The combined evidence supports a genuine group of three pins from the Lusk ecclesiastical settlement area. It does not establish an exact garden, a precise find date, individual dimensions, or present repository.

## 2. The primary specialist publication

Damian C. MacGarry, “Three Bronze Pins from Lusk, County Dublin”, was published in the *Journal of the Royal Society of Antiquaries of Ireland*, volume 110 (1980), pages 153–155, within the Miscellanea section.

JSTOR's issue contents confirm the title, author and page range. The article is therefore the key specialist publication to consult in full before any detailed object-level description or dating is finalised.

The full text was not accessible in the web records examined for this batch. No unseen details from MacGarry's three-page note are inferred here.

## 3. What the 1996 thesis records

Nigel King, *The Ecclesiastical Buildings at Lusk* (NCAD, 1996), printed page 54, places the pins under “Other Finds (privately owned)”.

King states:
- Three bronze pins.
- Found in the garden of a cottage.
- Approximately one hundred yards from the church.
- Dating expressed as “the tenth and twelfth century” in the thesis.
- The pins are illustrated as Fig.17 in the scanned thesis (the list of illustrations calls the same group Fig.18, an internal numbering discrepancy).

The thesis illustration is labelled “Three Bronze pin found at Lusk. (National Museum)”. It depicts three upright, long, narrow pins with tapered shafts and rounded or dome-like heads. The drawing also shows a separate rounded element above each pin head; the scan does not explain whether these represent detached components, a reconstruction convention or a drawing error. The illustration includes scale bars marked 0–2 cm.

**Important:** the 1996 text's “tenth and twelfth century” wording is ambiguous. It may mean a range or may be a typographical error. Do not silently convert it into a precise “10th–12th century” date unless the 1980 MacGarry note confirms that reading.

## 4. The 1988 county survey

Bradley and King, *County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey* (1988), p.268, lists:
“Three bronze stick pins. Found in the garden of a cottage at Lusk between 1920 and 1950. JRSAI 110 (1980), 153–5.”

This is important independent confirmation that:
- the find was understood as a group of three;
- the objects were stick pins;
- the discovery was in a cottage garden;
- the reported discovery period was between 1920 and 1950;
- the specialist publication was MacGarry (1980).

The date range 1920–1950 is broad and does not give a year. The 1988 survey does not supply a cottage address or precise coordinates.

Source:
https://www.archaeology.ie/app/uploads/2025/05/County_Dublin_Urban_Archaeology_Survey_1988-1.pdf

## 5. National Museum Topographical Files summary (published in 2012)

The Greater Dublin Drainage Phase Two cultural heritage assessment (May 2012), Appendix 6.14.3, reproduces entries from the National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files.

For Lusk, it records:
- Find: “3 Bronze stick pins”.
- Find place: surface soil of a garden near the round tower.
- Description: “Decorated dome headed pins”.
- Museum number: “Record only” (no individual accession number supplied in the table).

This is a valuable later summary of the NMI topographical-file record. It supports the decorated dome-headed description and places the garden near the round tower, but it does not identify the precise property, exact distance from the church or current custody.

A separate entry in the same appendix records three bronze stick pins with “Monastic enclosure” as find place and no museum number. It may be a generalised site attribution for the same group, but the table does not prove that this is a separate find. Do not count it as a second group of three.

## 6. Form and visual evidence

The 1996 thesis drawing is the accessible visual record identified in this batch. It shows three separate pins, each with a long, slender shaft and a rounded head. The right-hand example appears more textured than the other two in the drawing.

The later Topographical Files summary calls them “decorated dome headed pins”. This is the strongest available wording for their head form and decoration in the sources examined.

However, the available evidence does not establish:
- exact head decoration or motif for each pin;
- whether all three are complete;
- exact shaft lengths or head diameters;
- whether the separate rounded forms drawn above each head represent detached parts;
- metal composition beyond the published term “bronze”;
- manufacturing technique;
- individual typological classification.

The thesis's 0–2 cm scale bar appears to be a scale reference for the drawing, but without a reliably accessible original image measurement and confirmation of reproduction scale it should not be used to derive individual dimensions.

Do not create separate detailed object descriptions from the drawing alone.

## 7. Dating — retain the discrepancy

Two dating statements are present:
- King (1996): “the tenth and twelfth century” (ambiguous wording).
- The Topographical Files summary in the 2012 assessment does not provide a date in the table.
- The 1988 survey gives the discovery period, 1920–1950, not an archaeological date.

The tenth–twelfth-century dating should therefore be treated as King's published interpretation, pending verification against MacGarry (1980). Do not state that all three pins have been independently dated to a precise century or that they necessarily represent one single deposit.

## 8. Find context and location confidence

The source descriptions vary in precision:
- King (1996): cottage garden, approximately 100 yards from the church.
- Bradley and King (1988): garden of a cottage at Lusk; found between 1920 and 1950.
- NMI Topographical Files summary (as reproduced in 2012): surface soil of a garden near the round tower.
- Another NMI-file summary line gives “Monastic enclosure” as the find place, with no museum number.

These descriptions are compatible with a garden in the vicinity of the Lusk ecclesiastical enclosure, but they do not identify the same exact plot with certainty. The cottage is not named and the findspot is not surveyed to coordinates.

**Location decision:** townland/settlement-level association only. No point coordinate or map pin should be assigned to a particular garden.

## 9. Custody and ownership

King's 1996 thesis places the pins under “Other Finds (privately owned)”. The drawing caption credits “National Museum”, but that credit may identify the source of the illustration or record and does not by itself establish that the physical objects were then in the National Museum collection.

The 2012 appendix records no accession number and does not confirm present custody.

The evidence therefore leaves open whether:
- the three objects remained privately owned;
- they were later deposited or transferred to the National Museum;
- the National Museum holds a topographical-file record or photograph without holding the objects;
- the three pins have since been separated.

No current repository, accession number, owner, public display status or access arrangement has been verified. Do not describe them as currently held by the National Museum or OPW.

## 10. Related finds — keep separate

The bronze pins should not be merged with:
- the Lusk cresset stone, reported found during church maintenance work;
- the medieval floor tiles found in 1973 and 1975;
- the stone axehead found in Lusk;
- the church bullaun stone;
- the carved stone head high on the belfry;
- the lost “Idol” or warrior carving described by Dalton.

The 1996 thesis discusses the pins and axehead in the same “Other Finds” section, but this does not establish that they were found together or by the same person.

## 11. Research still required

1. Obtain and read MacGarry's full 1980 note (JRSAI 110, pp.153–155) to verify the original discovery account, typology, exact dating, individual descriptions and any illustrations.
2. Request a search of the National Museum of Ireland Topographical Files under Lusk, bronze pins, decorated dome-headed pins and the 1920–1950 cottage-garden discovery.
3. Ask whether the Topographical Files hold original photographs, drawings, correspondence or an accession cross-reference.
4. Determine whether the objects were ever accessioned by the National Museum or remained privately owned.
5. Check whether the three pins remain together and whether they can be photographed with a scale.
6. Verify the ambiguous dating wording in King's thesis against MacGarry's original article.
7. Avoid contacting or identifying a private owner based only on a historic cottage-garden find description.

## 12. Duplicate and production controls

Repository search found no existing dedicated Batch 199 report.

The canonical catalogue and visual registry must be restored and validated before any permanent object or image IDs are allocated. If later registered, the group should have one group-level discovery record linked to three individual object records only if the physical objects can be securely distinguished and their individual identities verified.

## 14. Conclusion and next target

The three Lusk bronze pins are a securely documented archaeological stray-find group. Their cottage-garden discovery is recorded in the 1988 county survey, the 1996 thesis includes an illustration, and a later report reproducing National Museum topographical-file information calls them decorated dome-headed pins from surface soil in a garden near the round tower.

The strongest unresolved issue is custody: King's thesis calls them privately owned, while its figure caption credits the National Museum; the later topographical-file summary gives no accession number. The original 1980 MacGarry note is the essential next source for resolving the form and dating, but its full text was not accessible in this batch.

**Next research target:** the Lusk polished stone axehead, reported from the foundations of a thatched cottage about 200 yards from the round tower and separately associated with Dan Sherry's discovery near Barrack Road. Investigate the apparent multiple discovery descriptions, the NMI record numbers, material identification, dimensions, date, original newspaper report and present custody. Do not assume these references all describe the same axehead until the evidence is reconciled.
