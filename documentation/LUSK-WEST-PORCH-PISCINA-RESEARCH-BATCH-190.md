**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Research the small piscina recorded in the west entrance porch of the 1847 Church of Ireland church at Lusk, and assess the claim that it may have been salvaged from the medieval parish church.  
**Status:** Research-only. Existing parent DUB-BLD-0177 — Saint MacCullin's Church, Lusk — remains unchanged. No permanent child ID, source-registry entry, visual record or production map pin created because the complete canonical catalogue remains unavailable for validation.

## 1. Object identity and building distinction

**Working name:** Lusk West-Porch Piscina  
**Object type:** Piscina (stone liturgical basin / fitting), as identified by the 1988 County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey.  
**Published location:** West entrance porch of the church described in the Lusk survey.  
**Parent:** DUB-BLD-0177 — Saint MacCullin's Church, Lusk.

An important site distinction is necessary. The surviving church building discussed in the 1988 survey is the Church of Ireland church built in 1847, attached to the fifteenth-century square tower and the earlier round tower. The NIAH record 11323010 describes this church as built in 1847 and now in use as a heritage/interpretative centre. It is not the modern Roman Catholic church at which the separate granite bullaun was recorded in Batches 148 and 189.

The piscina must therefore not be placed in the modern Catholic church or conflated with the font and bullaun objects recorded inside the belfry.

## 2. Primary description and provenance qualification

Bradley & King's 1988 survey states that the present church was constructed in 1847 and that the fifteenth-century west tower is the principal surviving part of the medieval building. It then records: “In the west entrance porch of the church is a small piscina which may have been salvaged from the medieval church.”

This gives us three defensible facts:
1. The survey identified a small feature as a piscina.
2. It located it in the west entrance porch of the church.
3. It considered salvage from the medieval church possible.

The word “may” is essential. The survey does not state that the object definitely came from the medieval church. It gives no original position, date, donor, maker, dimensions, material, decorative description or account of how it was moved.

The Fingal Historic Graveyards Project independently summarises the 1847 Church of Ireland building as containing earlier architectural features, including a **possible medieval piscina**, citing Bradley (1988, p.263). This is useful corroboration that the feature was understood as potentially medieval, but it appears to derive from the same archaeological survey rather than a separate physical examination.

Recommended wording:
“Small piscina recorded in the west entrance porch of the 1847 Church of Ireland church at Lusk. Bradley & King (1988) suggest it may have been salvaged from the medieval church; this provenance is unconfirmed.”

Sources:
- Bradley & King (1988), pp. 263–264.
- Fingal Historic Graveyards Project, Volume 2, FHG 25, historical context section.

## 3. What is a piscina? Functional interpretation

A piscina is a liturgical basin associated with the washing of the priest's hands and/or sacred vessels during the Mass. Medieval examples were commonly integrated into church fabric, often in a recess near the altar, with drainage arranged so that water passed into the ground.

A Trinity College Dublin research publication on Irish medieval stone sculpture discusses piscinae as stone fittings associated with the washing of vessels after Communion and notes their development as architectural features in Irish churches. The exact form and location varied.

This general definition explains the object type; it does not establish the Lusk example's original use, design or precise date. The Lusk survey does not describe a surviving basin, drain hole, niche, moulding or architectural setting in enough detail to independently test the classification.

Do not call it a holy-water stoup. A stoup is a different type of basin, normally intended to contain holy water for worshippers near an entrance. Do not call it a font or bullaun.

## 4. Date and material — not established

The 1988 survey does not provide a date or material for the Lusk piscina. Its possible salvage from the medieval church does not prove that it was made during the medieval period: it could be older, contemporary with the medieval church, or a later object reused in that building.

The 2008 graveyard survey calls it a “possible medieval piscina”, but this is a qualified attribution and appears to rely on the 1988 survey.

Archive fields should therefore read:
- **Material:** Not specified in the consulted sources.
- **Date:** Unknown.
- **Possible association:** Medieval church, unconfirmed.
- **Present recorded setting:** West entrance porch of the 1847 Church of Ireland church.
- **Maker / donor:** Unknown.
- **Original position:** Unknown.
- **Movement history:** Possible salvage/reuse, not proven.

Do not assign a medieval date solely from the word piscina or from the building's earlier history.

## 5. Building chronology and setting

The 1988 survey records that the medieval parish church survived until 1847, when the present church was constructed. Historical descriptions cited in the survey describe the medieval church as a large two-aisled building. The present Church of Ireland building dates to 1847 and is attached to the fifteenth-century square tower and earlier round tower.

The NIAH record 11323010 identifies the church as:
- Built 1847 (NIAH date range 1845–1850).
- Church of Ireland / Ecclesiastical Commissioners' church.
- Formerly a church, now recorded as a heritage/interpretative centre.
- Recorded by NIAH on 2 August 2000.
- NIAH coordinates: 321593, 254442 (retain source CRS; do not treat as the piscina's exact location).

The 2008 Fingal Historic Graveyards Project identifies the current Church of Ireland church as dating to 1847 and notes earlier architectural features, including the possible piscina. The church building's NIAH coordinates are building-level coordinates, not an object coordinate.

## 7. Relationship to other Lusk objects

Keep the following features separate:
- **Lusk Octagonal Limestone Font (Batch 188):** limestone, octagonal, with pedestal and trefoil-headed recessed panels; recorded in the ground floor of the square belfry.
- **Lusk Church-Entrance Bullaun (Batch 189):** granite stone recorded at the modern Roman Catholic church entrance; distinct from the missing well-associated stone.
- **Bremore Annunciation Fireplace Plaque (Batch 187):** limestone fireplace fragment associated with Bremore Castle and recorded in the belfry assemblage.
- **Lusk West-Porch Piscina (Batch 190):** small piscina in the west entrance porch of the 1847 Church of Ireland church; possible medieval salvage, unconfirmed.

The fact that the font, bullaun and fireplace fragment were recorded inside the belfry does not mean that the piscina is one of those objects or was located in the same room. The survey gives it a different location: the west entrance porch.

## 8. Photography and field-verification brief

If access is granted and the feature is still present:
1. Photograph the full west entrance porch to establish context.
2. Photograph the piscina from directly in front, including its relationship to the wall, doorway and floor.
3. Photograph the basin interior and any drainage aperture only from a permitted angle.
4. Record the outline, dimensions and material visually; do not assume stone type from colour alone.
5. Record any mouldings, carving, tooling, repairs, cracks or losses without interpreting them beyond what is visible.
6. Photograph any joints or insertion seams that may help assess whether it was reused, without touching or probing the fabric.
7. Record date, photographer, access permission and exact location within the porch.
8. Compare the field images with any historical photograph or drawing located in future research.

Do not remove coverings, move furniture, enter closed areas, touch the object, use chalk/rubbing, or place a ruler against the stone. If photography is not permitted, record the restriction and do not attempt to obtain an image.

## 11. Conclusion and next steps

A small piscina is documented in the west entrance porch of Lusk's 1847 Church of Ireland church. Both the 1988 archaeological survey and Fingal's 2008 graveyard survey allow for a medieval origin, but neither proves it. Material, dimensions, date, maker, original location and present condition remain unknown.

The archive should preserve the exact qualified statement: **“may have been salvaged from the medieval church.”** It should not silently turn that possibility into a confirmed medieval object or move the feature to the Catholic church or the belfry.

Next research target: the carved stone head high on the south-east corner of Lusk's fifteenth-century square tower, recorded in the 1988 survey (p. 263). Its description, position, survival and any photographic or conservation records should be checked before assigning a child record.
