# CICAN Dublin Archive — St Andrew's Malahide & Malahide Demesne (Batch 168)

**Status:** Evidence-led research report saved; no permanent archive IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.  
**Scope:** Fingal Historic Graveyards Project FHG 38 (St Andrew's, Malahide) and FHG 39 (Malahide Demesne / Castle Abbey).  
**Important separation:** These are two different graveyards in Malahide. FHG 38 is the Church of Ireland parish churchyard in the village. FHG 39 is the medieval church and graveyard beside Malahide Castle. Do not merge their people, monuments, boundaries or burial records.

## 1. St Andrew's, Malahide — FHG 38

### Identity, location and designations
- FHG reference 38; survey dated 3 August 2008.
- Address: Church Road, Malahide, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG survey coordinate fields: X 322710, Y 245810. The survey does not state a coordinate reference system. These values resemble the older Irish National Grid, not ITM; do not label them ITM or use them as precise map coordinates until the CRS is confirmed.
- NIAH church entry 11344049; NIAH coordinate E 322699, N 245800. This is close to, but not identical with, the FHG survey coordinate.
- RMP: FHG records none for this site. RPS 437.
- Denomination Church of Ireland; FHG records ownership by the Representative Church Body.
- The churchyard is on the west side of the R107 in central Malahide. FHG 2008 describes it as closed/no longer in use and in good condition.

### Church building
- St Andrew's opened for worship in 1822, funded primarily by the Board of First Fruits, with a contribution from Colonel Talbot.
- It was enlarged in 1870; a further extension and St Marnock's Chapel were added in 2004.
- The NIAH describes a cruciform-plan church with ashlar limestone nave, rendered transepts, a two-stage crenellated tower, lancet openings, rose window, stained glass, cut-stone font, carved marble altar fittings, timber reredos and lectern, pipe organ and gallery.
- The FHG survey describes a three-bay nave, two-stage battlemented west tower, slate roof and double-light windows.
- NIAH notes the possibility that the 1822 church incorporates fabric from an earlier church. This is a possibility, not a proven survival; no upstanding medieval church remains were identified by the FHG survey.

### Individual graves and memorial targets
1. **Frederick George Hicks family headstone** — Mid-20th-century monument decorated with cherubs and swags. FHG transcribes Meredith Hicks (d. 29 January 1928), daughter Patricia Mary (d. 24 July 1955), and Frederick George Hicks (1870–1965), with professional designations FRIBA, FRIAI and RHA. The parish history identifies Hicks as the architect who converted Malahide Martello Tower, now known as Hick's Tower.
2. **Nathaniel Hone (1831–1917)** — Artist recorded by the parish as buried in the churchyard. Identify and photograph the actual marker in the field; do not assume the grave's precise position from the name alone.
3. **Professor Edmund Curtis (1881–1943)** — Historian; the parish says his marker carries the simple inscription “Historian”. High-value transcription and photographic target.
4. **Flora H. Mitchell (d. 1973)** — Artist recorded by the parish as buried here. Marker and exact grave position require field verification.
5. **Talbot family graves** — The parish identifies Richard Wogan Talbot, 5th Baron of Malahide (1846–1921), James Boswell Talbot (1874–1948) and Milo Reginal Talbot (1912–1972). Confirm individual markers and inscription readings before creating records.
6. **Unknown John Tayleur shipwreck victims (1854)** — The parish history records two unidentified sailors buried in unmarked graves beneath the church carriageway because their religious affiliation was unknown. Treat this as a parish historical account; there are no visible individual markers and the exact burial positions are not established in the source. Do not create visible headstone objects for them.
7. **Other older headstones** — FHG records a range of 19th-, 20th- and 21st-century granite, limestone and marble headstones, Celtic Revival crosses and Celtic ringed crosses. Older markers are concentrated north of the church.
8. **Unusual hewn granite cross** — Located west of the church in the FHG survey. Photograph all faces and base details; verify its current condition and whether any inscription is present.

### Research and fieldwork priorities
- Parish source states the church opened in 1822 and enlarged in 1870; record this separately from the 2004 extension.
- FHG reports no significant conservation issues in 2008. Reassess current condition rather than treating the 2008 rating as current.
- Photograph the Hicks family stone, Curtis marker, Hone and Mitchell graves if located, Talbot family stones, unusual granite cross, Celtic Revival and ringed crosses, church tower, stained-glass windows and 2004 chapel junction.
- Record all headstone inscriptions exactly, including line breaks and punctuation where legible. Use a scale, straight-on frame and raking-light detail. Preserve unedited originals.
- Confirm whether parish records, graveyard plan or burial register can establish the exact location of the two John Tayleur victims. Do not infer their location from the carriageway story.
- Public parish history lists people but is not a grave plot plan. Names and exact marker locations must be field-matched.

## 2. Malahide Demesne / Castle Abbey — FHG 39

### Identity and designations
- FHG reference 39; survey dated 30 October 2008.
- Address: Malahide Demesne, Malahide, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG coordinate fields: X 322082, Y 245438. The 2008 FHG table does not specify the coordinate reference system. These values resemble Irish National Grid, not ITM; retain as source values pending formal CRS confirmation.
- The 2023 archaeological monitoring report supplies ITM values for the church and graveyard components, including church DU012-031001 at ITM 721991, 745446 and graveyard DU012-031006 at ITM 721995, 745455.
- FHG RMP reference DU012-03106 (shortened legacy form); current component-specific records include DU012-031001 (church), DU012-031002 and DU012-031003 (carved figures / possible sheela-na-gigs), DU012-031004 (architectural fragment / mitred head), DU012-031005 (effigial tomb) and DU012-031006 (graveyard).
- RPS 384; NIAH 11344020 (graveyard).
- FHG 2008 lists ownership as private. Later Fingal records and the 2023 monitoring report identify Fingal County Council as owner of the National Monument and graveyard. Record this as a time-dependent ownership discrepancy: the 2008 FHG entry is historic, while the 2023 official report describes current Council ownership.
- Located within Malahide Castle Demesne beside the castle. The Buried in Fingal entry gives the public burial-ground locator 53.44486989, -6.16453383, but says the graveyard is closed. Do not interpret public access to the wider demesne as permission to enter the graveyard or church interior.

### Church architecture and archaeological components
- The standing church comprises a late-15th-century nave and 16th-century chancel, with a triple bellcote at the west gable and a two-storey square tower / former sacristy at the southeast corner.
- The nave has stepped battlements, pointed-arch opposed doorways, a triple-light west window, and the chancel has large traceried windows.
- The southeast tower includes a vaulted ground floor and an upper floor with fireplace and wall presses; historically interpreted as sacristy, residence or burial place for the manor's lords.
- The church interior contains burials and tombs. The graveyard is sub-circular, bounded by a curving hedgerow and limestone wall with battlements and gate piers.
- A 2023 archaeological monitoring report describes the church as a National Monument in Fingal County Council ownership and records conservation works under ministerial consent C001197, works registration W000541 and excavation/monitoring registration E005524.

### High-priority carved architectural objects
1. **Maud Plunkett effigial altar tomb (15th century)** — Located inside the nave, within a railed plot. The recumbent limestone effigy depicts a woman wearing a horned headdress, hands folded in prayer, feet on a cushion. The tomb chest has carved side panels with shields supported by angels, including Passion symbols. A 1906 account describes worn facial features and damage to the neck; the 2023 report notes significant lichen, vegetation at the northeast corner, missing small pieces at the northeast corner and previous west-end damage/repair. The tomb is a distinct monument DU012-031005, not merely a generic grave marker.
2. **Two carved female figures / possible sheela-na-gigs** — DU012-031002 and DU012-031003. The 2023 report differentiates them: one is built into the northeast chancel quoin and may not be in its original position; the other is on the east gable and has damaged lower portions. Older literature describes them as two figures, but their exact interpretation and original positions should remain qualified. Photograph both independently, with precise orientation and scale.
3. **Mitred head / carved architectural fragment** — DU012-031004, at the apex of the exterior south doorway. The 2023 report notes a zoomorphic figure on the moulding stop as well. Record the head and associated moulding details as one architectural feature with child details only if the archive data model supports that hierarchy.
4. **Sandstone water stoup** — DU012-03104 in the FHG report / DU012-031004 in later report numbering requires care: the 2023 report table calls the mitred head DU012-031004 and describes a red sandstone stoup secured to the south wall in the church text. Do not merge these simply because of the repeated number pattern; consult the official RMP database and original record sheets to resolve component-number assignment before cataloguing.
5. **Possible piscina / ambry** — The 2023 report notes an ambry (recess for holding a tabernacle) on the internal east wall, close to present ground level, exposed in the course of grave-slab reinstatement. The older FHG survey does not list it. Record as a newly highlighted architectural feature requiring measured field photography and specialist review.
6. **Possible former opening in east gable tower** — 2023 vegetation removal exposed infilled masonry and a possible arch that may represent a former door or window. The report considers this tentative; preserve as a research lead, not a confirmed medieval opening.
7. **Possible table-tomb legs** — FHG 2008 records legs of a possible table tomb north of the church. Current location and whether the fragments remain in situ must be checked.

### Memorials and cemetery features
- FHG records 18th-, 19th- and early/mid-20th-century inscribed markers, undecorated stone markers and possible reused architectural fragments.
- The church interior contains several headstones, some lying on the ground in 2008.
- A 19th-century box tomb at the east end of the chancel was recorded as significantly leaning in 2008. During the 2023 conservation works, a box tomb on the south side of the chancel with a sinking west end was dismantled by hand under archaeological supervision and reconstructed in the same position on a new foundation. The 2023 report says no human remains or archaeological material were exposed during this work. Confirm whether this is the same tomb as the FHG-described east-end tomb before treating the records as one object.
- Two fallen headstones were reported in 2008 as having been replaced by modern stones carrying copies of the original inscriptions, including the Peter Lamb 1789 memorial. This distinction is critical: the replacement stones are not original 18th-century fabric.
- The 2023 report confirms that partially sunken grave markers and slabs inside the church were moved and reinstated under archaeological supervision. One marker formerly under the west window was relocated to the western corner of the church. Preserve both pre- and post-conservation positions as a movement history if the archive schema supports it.
- The 2008 FHG report describes rough and undulating ground that may contain unmarked graves.
- 2001:452 excavation / monitoring and later archaeological reports are relevant. The 2023 report cites earlier service installation monitoring that uncovered four truncated east-west aligned individuals about 1m outside the graveyard boundary, at c.0.5m depth. These are outside the present graveyard and must be separate archaeological records, not graveyard headstone objects.

### Conservation history and current condition evidence
- FHG 2008: loose church stonework; missing pieces at northeast corner of effigial tomb; leaning 19th-century box tomb; leaning headstones at foot of east wall; overgrowth and vegetation damage south/east of church; modern replacement stones for two fallen headstones.
- 2023 works (September–November): careful hand removal of ivy/vegetation under archaeological monitoring; lime-mortar repointing and boundary wall repairs; minimal reconstruction of crenellations; inspection and retention/reuse of loose stone; grave slabs reinstated; box tomb reconstructed in same position on new foundation; small-scale ground works monitored.
- 2023 report states no new archaeological features, finds, deposits, human remains or archaeological objects were identified during the monitoring works. This does not negate the earlier external skeletal remains or known burials inside the church.
- Do not repeat the 2008 overgrowth and leaning-stone condition as current without a fresh inspection. The 2023 report documents significant completed conservation interventions.
- No ivy removal, stone movement, cleaning, resetting, digging or other interventions by archive fieldworkers. Photograph only from permitted areas and seek the relevant permissions for interior access.

### Historical narratives — evidence grading
- The church is said by Walsh to have been founded when the Talbot family settled at Malahide. This is a historical account, not an archaeologically demonstrated foundation date.
- The 2008 FHG report repeats a story that Myles Corbet used the church to stable horses and stripped roof lead for bullets. The 2023 report also repeats the account of Cromwellian forces using the church as a stable and removing lead. Preserve as a historical tradition reported in written sources, not as direct proof of specific damage layers.
- The story that Maud Plunkett was “maid, wife and widow” in one day is a local/historical tradition. The 2023 report notes multiple husbands in the historical interpretation and records the possibility, suggested by Lord Walter Fitzgerald, that the tomb may be a cenotaph erected during her lifetime. Do not state the popular story as proven biography.
- The Buried in Fingal page says the Talbots of the castle are not buried in this graveyard, despite the graveyard's historical association with the estate. Do not infer Talbot family burials here.

## 3. Coordinate, name and record reconciliation
- FHG 38 and FHG 39 are separate archive sites with different RPS/NIAH/RMP identities.
- FHG 38 FHG coordinate 322710/245810 and NIAH coordinate 322699/245800 are close but not identical. Coordinate reference system for the FHG X/Y pair is unstated; do not call it ITM.
- FHG 39 legacy FHG X/Y 322082/245438 is not the same coordinate representation as the 2023 ITM 721995/745455 graveyard centroid. Preserve source coordinates and their CRS metadata separately.
- FHG 39 component numbering must be reconciled against the current Archaeological Survey of Ireland database. In particular, the 2023 report uses DU012-031004 for the mitred head while also describing the sandstone stoup as a separate feature; resolve the apparent numbering ambiguity from the primary RMP record before publication.
- The FHG 39 “private ownership” statement from 2008 conflicts with the 2023 report and current Fingal conservation information describing Fingal County Council ownership. Preserve both with dates and source provenance.
- The FHG 39 box tomb described in 2008 and reconstructed in 2023 may be the same object, but location descriptions differ (east end vs south side of chancel). Reconcile with the 2023 plans and field photography before merging.
- The Buried in Fingal locator identifies the Malahide Castle Abbey graveyard and says it is closed; the wider demesne's visitor access does not imply access to the closed graveyard or church interior.

## 4. Recommended fieldwork sequence
1. St Andrew's: church exterior and tower, churchyard boundary, unusual hewn granite cross, older north-side markers, Hicks family headstone, Curtis “Historian” marker, Hone and Mitchell graves, Talbot stones, Celtic Revival and ringed crosses.
2. St Andrew's: verify the parish account of the two John Tayleur victims beneath the carriageway through burial records or archival plans; do not attempt any ground disturbance.
3. Malahide Demesne: from permitted access, document west bellcote, opposed doorways, mitred head and zoomorphic moulding, both carved figures, stoup, chancel windows, sacristy/tower, battlements and wall.
4. Malahide Demesne: obtain approved interior access to document Maud Plunkett tomb, shields, angel supports, horned headdress, damaged areas, ambry, reinstated slabs and reconstructed box tomb.
5. Compare current photography with 2008 FHG plates and 2023 monitoring report plates to create a dated conservation change record.
6. Maintain separate parent entities for the graveyard, church, Maud Plunkett tomb, carved figures, stoup, architectural fragment, individual memorials and external archaeological burial discoveries.
