# CICAN Dublin Archive — St Margaret's & St Fintan's Sutton (Batch 172)

**Status:** Research report saved and fetched back for verification. No permanent CICAN IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.  
**Scope:** Fingal Historic Graveyards Project FHG 46 St Margaret's and FHG 47 St Fintan's, Sutton.  
**Core distinction:** The historic FHG 46 graveyard beside the ruined church/chantry chapel is not automatically the same location as the separate modern St Margaret's Cemetery opened c.1930 in Fingal's burial database. St Fintan's FHG 47 is the large active cemetery at Carrickbrack Road, Sutton, and includes a distinct historic churchyard and ecclesiastical enclosure.

## 1. St Margaret's — FHG 46 (historic church and graveyard)

### Identity and location controls
- FHG 46; survey dated 30 July 2008.
- Townland and parish: St Margaret's; address in FHG is simply St Margaret's, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG source coordinates: X 313032, Y 243865. The survey does not specify the coordinate reference system; retain the original pair and do not label it ITM until verified.
- RMP graveyard DU014-00202; church DU014-00201; chantry chapel DU014-00203.
- RPS 626; NIAH 11348001 (graveyard).
- Ownership: Fingal County Council.
- FHG setting: pasture fields west of the R122. No upstanding early-medieval features were identified by the 2008 survey.
- The separate Buried in Fingal page for “St Margaret's Cemetery” gives 53.43312003, -6.30005406 and says that cemetery opened around 1930. That is not sufficient evidence to use its locator for FHG 46: the FHG 46 record describes an older rural church-and-chantry complex west of the R122. Keep the two cemetery/site identities separate until coordinates and boundaries are reconciled from the RMP/NIAH mapping and a ground plan.
- The historic graveyard is set in agricultural land beside a farmhouse; do not assume public access. The modern cemetery's access instructions do not apply to the historic monument.

### Historical context and architectural phases
- A late-12th-century document confirmed the site to the Archbishop of Dublin and refers to “the town of St Margaret's”.
- The church was originally called Donaghmore and probably fell into ruin between 1630 and 1650, according to Walsh (1888), cited by the FHG survey.
- The surviving church remains lie at the west of the graveyard and represent a building described as having been extensive.
- Sir John Plunkett built a separate chantry chapel in the 16th century east of the church remains.
- An early-19th-century mausoleum is attached to the southeast corner of the chantry chapel.
- An 18th-century mausoleum dedicated to the Morgan family stands at the southern boundary.
- The church, chantry chapel and two mausoleums are separate architectural/memorial objects; they must not be represented as one building.
- The surviving medieval church fabric includes the west wall and parts of north and south walls in roughly coursed squared-rubble limestone. A large buttress or corner-tower foundation survives at the northwest corner.
- The chantry chapel walls survive almost to roof level in squared limestone masonry. A pointed-arched opening in its west wall has decorative mouldings and a carved head. A wall extends from its northwest corner and shows evidence of rebuilding.

### Individual memorials and features
1. **John Mansfield / Mary Mansfield headstone (1727)** — Curved-top early-18th-century limestone headstone west of the chapel. FHG transcription: “This stone and burial place belongs to John Mansfield wand also here let ye body of Mary Mansfield wife of ye said John Mansfield who died May ye ? 1727”. The word “wand” and missing day are transcription uncertainties; do not silently correct or complete them. Photograph the inscription letter-by-letter.
2. **Morgan family mausoleum (18th century)** — At the southern boundary. FHG identifies it as dedicated to the Morgan family. It is a separate architectural object and a priority for conservation photography.
3. **Early-19th-century mausoleum** — Attached to the southeast corner of the 16th-century chantry chapel. Its commemorated family/person is not specified in the FHG text; do not assume it belongs to the Morgan family.
4. **Carved head at pointed west opening** — Decorative head integrated into the moulded pointed arch of the chantry chapel. Photograph as an architectural detail linked to the chapel, with orientation, scale and condition.
5. **Cast-iron angel head** — FHG Plate 46.2 specifically records a cast-iron angel-head motif. Locate the original object and document whether it is a headstone finial, plot ornament or a detached component. Do not assume it remains in situ.
6. **Other 18th–20th-century markers** — Predominantly limestone with later marble and cast-iron examples; free-standing crosses, undecorated stones, reused architectural fragments and individual table tombs with limestone slabs and granite legs. Later plots have low kerbing.
7. **Moved markers and fragments** — The 2008 survey says several markers had been moved from their original positions and gathered at a wall. Record their present positions as observed, but do not reconstruct original plots without a plan or evidence.
8. **Boundary and entrance** — Sub-rectangular graveyard with roughly coursed squared-rubble limestone wall, dressed limestone entrance pillars and raised undulating interior ground. Photograph wall construction, coping, entrance and slope.

### Conservation condition — 2008 baseline only
- FHG 2008 rated the graveyard good but recorded heavy overgrowth and ivy.
- Ivy and trees were damaging the two mausoleums and masonry.
- Sloping ground showed evidence of headstone movement; several markers had been gathered against a wall.
- Some southern stones had been cleaned abrasively; some showed heavy lamination.
- FHG recommended careful specialist-led vegetation removal, investigation of leaning stones, recording and retention of loose fragments, avoidance of abrasive cleaners and conservation/repair of the mausoleums.
- These are 2008 condition observations, not a current assessment. Do not repeat them as present-day conditions without inspection.
- Fingal's 2012 archaeological-resource management report separately describes the chantry chapel as about 9m x 4.9m x 0.90m thick, with ornamental pointed doorway, carved head on the north impost, blocked windows with chamfered limestone jambs, internal burials, a large tree and ivy overgrowth. The measurements and report refer to the chapel, not the entire graveyard.

### Nearby heritage features — keep separate
- Dunsoghly Castle (DU014-005001 etc.) is a separate 15th-century tower-house complex.
- St Bridget's Well (DU014-003) is a separate protected holy well west of the modern Catholic church, not the St Margaret's FHG 46 graveyard.
- St Margaret's Church (RC), RPS 625, is a 19th-century Catholic church and is not the ruined medieval church/chantry chapel in FHG 46.
- The modern St Margaret's Cemetery in Buried in Fingal opened around 1930. Do not merge its interments or coordinates with FHG 46 without documentary and spatial proof.
- Chapelmidway (FHG 16) is a separate graveyard between Kilsallaghan and St Margaret's; it is not FHG 46.

### Fieldwork priorities
- Secure landowner permission and confirm the precise FHG 46 boundary before visiting.
- Photograph the medieval church west wall, northwest buttress/corner-tower foundation, surviving north/south wall sections and masonry phases.
- Photograph the 16th-century chantry chapel, pointed doorway, mouldings and carved head.
- Document both mausoleums independently, including all elevations, entrances, inscriptions, roof details, masonry and vegetation impacts.
- Locate and transcribe the John and Mary Mansfield 1727 headstone.
- Locate the cast-iron angel head; record its actual context and whether it is still attached to a marker.
- Photograph loose/moved fragments in place before any conservation handling.
- Obtain a reliable site plan and resolve FHG coordinates vs RMP/NIAH coordinates before mapping.
- Do not enter vaults, move stones, clean inscriptions or remove ivy.

## 2. St Fintan's, Sutton — FHG 47

### Identity and location
- FHG 47; survey dated 6 September 2008.
- Address: Carrickbrack Road, Sutton, County Dublin, Ireland.
- Townland: Sutton South; parish: Howth.
- FHG source coordinates: X 327074, Y 238057. Coordinate reference system is not stated in the 2008 volume; retain as source values and do not relabel as ITM.
- The 2025 Conservation Management Plan gives the site coordinate ITM 726977, 738087. This is the more recent explicitly identified ITM locator; it represents the church/graveyard site, not each separate feature.
- Buried in Fingal cemetery locator: 53.37980407, -6.09369399.
- Graveyard DU015-031003; church DU015-031002; ecclesiastical enclosure DU015-031001; cross DU015-031004.
- RPS 575; NIAH 11358033 (graveyard).
- Ownership: Fingal County Council.
- Site lies on the west side of the R105 between Sutton and Howth. The older section around the church is distinct from the later cemetery extensions.
- 2025 CMP is CMF24-2-DF002, prepared by IAC Archaeology for Fingal County Council, authored by Paul Duffy.

### Ecclesiastical enclosure and church chronology
- The wider site may have early-medieval origins; the western curve in the old graveyard boundary and a low flat-topped bank to the east are interpreted as the remains of a possible 6th/7th-century ecclesiastical enclosure.
- The 2025 CMP regards the standing church structure as Early-Norman in date with later medieval alterations; the 2008 FHG survey describes it as similar in form to a pre-Norman oratory. Preserve this distinction: the site may have early-medieval roots, while surviving church fabric is interpreted as Early-Norman/later medieval.
- The 2025 CMP states that the oldest section of the graveyard dates to 1189, based on the SMR file, and that burial continued into the mid-20th century, with a 1954 marker in the site.
- FHG records the old boundary curve as shown on the 1837 Ordnance Survey map; the western hedgerow and eastern bank together may mark the former enclosure.
- The church is a small structure of squared rubble sandstone brought to irregular courses. The west gable stands to full height with a tall narrow bellcote and slightly pointed-arched opening; square-headed opening in south wall; round-headed opening in east wall with sandstone jambs and sill; small square-headed and round-headed openings in north wall.
- The 2025 CMP identifies multiple construction phases, including an initial phase around c.1100 (tufa elements and lower wall courses), remodelling c.1190–1300 (Dundry stone jambs/window base) and later medieval/early modern reuse and rebuilding. These are the CMP's archaeological interpretations, not dates inscribed on the building.

### Exceptional small architectural objects — 2025 CMP discoveries
1. **Circular stone mortar rim reused above the doorway** — The circular aperture above the doorway and below the bellcote contains the rim of a medieval stone mortar, likely with four lugs and one channelled lug. The CMP suggests it was inserted into the opening during a later decorative/stabilisation phase. This is a reused portable object incorporated into church fabric, not a purpose-built window or bullaun.
2. **Possible second mortar base in north-wall niche** — A hemispherical stone fragment in a cavity/press in the northern wall may derive from a second mortar. The CMP says the diameters do not match well enough to confidently identify it as the base of the doorway mortar; it may be a separate reused mortar fragment.
3. **Small lugged white-stone mortar in upper graveyard** — A separate mortar of medieval or early-modern date is located in the upper graveyard close to cross DU015-031004. The CMP includes a dedicated plate. It is a distinct movable/portable archaeological object, not the same object as the circular mortar rim reused above the church doorway.
4. **Reused sandstone window heads and fragments** — Two rounded sandstone window heads survive, including one set into a niche/press in the north wall. A sandstone window-base fragment has been reused as the head of a niche/press in the south wall. These fragments may represent displaced/reused medieval architectural material; their original positions are not proven.
5. **Possible Dundry Stone window elements** — The northeastern window contains jambs/base potentially of imported Dundry Stone. Petrographic analysis would be required to confirm the material. The CMP suggests possible 12th/13th-century remodelling but explicitly treats material identification as provisional.
6. **Large septfoil east window** — The east window is unusually large for the church, with a seven-foil head and sandstone voussoirs. The CMP suggests a later medieval remodelling phase, possibly 15th century. Photograph each foil, joints, jambs, glazing groove and stone differences.
7. **Doorway striations** — The southern doorway jamb carries multiple striations, potentially resulting from blade sharpening or stone dust collection for apotropaic practices. Record as observed tool/wear marks; do not state a magical function as proven.
8. **Wall presses/niches** — The church contains multiple niches with reused architectural fragments. The CMP argues these may be decorative/reconstruction features rather than functional liturgical fittings. Preserve this as an interpretation.
9. **Bellcote** — Although visually prominent, the CMP finds no fittings for a bell or bell-rope and no roof scars. It interprets the current bellcote as largely rebuilt as a picturesque feature in the 18th/19th century. Do not automatically call it a surviving medieval bellcote.

### Cross and graveyard memorials
1. **Plain granite cross DU015-031004** — Located in the southwestern part of the older graveyard. It may date to the period of the enclosure, but the CMP describes this as potential rather than certain. Photograph all faces, base, pecking/patina and setting; preserve its current position.
2. **Jackson family limestone obelisk and ledger memorial** — Immediately west of the church, with cast-iron railings. The 2025 CMP notes rusted/damaged rails around the Jackson ledger memorial. The obelisk and ledger should be separately documented only if physically distinct objects; verify their relationship in the site plan.
3. **Box tombs and table tombs** — FHG Plate 47.2 shows a box tomb; the older section contains multiple box/table tombs and individual obelisks. The 2023 Fingal Historic Stone Tombs Project reports 20 tombs at St Fintan's among 99 tombs surveyed in 19 Council-managed graveyards. This indicates a substantial tomb assemblage deserving a separate systematic survey.
4. **Stephen Hughes memorial** — 20th-century marker with bronze statue by Henri Chapu, in the southeast extension. Photograph statue, pedestal, inscription, artist attribution and condition; verify artist attribution against the monument itself and authoritative catalogue.
5. **Grace Burke miniature temple** — Unusual miniature temple memorial in the southeast extension. Record architectural form, inscription, maker/material and exact plot.
6. **Tufa grave markers** — The 2025 CMP plate list identifies a tufa grave marker in the graveyard. Record the material identification as CMP-derived pending specialist verification and photograph texture/tooling.
7. **Other 18th–20th-century headstones and crosses** — The oldest 1889 section around the church contains headstones, free-standing crosses, obelisks, box tombs and undecorated markers. The 1907 and 1954 sections contain further headstones and crosses; the newest western lawn section contains flat slabs.
8. **Stardust memorial** — Memorial at the southern boundary dedicated to victims of the Stardust fire of 1981. Treat the memorial as a distinct commemorative object and verify inscription and design through field photography.
9. **Notable burials** — Sources list Phil Lynott (1986), Ray McAnally (1989), Michael MacLiammoir (1978), Pádraic Colum (1972), Mary Colum (1957), William Stokes (1878) and Mainie Jellett (1944). Buried in Fingal also lists the churchyard's cultural figures. Confirm exact grave markers and plot positions before linking persons to physical memorials.

### Graveyard phases and landscape
- Four sections are described: oldest 1889 section beside church; 1907 extension east; 1954 extension west; newest “lawn” at far west with flat grave markers.
- FHG 2008 says extensions remain in use. Buried in Fingal provides interment books covering multiple periods including 1889–1943, 1943–1964, 1964–2013 and 1973-extension records. Treat these registers as documentary evidence for burials, not as substitutes for a physical plot survey.
- Extensive views across Dublin Bay are part of the site's setting.
- St Fintan's Well is southwest of the church. The 2008 FHG account records offerings of rags, handkerchiefs, medals and money and traditions of cures, particularly for sore eyes. Treat healing claims as folklore, not medical fact. The well is a separate feature from the church, graveyard and cross.
- The churchyard, cross, enclosure and well must be separate spatial entities with supported relationships; do not make the graveyard boundary equal to the entire enclosure.

### 2025 Conservation Management Plan — current baseline
- The CMP records degradation of the wrought-iron roof grille, corrosion of window grille and gate fittings, an embedded ivy root in a deep fissure in the north wall, loss of pointing, inappropriate hard flaunching at the east end of the south wall, damaged rails around the Jackson ledger memorial, toppled headstones and evidence of biocide use around markers.
- Recommendations include conservation-grade lime repointing, treatment/replacement of corroded metal fittings, careful management of ivy roots, correction of inappropriate flaunching and further graveyard maintenance.
- CMP includes measured/rectified photographic models of church elevations, a digital terrain model, photogrammetry, geophysical survey, cartographic analysis and detailed condition assessment. This should be the principal current baseline for future field verification.
- The report includes historical photographs and drawings from 1840s, 1853, 1887, 1890s and c.1917/1920, plus modern surveys. These offer a valuable change-over-time record.
- The CMP recommends further research into St Fintan and local associations.
- Do not repeat 2008 FHG condition notes as current where the 2025 CMP gives a later condition baseline. Conversely, do not assume recommended repairs have been completed; verify implementation.
- No intervention by archive fieldworkers: no cleaning, scraping, lifting mortars, moving headstones, pulling ivy, treating biocide, resetting monuments or entering unsafe structures.

### Fieldwork priorities
- Use the 2025 CMP photogrammetry/elevation drawings and plates as the fieldwork baseline.
- Photograph the doorway circular mortar rim, north-wall niche/hemispherical fragment and small lugged mortar in upper graveyard separately.
- Photograph and measure the granite cross DU015-031004, including all faces and relationship to the small mortar.
- Record tufa marker, sandstone/Dundry window pieces, septfoil east window, doorway striations, wall presses, bellcote and masonry phases.
- Document the Jackson obelisk/ledger/railings, Stephen Hughes statue memorial, Grace Burke miniature temple, box tombs, table tombs and Stardust memorial.
- Survey the oldest graveyard section, enclosure bank/hedgerow and 1889/1907/1954/lawn sections separately.
- Locate St Fintan's Well southwest of the church; verify exact position and present condition without assuming the 2008 folklore description is current.
- Confirm notable burials using the council interment books and then physically match them to stones.

## 3. Duplicate prevention and naming controls
- FHG 46 historic St Margaret's church/chantry graveyard: DU014-00202 graveyard, DU014-00201 church, DU014-00203 chantry chapel; RPS 626; NIAH 11348001. Do not merge with the separate modern St Margaret's Cemetery opened c.1930, or with St Margaret's RC church (RPS 625), St Bridget's Well (DU014-003), Dunsoghly Castle or Chapelmidway FHG 16.
- FHG 47 St Fintan's: DU015-031003 graveyard, DU015-031002 church, DU015-031004 cross, DU015-031001 ecclesiastical enclosure; RPS 575; NIAH 11358033.
- The doorway mortar rim, north-wall mortar fragment and upper-graveyard lugged mortar are three distinct objects/contexts. Do not duplicate or merge.
- St Fintan's Well is separate from the graveyard and from the cross.
- Preserve FHG 2008 coordinate pairs with unknown CRS and CMP 2025 explicit ITM coordinate separately.
- No permanent CICAN IDs, production catalogue entries or map pins created. Master catalogue access and duplicate/schema checks remain prerequisites.
