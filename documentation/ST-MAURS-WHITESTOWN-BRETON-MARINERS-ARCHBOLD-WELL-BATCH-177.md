# CICAN Dublin Archive — St Maur's, Whitestown (Batch 177)

**Official FHG reference:** 54  
**Survey date:** 26 July 2008  
**Location:** Whitestown, south side of R128 between Lusk and Rush, County Dublin, Ireland  
**Status:** Research report committed and fetched back for verification. No permanent CICAN IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.

## 1. Site identity and location
- Fingal Historic Graveyards Project FHG 54 — St Maur's, Whitestown.
- Townland Whitestown; parish Lusk.
- Address: Whitestown Road, Rush, County Dublin, Ireland.
- FHG source coordinate pair: X 324643, Y 253561. Coordinate reference system is not stated in the 2008 survey; preserve as raw source values, not GPS/ITM.
- Fingal County Council's Buried in Fingal portal locator: latitude 53.51731341, longitude -6.12083735. This is the cemetery-level locator and should not be treated as the exact position of every feature.
- Site lies south of the R128 between Lusk and Rush, overlooking Rogerstown Estuary.
- Ownership: Fingal County Council; denomination unknown.
- RMP DU008-02102 (graveyard), with DU008-02101 (church). Volume 1 lists the church and graveyard as separate components.
- RPS 279; NIAH 11324015 (graveyard).
- The old graveyard around the church is distinct from the later modern extension. St Maur's Well on Spout Road is a separate nearby devotional/archaeological feature, not part of the cemetery itself.
- The site is sometimes described online as Whitestown Cemetery, Whitestown Road, Rush. Retain FHG name St Maur's, Whitestown as canonical site title with “Whitestown Cemetery” as a source alias.

## 2. Historical identity and foundation tradition
- The Civil Survey of 1654–56 described “ye walls of an old chapel of ease at Whytestowne”.
- The church is dedicated to St Maur, identified in the FHG source as patron saint of Rush; the recorded feast day is 17 January.
- Local tradition says French or Breton sailors were in danger of shipwreck off Rogerstown. One sailor vowed that if they landed safely they would build a church in honour of St Maur. Their ship was driven ashore near Whitestown and they built a church and dug a well.
- The tradition is recorded in the Irish Folklore Commission Schools Manuscript Collection, Vol. 786, pp.130–131. It is a local foundation legend, not independently established as a historical event.
- The FHG describes the upstanding archaeological features as late-medieval. The old chapel was already recorded as ruinous in the 1654–56 Civil Survey.
- The nearby well is on Spout Road. The well and graveyard must remain separate archive records with a relationship note.
- Do not turn the Breton mariners' plaque into proof of a dated shipwreck or named vessel. The plaque commemorates the tradition in its own words.

## 3. Church remains — DU008-02101
### Surviving east gable
- The principal surviving church feature is the stepped east gable wall.
- It is constructed of rubble limestone and sandstone.
- A pointed-arched window opening is centred in the wall.
- A raised area or mound extends west from the gable and marks the former nave footprint.
- Fingal's 2019–2021 constraints study describes the distinct mound as approximately 17m east–west by 8.5m north–south.
- The church was formerly an “old chapel of ease” in the Civil Survey.
- The 2008 survey and later constraints study refer to previous stabilisation. The constraints study also recorded vegetation growth on the wall top, mortar washout at the base and some cracking. These are dated observations; current condition must be field-checked.
- A stone at the base of the church ruin requires repair according to the 2008 FHG conservation notes. Do not assume the repair has or has not occurred since 2008.
- The gable, window opening, wall steps, wall-top coping, masonry junctions and mound are architectural features to document separately within the church record.

### Pointed east window
- The pointed-arched window opening is a distinctive surviving feature.
- Record the full opening, arch profile, jambs, sill, masonry around it, stone types and any evidence of rebuilding or stabilisation.
- Do not assign a precise construction date to the window solely from the pointed form; the FHG classifies the upstanding features broadly as late medieval.
- Photograph from both east and west sides only where safely and lawfully accessible. No climbing, touching or placing equipment against the wall.

### Raised nave mound
- A raised area runs west from the east gable and represents the former nave footprint according to council mapping.
- The 2019–2021 constraints study describes a distinct mound of approximately 17m × 8.5m.
- The mound is a landscape/archaeological feature, not a second church.
- Do not dig, probe, stake, or conduct intrusive measurement.

## 4. Breton mariners' plaque — high-priority individual object
- The plaque is fixed to the east side of the surviving east gable.
- FHG transcription: “Breton mariners saved from a storm erected this chapel of St. Maur”.
- The Buried in Fingal description reproduces the same wording.
- The plaque is a commemorative inscription associated with the local sailors' foundation tradition.
- It should have its own object record, linked to church DU008-02101 and the St Maur's graveyard parent site.
- The date of erection is not specified in the 2008 survey. Do not describe it as medieval or original church fabric.
- Fieldwork: capture a full contextual image showing the plaque's position on the east face, straight-on inscription photograph, oblique views, dimensions, material, attachment/fixing method, weathering and any maker/date marks.
- Transcribe exactly, preserving spelling and punctuation. Compare field transcription with FHG and Buried in Fingal.
- Avoid rubbing, chalking, wetting, cleaning or removing biological growth.

## 5. Graveyard layout and development
- The graveyard is sub-rectangular.
- Older section surrounds the church remains on raised ground in the northwest of the cemetery.
- The remainder is a modern extension.
- The northwest boundary retains an older stone wall; other boundaries are described as concrete breeze-block wall.
- Older section has 18th- and 19th-century headstones and some unmarked stones.
- Modern section has simple headstones and free-standing stone crosses arranged in east- and west-facing rows.
- The 2008 survey records the cemetery as still in use and regularly maintained.
- Buried in Fingal says the old section was closed by Order in 1973 and that a new section opened. Reconcile these descriptions: the old historic section and modern/new section have different burial histories. Do not apply the 1973 closure to the entire cemetery.
- The older churchyard is elevated and overlooks Rogerstown Estuary. The modern extension is a separate phase of cemetery growth.

## 6. Archbold family headstones
- The 2008 FHG site description identifies two 18th-century headstones dedicated to members of the Archbold family in the older section.
- Their exact names, dates, wording, decoration and positions are not fully transcribed in the available FHG text excerpt.
- Buried in Fingal's Whitestown entry gives the oldest recorded headstone as 1724, according to Dr Egan's survey. Do not automatically assign 1724 to either Archbold stone without checking the transcription and inscriptions.
- The cemetery's interment books and headstone transcriptions are available through the Buried in Fingal page and Local Studies & Archives. The old graveyard is also covered by J.A.P.M.D.I., Vol. XII, pp.524–532 and M.J.S. Egan, *Memorials of the Dead*, Vol. 8, pp.189–229.
- Field task: locate both Archbold headstones, photograph all faces and edges, transcribe complete inscriptions, record exact dates and determine whether any crest, IHS, cross or mortality symbol is present.
- Record the two stones as distinct physical objects, even if members of one family.

## 7. Table tomb — Plate 54.2
- FHG Plate 54.2 shows a stone table tomb near the church ruin.
- The site description confirms one table tomb south of the church.
- The tomb is a separate funerary monument, not part of the church wall.
- The plate image should be used as a visual matching reference during fieldwork.
- Record the top slab, supports/legs, base, inscription, material, tooling, weathering, settlement, cracks and relationship to nearby headstones.
- No inscription or burial identity is established in the FHG text excerpt; do not invent a name or date.
- The 2019–2021 constraints study identifies the site as a graveyard with memorials dating approximately from 1740 to present, but that general range is not a date for this table tomb.

## 8. Rough-hewn granite cross
- FHG records a simple rough-hewn granite cross east of the church.
- It is a separate free-standing memorial object.
- Its inscription, date, dimensions, orientation and original purpose are not stated in the source text available here.
- Fieldwork: photograph the cross in context, front/back/sides, junctions and base; record any inscription or tool marks and whether it is fixed or set into the ground.
- Do not call it a Celtic cross or assign a religious dedication unless its form or inscription supports that description.

## 9. Other older grave markers and inscriptions
- FHG records 18th- and 19th-century headstones and some unmarked stones in the older section.
- Decoration includes IHS motifs in sunbursts, crosses and Lamb of God motifs.
- A number of people drowned or were lost at sea are buried here. Buried in Fingal names:
  - William Joseph Creighton, aged 16.
  - James Gough, aged 18.
  - Nicholas Grimly, aged 64.
  - Captain Richard Herbert, aged 35.
- These names are reported in the council's burial-ground history; identify their actual memorials and verify inscriptions before connecting individual stones to these biographies.
- The Rev. Nicholas Andrews, P.P. of Rush, who died of cholera in 1849, is also buried here. Verify his memorial and exact inscription before creating a dedicated object record.
- Elizabeth Kelly erected a headstone to her son John, who the inscription says was murdered at Trim Industrial Schools on 12 February 1912, aged 27. This is a sensitive historical allegation. Preserve the exact inscription as a primary-source statement; do not embellish or present unverified surrounding details as fact.
- The cemetery has a kerbed plot with a large tree growing in the centre; this is a condition/landscape concern, not a memorial identity.
- Each distinct stone, cross, kerb, railing and inscription is a separate candidate for the visual archive. Do not create a single generic “headstones” record.

## 10. St Maur's Well — separate feature on Spout Road
- The well is located on Spout Road near Whitestown, not within the graveyard.
- FHG cites the Irish Folklore Commission Schools Manuscript Collection, Vol. 786, pp.56–57 for its location and reported healing traditions.
- The source says the well was believed to cure headaches and sore eyes. This is folklore and must not be represented as a medical fact.
- The well is associated in local tradition with the French/Breton mariners' vow and church foundation story.
- Buried in Fingal repeats the same story and states that the well is on Spout Road.
- No exact coordinate for the well is established by the FHG graveyard coordinate. Do not use the cemetery locator as the well pin.
- Fieldwork: locate the well from official mapping, historic sources and landowner/council information; verify access and exact aperture. Record any stone surround, steps, water, offerings, inscriptions, nearby spring or road relationship. Do not enter private land without permission or disturb the water/structure.
- Create a separate well record only when exact identity and location are established. Link to St Maur's graveyard through a “associated tradition / nearby devotional site” relationship, not as a component within the graveyard boundary.

## 11. Condition and conservation
- FHG 2008 describes the cemetery as well and regularly maintained, with the modern section still in use.
- The same survey records leaning and broken headstones and a kerbed plot with a large tree growing in its centre.
- The FHG recommends continuing regular maintenance and checking the stability of leaning stones before any straightening/resetting.
- It notes repairs required to stone at the base of the church ruin and refurbishment of old entrance gates.
- Fingal's 2019–2021 constraints study describes the church as previously stabilised, but records vegetation growth on the wall top, mortar washout at the base and cracking.
- These are dated observations and recommendations, not a current 2026 assessment.
- The site is an archaeological monument. Fingal County Council warns that unplanned ivy removal or maintenance can cause damage. No cleaning, scraping, re-pointing, vegetation removal, resetting stones, moving fragments or repairs by CICAN.
- Report any obvious new damage to Fingal County Council and leave all objects in place.

## 13. Duplicate prevention and publication controls
- FHG 54 St Maur's, Whitestown is not the same site as FHG 43 Kenure CofI or FHG 44 Kenure medieval graveyard.
- The old churchyard and modern cemetery extension are separate spatial phases within one burial-ground site.
- Church DU008-02101 and graveyard DU008-02102 are separate component records.
- Breton mariners' plaque, east gable, pointed window, table tomb, each Archbold headstone and granite cross are separate physical object candidates.
- St Maur's Well on Spout Road is a separate nearby feature; do not use cemetery coordinates for it.
- Local French/Breton sailors' foundation narrative is a tradition, not proven historical fact.
- The 1973 closure applies to the old section according to Buried in Fingal; the new section opened and the cemetery page also says a modern section remains. Do not describe the entire site as closed.
- No permanent CICAN IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.
