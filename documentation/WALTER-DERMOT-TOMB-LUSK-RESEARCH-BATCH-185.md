# CICAN Dublin Archive — Walter Dermot Tomb, Lusk Research Batch 185

**Date:** 17 November 2026  
**Scope:** Parent-linked micro-object research for the Walter Dermot funerary slab in the historic belfry / Lusk Round Tower complex, St MacCullin's, Lusk, County Dublin.  
**Status:** Research-only. Existing parent record DUB-BLD-0177 remains unchanged. No permanent child ID, source-registry entry, visual record or map pin created because the complete canonical object and component catalogues cannot be validated.

## 1. Identity and location

- **Object name:** Walter Dermot Tomb / Dermot Tomb / Dermott Tomb.
- **Commemorated:** Walter Dermot and his wife (the 1988 inscription transcription refers to “uxor eius”, his wife; the wife's personal name is not securely legible in the transcription consulted).
- **Parent:** DUB-BLD-0177 — Saint MacCullin's Church, Lusk.
- **Setting:** Ground-floor / interior of the historic square belfry adjoining Lusk's round tower, within the St MacCullin's ecclesiastical complex.
- **Monument type:** Rectangular limestone grave slab with a relief depiction of the Holy Trinity and a marginal Latin inscription.
- **Historic survey:** Bradley & King, *County Dublin Urban Archaeology Survey*, Part VIII(iv), 1988, Lusk monuments section, pp. 264–265; Fig. 47 captioned “Lusk: the Dermot tomb 1535x8”.
- **Modern official visitor context:** Heritage Ireland identifies a commemoration of Walter Dermot, d.1538, among the medieval tombs in the belfry.

Keep “Dermot” and “Dermott” as source-name variants. Do not create a second parent building or an independent outdoor pin.

## 2. Date discrepancy — preserve both readings

The principal source records are not identical:
- The 1988 survey's figure caption reads **1535x8**.
- Its monument heading also reads **Walter Dermot. 1535x8**.
- The transcribed marginal inscription includes the year **M CCCCC XXX V** (1535) in the surviving reading, though the transcription is partly damaged/uncertain.
- Heritage Ireland's current visitor page identifies Walter Dermot as **d.1538**.
- The 1837 *Topographical Dictionary of Ireland* says the tomb inscription had suffered obliteration and that antiquaries had assigned the monument dates as early as the sixth century or as late as the sixteenth century.

**Archive date wording:** “Sixteenth-century funerary slab; the 1988 survey labels it 1535x8 and transcribes a damaged date as 1535; Heritage Ireland identifies Walter Dermot as having died in 1538. The exact date and meaning of the inscription require specialist epigraphic re-examination.”

Do not silently change 1535x8 to 1538 or assert the slab was made in 1535. The inscription date may refer to a commemorative event, and the current evidence does not resolve the discrepancy.

## 3. Physical description and published measurements

The 1988 survey describes:
- Rectangular limestone slab.
- A depiction of the Holy Trinity in false relief.
- Marginal inscription in Latin.
- Published dimensions: **length 166 cm; width 68 cm; thickness 18 cm**.

These are historical survey measurements, not measurements made by CICAN. “False relief” is the terminology used by the 1988 survey; preserve it as source wording rather than silently substituting a different carving classification.

The survey's figure 47 is a black-and-white photograph of the carved slab. It shows a tall rectangular stone with a central religious composition, raised lettering around the margins and visible surface wear. The visual evidence is useful for recognising the object, but a scan of the published photograph is not a newly captured field photograph and does not automatically grant reuse rights.

## 4. Inscription — transcription and uncertainty

Bradley & King transcribe the marginal inscription as:

“[H]IC IACET WALTERUS DERMOT ET OXSOR EU MOLINA QURU / AIABUS PPICIETUR CYS AMEN ... ANNO DNI M CCCCC XXX V ...”

This is visibly an imperfect transcription of a damaged inscription and includes uncertain or corrupted letter sequences. The opening is recognisable as a Latin funerary formula (“Here lies Walter Dermot and his wife…”), followed by a prayer for the souls and an abbreviated year formula. However:
- The wife's name is not reliably recoverable from this transcription.
- The exact wording of the prayer should not be normalised without direct examination of the stone and a specialist reading.
- The terminal date is reported as 1535 in the survey transcription, but the current Heritage Ireland visitor text gives Walter Dermot's death as 1538.

The earlier 1837 description also says the inscription had suffered obliteration. This provides historical evidence that legibility was already a problem in the nineteenth century.

**Archive policy:** retain the survey's transcription verbatim as a source quotation, mark uncertain characters, and do not supply a confident full translation. A future epigraphic record should include photographs under permitted access, letter-by-letter reading, expansions of abbreviations in brackets, and a separate translation with confidence notes.

## 5. Historical interpretation and antiquarian uncertainty

The 1837 *Topographical Dictionary* notes that the tomb was “curiously sculptured in relief” and that different antiquaries had assigned it dates ranging from the sixth to the sixteenth century because of obliteration in the inscription. This is important evidence of the monument's changing interpretation, but the sixth-century date is not supported by the 1988 survey or Heritage Ireland's modern identification.

The 1988 survey places the monument within the medieval ecclesiastical complex at Lusk and lists it alongside the Barnewall–Sharl tomb and James Bermingham slab. Heritage Ireland likewise lists Walter Dermot among the tombs in the belfry. These sources support its identification as a historic funerary monument in the complex; they do not establish the exact date of carving beyond the disputed inscription evidence.

## 6. Setting, protection and access

Heritage Ireland describes the Lusk Round Tower and Belfry as a National Monument protected under the National Monuments Acts. Its visitor page states that the belfry contains several historic monuments, including the Walter Dermot commemoration.

The current Heritage Ireland page gives a limited 2026 tour programme and explicitly states that interior access is by guided tour only and restricted to the ground floor of the belfry. The listed 2026 dates are May 2–4, May 30–June 1, July 4–5, August 15–16 and 22–23, and September 5–6, with tours at 11am, 1pm and 3pm, subject to limited capacity and first-come-first-served admission. These are the dates currently published on the page; do not assume additional opening dates or unrestricted interior access.

The official page's description places the round tower and belfry in the graveyard of St MacCullin's, within the ecclesiastical enclosure. Do not interpret public access to the graveyard or guided access to the ground floor as permission to enter upper floors, handle the slab, use flash, or conduct conservation work.

Source: https://heritageireland.ie/places-to-visit/lusk-round-tower-and-belfry/

## 7. Founder photography brief — guided access only

If the slab is visible during a permitted guided visit and photography is allowed:
1. Photograph the complete slab in situ, including its relationship to the belfry floor and surrounding masonry.
2. Take a straight-on full-frame image with the camera plane parallel to the slab.
3. Capture the Holy Trinity relief with diffuse light; avoid harsh flash and raking light unless explicitly permitted by the guide.
4. Photograph all four margins and corners to record the full inscription sequence.
5. Take overlapping high-resolution details of damaged or unclear letters, preserving the original images unsharpened.
6. Include a scale only if the guide permits and it can be placed without touching the monument.
7. Record date, time, tour/access conditions, camera orientation and whether the image is a complete view or a detail crop.

Do not touch, wet, chalk, rub, trace or clean the slab. Do not move objects or attempt to improve visibility by altering the monument or its surroundings.

## 8. Distinction from nearby monuments and features

The belfry contains multiple separate objects. Keep the following distinct:
- **Walter Dermot slab:** sixteenth-century limestone slab with Holy Trinity relief and marginal inscription.
- **Barnewall–Sharl table tomb:** late-sixteenth-century double-effigy Renaissance table tomb, researched in Batch 184.
- **James Bermingham slab:** 1637 limestone slab with armoured knight effigy and Birmingham/FitzWilliam heraldry.
- **Seventeenth-century fireplace/chimney-breast plaque:** Annunciation and six shields, reported as removed from Bremore Castle.
- **Octagonal font:** tentatively seventeenth century.
- **Bullaun stone:** pink granite, recorded beside the south entrance.

Do not merge any of these into a single monument record. If later promoted, use the existing parent DUB-BLD-0177 and distinct child-object relationships.

## 11. Conclusion and next steps

The Walter Dermot slab is a distinct, high-value funerary object within the existing St MacCullin's parent building record. Its principal research value lies in the Holy Trinity relief, marginal Latin inscription and the discrepancy between the 1988 “1535x8” reading and Heritage Ireland's “d.1538” identification. The 1837 description confirms that the inscription was already partly obliterated by the nineteenth century.

Next:
1. Preserve the date discrepancy and inscription uncertainty in any future catalogue record.
2. Verify current visibility and photography permission during a guided ground-floor visit.
3. Seek specialist epigraphic review before publishing a full transcription or translation.
4. Reconcile the complete object and visual catalogues before assigning a permanent child ID.
5. Continue with the separate James Bermingham memorial, dated 1637, as the next Lusk micro-object candidate.
