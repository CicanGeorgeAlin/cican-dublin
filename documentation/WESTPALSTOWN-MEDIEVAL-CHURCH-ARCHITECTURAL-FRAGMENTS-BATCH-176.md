# CICAN Dublin Archive — Westpalstown medieval church and graveyard (Batch 176)

**Official sequence:** FHG 53 — Westpalstown  
**Survey:** Fingal Historic Graveyards Project, field survey 27 July 2008  
**Location:** Ballyboughal Road / R129, Westpalstown, near Naul, County Dublin, Ireland  
**Status:** Research report committed and fetched back for verification. No permanent CICAN IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.

## 2. Historical context
- FHG describes the site as containing the remains of a late-medieval parish church which was already a ruin by 1630.
- The parish church is described by Walsh (1888, pp. 209, 246) as ruined by 1630.
- E. Dix's 1892 note, “Wespalstown”, records architectural remains including a piscina and church fragments; this is an early antiquarian source and should be checked against surviving fabric rather than treated as a modern measured survey.
- The nearby old building with niches and alcoves was described as resembling a chapel and possibly associated with the church. Its function and date remain uncertain.
- The graveyard was previously surveyed in 1992 (M. Egan, *Memorials of the Dead*, Vol. 5; source year in the FHG bibliography is 1992, although the volume reference elsewhere may be abbreviated as 1991).
- The external ditch runs north–south, then returns towards a field boundary. FHG cites Dix's suggestion that it may represent an earlier graveyard boundary. Treat this as a probable/possible boundary, not a confirmed early-medieval enclosure.
- The extant upstanding archaeological features are late-medieval in date according to the FHG site-setting description.

## 3. Late-medieval parish church — DU007-00801
### FHG 2008 description
- The church walls are in the northwest corner of the graveyard.
- They survive to approximately 0.8m high and are built of squared rubble limestone.
- An opening in the north wall has dressed limestone jambs.
- A splayed window opening survives in the south wall.
- The graveyard survey identifies the surviving walls as late-medieval.

### 2012 Field Monument Advisor / RMP description
Fingal's 2012 *Managing Fingal County Council's Archaeological Resource* gives a more detailed architectural description:
- Plain rectangular church, approximately 17.40m east–west by 6m north–south, with recorded height around 0.85m in the RMP description.
- Randomly coursed masonry, with dressed quern stones reused in the wall fabric at the “rove end” (source wording; retain original wording pending specialist interpretation).
- Two openings on the north side indicate a former division between nave/rove and chancel.
- West doorway with round arch and chamfered jambs.
- Door rebate in the eastern opening.
- Plain headless recess at the east end of the south wall, possibly the remains of a piscina.
- Interior lit by a single slit opening with widely splayed embrasure in the south wall.
- FMA field assessment says the church stands about 1–2m high, walls approximately 1m wide, with bases of two south window openings, a chamfered north doorway and a large entranceway. It notes higher ground south of the church and north–south undulations.
- The 2012 report says the church had been ribbon-pointed with concrete and the wall tops were uncapped, raising a concern that concrete pointing could contribute to internal decay.

### Interpretation controls
- FHG 2008 and the 2012 FMA description differ in measured visible height. Preserve both observations with dates and source attribution.
- Do not describe the entire standing church as medieval without qualification: the lower medieval masonry is documented, while concrete ribbon pointing and other later repairs are also recorded.
- The possible piscina is not a confirmed liturgical fitting. Record it as a plain headless recess, possibly a piscina, pending specialist architectural assessment.
- The dressed querns reused in the wall are physical stones within the masonry; do not extract or treat them as loose objects.
- The two north-side openings and eastern door rebate should be photographed as distinct architectural details, linked to the church parent record.

## 4. Piscina / possible liturgical recess — high-priority architectural detail
- The 2012 RMP/FMA account identifies a plain headless recess at the east end of the south wall, possibly the remains of a piscina.
- Dix 1892 and the FHG historical narrative refer to a piscina among the church remains.
- This is an important convergence of historical and architectural evidence, but it is not sufficient to confirm the identification without a close architectural inspection.
- Field photography should include the full south wall, the recess in context, a straight-on image and oblique views showing depth, sill, shape and any surviving basin/drain.
- Do not insert a probe, clean, wet, scrape or otherwise touch the recess.
- If no basin or drain is visible, retain “possible piscina / headless recess” rather than upgrading the identification.

## 5. Reused medieval architectural fragments — DU007-00804
### General group
- A considerable number of architectural fragments from the church have been reused as grave markers.
- The FHG describes many fragments as located on the ground east of the church.
- Buried in Fingal describes sections of dressed window surrounds, including bar holes, lying east of the church.
- The 2012 FMA report says the fragments were placed in orderly rows southeast of the church.
- These descriptions may reflect different parts of the same fragment group or different survey dates. Map the actual positions individually during fieldwork; do not assume every fragment is in one single row or exact compass position.

### High-priority fragment types
1. **Fleur-de-lis panel grave marker** — One reused fragment has a large panel with a fleur-de-lis. This is a distinctive object suitable for a dedicated visual record. The motif does not by itself identify a family, patron or date.
2. **Dressed window-surround sections with bar holes** — FHG and Buried in Fingal describe dressed window fragments with bar holes. Record each distinct stone and the profile, bar-hole position, tooling, dimensions and current use as a grave marker.
3. **Plate 53.2 architectural fragment** — The FHG photograph shows a loose stone fragment lying on grass, with a central raised stump-like element and two lower lateral projections. Do not identify it as a specific architectural feature without matching the original photograph and field object. It may be one of the group of reused fragments, but its function remains unresolved.
4. **Other cut-stone pieces** — Inventory each fragment separately if physically distinguishable. Capture front/back/edges, dimensions, stone type, tooling, weathering, any inscription and current position.
5. **Reused quern stones in church masonry** — Record in situ as part of the church wall; do not confuse them with loose window fragments.

### Fragment conservation
- The 2012 FMA report explicitly recommends a detailed survey of the architectural fragments to record their quantity, quality and current position.
- It records risk of damage from grass-cutting machinery and removal.
- FHG 2008 notes broken and leaning slabs and recommends investigating loose cut-stone fragments.
- The current number, condition and position of the fragments are not verified in 2026.
- No fragments should be lifted, rearranged, cleaned or moved for photography.

## 6. Fleur-de-lis and medieval memorial evidence
- FHG identifies a grave marker with a large fleur-de-lis panel.
- This could be an architectural fragment reused as a grave marker; its exact original function is not stated.
- Do not describe it as a heraldic family crest or assign a family identity without inscription or specialist evidence.
- Record the entire stone, not just the motif, including dimensions, material, panel frame, edge profile, evidence of reuse and any funerary inscription.
- Check M. Egan's 1992 *Memorials of the Dead*, Vol. 5, p.208, for any transcription or description of the grave marker; confirm the page reference before citing a transcription.

## 7. Nearby chapel-like building — DU007-00802
- FHG records an old building near the graveyard which resembles a chapel, with niches and alcoves.
- Walsh (1888, p.246) and Dix (1892, p.182) are cited for the possible association with the church.
- Fingal's 2012 management report treats it as a separate second monument in the Westpalstown complex, DU007-00802, described as an ecclesiastical building.
- The nearby building must not be merged with the parish church DU007-00801.
- The source descriptions do not establish its precise original function, dedication or date. Do not label it a chapel as a certainty; use “ecclesiastical building / chapel-like structure, function unresolved”.
- Field task: establish its exact location using the 2012 site plan and RMP mapping; photograph each elevation, openings, niches, alcoves, masonry joints and relationship to field boundaries. No entry if unsafe or access is not authorised.

## 8. Graveyard layout and memorials
- The graveyard is sub-rectangular, enclosed by hedgerow and an external ditch in places, and lies within a larger field.
- FHG records 18th-, 19th- and 20th-century grave markers concentrated south and southwest of the church.
- Later markers are arranged in rows south of the church.
- A large railed plot south of the church contains a limestone table tomb.
- 19th-century headstones include Lamb of God and IHS motifs set within sunbursts.
- The general FHG list of marker categories includes chest/box tombs, cross slabs, free-standing crosses, flat slabs, headstones, mausolea, mural tablets, reused architectural fragments, sarcophagi, table tombs, undecorated markers, vaults, iron markers and railings. This is a project-wide classification list; do not assume every category is present at Westpalstown.
- Fingal's current Buried in Fingal page states that there are 30 headstones in the churchyard and that Dr Egan dates the earliest to 1744. This differs from the broad 18th-century range in FHG and provides a useful transcription cross-check.
- The 1744 earliest headstone statement does not contradict the late-medieval architectural fragments: the fragments are reused building stones, not necessarily inscribed grave markers of medieval date.
- The exact identities and inscriptions of the 30 headstones should be checked against the interment book, Egan's survey and physical stones.

## 9. Boundary ditch and landscape
- The external ditch runs north–south, returns and meets a field boundary.
- FHG cites Dix's interpretation that the ditch probably represents an earlier graveyard boundary.
- The 2012 FMA mapping shows the church, graveyard and fragment group as separate locations/features within the rural field setting.
- The 2012 report notes the graveyard expanded to a more rectangular form, with a low bank and established hedgeline; it also mentions a removed/exposed western hedgeline and machinery risk.
- Do not map the ditch as a confirmed early-medieval enclosure. Record its visible alignment, cross-section only if naturally visible, vegetation, field-boundary junctions and historic-map relationship.
- Do not clear hedges or dig to establish the boundary.

## 10. Condition, access and protection
- FHG 2008 described the graveyard as in good condition, regularly mown and still in use.
- FHG noted broken and leaning slabs, loose cut-stone fragments and the need to investigate the stability of leaning headstones.
- FHG recommended regular maintenance, investigation of loose fragments and careful clearance of overgrown boundary vegetation.
- The 2012 FMA report separately notes concrete ribbon pointing, uncapped wall tops, possible internal decay, grass-cutting machinery damage and risk of fragment removal.
- These are dated observations and recommendations, not a 2026 condition statement. Current condition and any conservation works since 2012 remain unverified.
- The 2012 report describes access through the field and graveyard, via gate and stile; the FMA notes access through Keogh's land. This indicates access across private land may be involved. Obtain landowner/council permission before entering.
- Fingal County Council warns that historic graveyards are protected archaeological monuments and that even pulling ivy from a wall can cause damage. No intervention, vegetation clearance, stone straightening, digging or moving fragments by CICAN.
- Photography should be non-contact and from authorised access. If the fragment group is at risk from machinery, report concerns to Fingal County Council rather than moving stones.

## 12. Duplicate prevention and archive decision
- FHG 53 Westpalstown is distinct from Westpalstown House ruins and any other local church or burial ground.
- Church DU007-00801, nearby ecclesiastical building DU007-00802, graveyard DU007-00803 and architectural fragments DU007-00804 are separate components of one wider complex.
- Individual reused fragments should receive separate object records only after physical identification and duplicate checking; no bulk “fragment group” record should conceal individual object identities.
- The fleur-de-lis panel and Plate 53.2 fragment are candidates requiring image-to-field matching.
- The possible piscina remains an architectural interpretation, not a separate object record until verified.
- No permanent CICAN IDs, production catalogue records or map pins created.
