# Whitestown Flat Bronze Axehead — NMI R.1695
## Research Batch 209

**Project:** CICAN Dublin Archive  
**Research status:** Documentary research; object not independently examined  
**Place:** Whitestown, parish of Lusk, County Dublin  
**NMI registration:** R.1695  
**Subject:** Flat bronze axehead, classified in a later NMI-files summary as an undecorated Ballyvalley type

## 1. The 1988 published stray-find record

Bradley and King’s *Urban Archaeology Survey: County Dublin* lists a flat bronze axehead found at Whitestown, Lusk. It states that it was discovered **seven feet below the surface while digging a ditch**, gives the NMI number **R.1695**, and cross-references Harbison 1969a, p.48, no.1360.

This is the clearest published account located for the reported discovery circumstances. Seven feet is approximately 2.13 metres. The survey does not identify the exact field, ditch, date of discovery, finder, stratigraphic layer, or the circumstances by which the object entered the NMI collection.

Source: John Bradley and Heather A. King, *Urban Archaeology Survey: County Dublin* (1988), Lusk, “List of Archaeological Stray Finds”, p.268.  
https://www.archaeology.ie/app/uploads/2025/05/County_Dublin_Urban_Archaeology_Survey_1988-1.pdf

The Greater Dublin Drainage Cultural Heritage Assessment reproduces an NMI Topographical Files entry for:

| Museum number | R.1695 |
| Townland | Whitestown |
| Find | Bronze Axehead |

This later summary confirms the townland, registration and broad object identification. Its “Find place: No detail” entry does not repeat the seven-foot ditch discovery recorded in the 1988 survey. The two sources should be preserved side by side: the 1988 publication supplies the reported discovery story; the 2012 reproduction of the NMI file says the file's find-place field contains no detail. It is not possible to tell from the accessible sources whether the older account derives from a now-unavailable record, a separate publication, or information omitted from the later summary.

## 3. Harbison's classification

The 1988 survey cites Peter Harbison, *The Axes of the Early Bronze Age in Ireland* (1969), p.48, catalogue no.1360. The National Library of Ireland catalogue confirms the book's 1969 publication by Beck in Munich, its place in the *Prähistorische Bronzefunde* series, and its illustrated format (108 pages and 80 plates).

The 2012 NMI-files summary explicitly classifies R.1695 as a **Ballyvalley type, undecorated** flat bronze axehead. This is the best accessible published type attribution located in this batch. The spelling “Ballyvalley” is retained as it appears in the NMI-files summary; Harbison's book and specialist literature may use related variant spellings, so the original catalogue entry should be checked before normalising the type name.

The accessible NLI catalogue record confirms the bibliographic work but does not expose Harbison's individual catalogue entry or plate for no.1360. Therefore this research does not independently verify the axehead's detailed outline, dimensions, weight, alloy composition, edge condition, or illustration.

Sources:
- Harbison, Peter (1969), *The Axes of the Early Bronze Age in Ireland*, NLI catalogue record: https://catalogue.nli.ie/Record/vtls000073400
- GDD 2012, Appendix 6.2.3, above.

## 4. What the seven-foot depth does — and does not — tell us

The 1988 account is notable because it reports a depth of seven feet (approximately 2.13m). However, the published wording only says that the axehead was found below the surface while a ditch was being dug. It does not describe:
- a sealed archaeological deposit;
- the soil or geological layer in which it lay;
- whether the ditch cut through made ground, colluvium, peat, or natural subsoil;
- associated objects, structures, or datable material;
- whether the axehead was deliberately deposited or accidentally lost.

Consequently, depth alone cannot establish a burial, hoard, ritual deposit, or precise date. It is a reported discovery circumstance, not a secure archaeological context.

## 5. Relationship to the Whitestown cist burial

The axehead and the 1932 Whitestown cist burial (NMI 1932:5614 / 1932:5614A in later summaries) are separate records. The 1988 survey lists them as separate stray-find entries. The 2012 NMI-files appendix likewise gives separate museum numbers and entries for R.1695 and the cist burial. There is no evidence that the axehead was associated with the grave or its bowl.

## 6. Current location and image status

The sources identify the National Museum of Ireland registration number but do not establish the axehead's present display location, storage location, current condition, or whether it is currently available for examination. No securely matched open-access photograph or drawing of NMI R.1695 was located in this research.

Do not use an image of another Ballyvalley-type axehead as a substitute. Harbison's original plate no.1360 should be checked in the 1969 volume, and image reproduction rights should be established before adding it to the archive.

## 7. Archive decision

**Research outcome:** A distinct Whitestown flat bronze axehead is documented as NMI R.1695. The 1988 County Dublin survey reports discovery seven feet below ground during ditch digging and cites Harbison 1969a, p.48, no.1360. A 2012 reproduction of the NMI Topographical Files identifies it as an undecorated Ballyvalley-type flat bronze axehead, but records no detail in the find-place field.

**Production decision:** Strong candidate for a documentary artefact record after duplicate checking against the canonical catalogue. Do not assign exact coordinates, infer a hoard or grave context, infer a date from depth, or claim present custody beyond the museum registration. Seek the original NMI file and Harbison's plate for the object's dimensions, form and image.

**Next research target:** Investigate the separate Whitestown human skeletal remains record NMI 1989:53, found in a sand pit across the road from the entrance to Rogerstown Hall. Keep it distinct from the 1932 cist burial: the 2012 NMI-files summary says loose bone was found and there was no evidence for a grave.
